The **equine KIT locus** is one of the central “white-patterning” regions in horse coat genetics. It does **not** choose whether a horse makes red versus black pigment; instead, it chiefly determines where pigment-producing cells successfully reach and persist in the skin and hair follicles. In other words, KIT usually makes areas **unpigmented**—white hair over pink skin—while the horse’s underlying red/black genotype still determines the color of whatever pigmented areas remain. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

## What KIT does biologically

*KIT* is on horse chromosome 3 (ECA3) and encodes a receptor tyrosine kinase, a cell-surface signaling protein. Its ligand, KITL/SCF (stem-cell factor), binds to KIT, activating a signaling cascade that supports cell survival, proliferation, migration, and differentiation.

For coat color, the crucial cell is the **melanoblast**: a neural-crest-derived precursor that migrates during embryonic development and matures into a melanocyte. Melanocytes occupy the skin and hair follicles and make pigment. Normal KIT signaling helps enough melanoblasts survive, migrate, and establish themselves. Reduced or altered KIT activity means some regions are not colonized by melanocytes; those regions grow white hair and have pink skin. KIT signaling also interacts with MITF, a major pigment-cell transcription factor. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

That gives a useful genetic-design shorthand:

\[
\text{MC1R/ASIP and modifiers} \rightarrow \text{pigment type and shade}
\]

\[
\text{KIT and other spotting genes} \rightarrow \text{where pigment cells are absent}
\]

So a KIT-patterned chestnut has red pigment in colored regions; a KIT-patterned black horse has black pigment in colored regions; a bay retains black points and red/brown body pigment where melanocytes are present. KIT does not chemically “turn black into red” or “turn red into black.” It creates regions with **no functional hair-follicle melanocytes**, so neither eumelanin nor pheomelanin is made there. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

## KIT versus red and black pigment

The equine **Extension/MC1R** locus is the primary red-versus-black switch:

| Locus or system | Main role | Effect on red/black pigment |
|---|---|---|
| **MC1R (Extension)** | Determines whether the horse can produce black eumelanin in the coat | \(E\) permits black pigment; \(e/e\) produces red-only/chestnut pigment |
| **ASIP (Agouti)** | Distributes black pigment when the horse has an \(E\) allele | Restricts black to points in bay; does not act meaningfully on \(e/e\) chestnuts |
| **KIT** | Controls migration/survival/function of melanocyte lineage in particular body areas | Removes both red and black pigment from affected patches; does not select one pigment type |
| **Dilutions/modifiers** | Change pigment amount, tone, distribution, or progressive graying | May alter the appearance of colored regions, but are separate from KIT spotting |

Thus:

- A **chestnut W20/W20** may have a red coat with a blaze, socks, belly white, or sometimes very little visible white.
- A **black W5/n** may have black patches separated by large white/pink regions.
- A **bay SB1/SB1** can be almost or entirely white externally, even though genetically it still carries bay genetics underneath.
- An all-white KIT-patterned horse is not genetically “albino.” In horses, true albinism has not been established as a normal viable coat-color category; KIT white horses retain ordinary pigment genetics in the regions that still possess melanocytes, and their eyes are generally normally pigmented. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

This also explains why base color becomes hard or impossible to identify visually in maximal-white individuals: the base-color program may be genetically present but has little or no pigmented hair available to display it.

## The KIT family of patterns

“KIT locus” is broader than the numbered **W** series. Several historically named patterns map to, or closely beside, KIT:

| Pattern / notation | Molecular status | Usual expression | Homozygous status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tobiano (TO)** | Large inversion near KIT, thought to disrupt KIT regulation rather than KIT coding sequence | Smooth-edged white patches, usually white legs, body white commonly crossing the topline; colored areas retain base color | Viable; homozygotes are common and pass TO to every foal |
| **Sabino 1 (SB1)** | KIT intron-16 splice-site variant; causes partial exon 17 skipping | Jagged-edged leg and facial white, belly spotting/roaning; can be minimal or extensive | Viable; SB1/SB1 is usually nearly or fully white |
| **Dominant White / White Spotting (W1–W35)** | A large collection of different KIT variants: missense, nonsense, splice, small deletion, gross deletion, frameshift, and regulatory variants | From no visible extra white to sabino-like markings to nearly all-white | Depends on the particular allele; some are viable in homozygous form, many have not been observed homozygously |
| **Roan (RN)** | Strongly associated with the KIT region, but the causal variant remains unsettled | Even intermixture of white hairs through body coat while head/lower legs typically remain dark | Genetics and homozygous viability remain unresolved |

The phrase **“dominant white”** is historical and increasingly imperfect. It originally described white-born horses with dominant inheritance and extensive white coats. But the W series now includes subtle, viable, incompletely dominant, and sometimes visually cryptic variants. Many researchers prefer **KIT white spotting** rather than “dominant white,” and reserve a phenotype description—minimal, sabino-like, extensive, or all-white—for what the horse actually looks like. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

## W-series alleles: the practical map

There are at least **35 numbered W variants** reported in the KIT gene in the commonly used W1–W35 nomenclature. They are not one mutation with 35 strength levels. Each is a distinct DNA change, often founded in a particular family or breed, and the numbers mostly reflect discovery order—not severity. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

### Broad phenotype groups

| Functional/phenotypic group | Common examples | Typical result |
|---|---|---|
| High-impact all-white tendency | W1, W2, W3, W4, W7, W9, W11, W14, W16, W17a/b, W23–W25, W27, W30 | One copy often produces extremely extensive depigmentation or an all-/near-all-white, pink-skinned horse |
| Sabino-like to very extensive | W5, W6, W8, W10, W12, W13, W15, W18, W19, W21, W22, W26, W28, W31, W33 | Variable leg/face/belly white with jagged edges; some heterozygotes are near-white or all-white |
| Mild or “booster” variants | W20, W32, W34, W35 | May give little or no visible spotting alone; commonly increase white when combined with other white-patterning alleles |
| Separate named KIT-associated patterns | SB1, TO, RN | Sabino 1 and tobiano have identified causative changes; roan remains incompletely solved |

A current review tabulates the W series as W1–W35 and classifies W20, W32, W34, and W35 as especially mild on average, while numerous truncating, splice-disrupting, or deletion variants tend to yield stronger white expression. But no allele predicts an identical drawing on every horse. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

### Important individual alleles

**W20** is the most misunderstood KIT variant. It is real and associated with increased white spotting, but it is not a guarantee of dramatic color. A horse with W20/n or W20/W20 may be solid-looking, may have ordinary-looking face/leg white, or may show sabino-like features. Its most useful description is a **probabilistic white-patterning enhancer**, not a universal “sabino gene.” It can increase the extent of white from other KIT alleles and other spotting systems. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

**W32, W34, and W35** are likewise comparatively mild variants. Some carriers have little or no conspicuous white patterning, yet these alleles can contribute to more white in combination. W35 is a 5′UTR/regulatory-region variant rather than a coding change, illustrating that KIT expression can be altered without changing the KIT protein sequence itself. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

**SB1** is a well-characterized, incompletely dominant KIT allele. One copy typically yields variable sabino markings: uneven stockings, face white, belly spotting, roaning or speckling at edges, and irregular/jagged borders. Two copies are viable and commonly produce a nearly or completely white, pink-skinned horse. SB1 is an actual molecular test, but “sabino” as a visual label is much broader than SB1: many non-SB1 horses are visually sabino-like. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

**Tobiano** is functionally a KIT-regulatory rearrangement rather than a conventional single-base W allele. It is a large paracentric inversion upstream/near KIT, approximately 43 Mb in the review’s description. It produces the familiar body-crossing, smooth-edged tobiano pattern. TO/TO horses are viable and fertile in available evidence, and are not reliably distinguishable by appearance from TO/n horses; a genetic test is needed to know whether they will transmit tobiano to every foal. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

**Roan** is the major unresolved case. Classical roan is genetically distinct from gray and from Appaloosa varnish roan: it is white hair interspersed through the body coat, while head, lower legs, mane, and tail generally remain more solid. Several KIT-region markers have shown breed-specific association, but no universally accepted causative mutation explains roan across all roan populations. “Roan is a KIT allele” is therefore too confident; the accurate wording is that classical roan maps near/in the KIT region in several populations, but its molecular basis is still unsettled and may be heterogeneous. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

## Why expression is so variable

KIT white spotting has **variable expressivity** and incomplete/threshold-like visibility. A genotype is not a paint-by-numbers phenotype.

Several mechanisms create that variability:

- **Stochastic embryonic cell migration.** Melanoblast migration and settlement happen during development. If reduced KIT signaling leaves development close to a threshold, chance differences in how many cells reach each region can make two same-genotype horses look markedly different.
- **Allele severity.** A nonsense, frameshift, major deletion, or critical splice disruption will often have a larger effect than a subtle missense or regulatory change.
- **Dosage.** Some alleles become more extensive with two copies, notably SB1 and the viable mild W variants.
- **Allelic combinations.** White variants can stack. A horse can carry more than two named KIT variants because multiple variants may reside on the same physical copy of chromosome 3—on the same **haplotype**.
- **Other spotting genes.** KIT can interact visually with tobiano, splashed white, frame overo, leopard complex, PATN1, and as-yet-unidentified modifiers. Phenotype alone cannot reliably identify all components.
- **Background genetics and ascertainment.** Breed, lineage, selection, unrecognized linked variants, and photo-based phenotype scoring can all skew what breeders perceive as an allele’s “normal” expression. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

An important implementation point for a breeding simulator: treat white extent as a **probability distribution conditional on genotype**, not a fixed pattern sprite. A major W allele may have a high probability of near-total white; W20/W20 should have a broad distribution centered much lower; and combinations should shift the distribution upward without guaranteeing a particular pattern geometry.

## Multi-allele haplotypes: a major correction

A common belief is that a horse cannot carry more than two KIT white alleles because it has only two KIT chromosomes. That is incorrect. A horse has two KIT-bearing chromosomes, but a single chromosome can carry a **haplotype containing multiple named variants**.

For example, research has identified:

- W22 consistently linked with W20 in studied horses.
- W19 occurring alone or in linkage with W34 and W35.
- Viable multi-variant haplotypes such as W32W35, W34W35, W20W32, W32SB1, and W19W34W35.
- Horses with three, four, five, and even six annotated KIT variants across the two homologous chromosomes. In one dataset of 8,334 horses, 434 had three or more KIT variants; the authors found homozygous viable haplotypes involving SB1, W19, W20, W32, W34, and W35. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854588/)

The practical consequences are substantial:

1. A laboratory report that presents only “W20/W22” can obscure phase—whether the alleles sit on the same chromosome or opposite chromosomes.
2. Phase matters for inheritance and breeding predictions.
3. “Two mutations maximum” is biologically wrong.
4. More KIT variants often, but not invariably, produce more depigmentation.
5. A rare strong W allele plus mild variants is not automatically lethal; viability depends on the functional total of the two chromosome copies, not simply on a count of labels. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854588/)

## Health effects: established facts

### What is well supported

KIT is not pigment-only biology. It has developmental roles in:

- Melanoblasts/melanocytes.
- Blood-cell development and hematopoiesis.
- Mast cells.
- Spermatogonia/germ-cell biology.
- Interstitial cells of Cajal in the gastrointestinal tract. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2065884/)

At the extreme, complete or near-complete loss of KIT function is expected to be developmentally catastrophic: comparative work identifies prenatal/perinatal lethality associated with severe KIT loss, including anemia. That biological role is why many high-impact equine KIT variants are suspected to be nonviable when inherited in a damaging two-copy combination. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2065884/)

### What is documented in horses

- **SB1/SB1 is viable.** SB1 homozygotes may be nearly or fully white but are reported as healthy, with no established SB1-specific systemic disease. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)
- **TO/TO is viable.** Tobiano homozygotes are reported as viable and fertile, with no established TO-specific health syndrome. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)
- **W20, W32, W34, W35, W19, and W15 have been observed in viable homozygous states** in current datasets, but that does not mean every possible compound genotype is known to be safe. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)
- **No KIT-W syndrome comparable to lethal white foal syndrome has been established** for heterozygous KIT white-patterning horses. The early dominant-white literature reported normal blood parameters and at least limited evidence of normal fertility in studied heterozygous horses, though sample sizes were small. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2065884/)
- **Pink skin under white areas increases practical sun sensitivity.** This is not necessarily a unique KIT disease; it is a predictable consequence of low melanin protection. Horses with large pink-skinned areas can need sun avoidance, UV-protective gear, and monitoring for sunburn or photosensitivity-related skin irritation. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

### What KIT is *not* responsible for

Do not confuse KIT white spotting with the better-established health risks attached to other equine white patterns:

| Pattern or locus | Gene(s) | Main health concern | Relationship to KIT |
|---|---|---|---|
| Frame overo / OLWS | **EDNRB** | Two copies cause lethal white foal syndrome with intestinal aganglionosis | Not a KIT condition |
| Splashed white | **MITF**, **PAX3** | Elevated deafness risk in certain alleles/combinations; blue eyes common | Separate loci, but in the same melanocyte-development network |
| Leopard complex | **TRPM1** | Congenital stationary night blindness in LP/LP horses | Not KIT |
| Gray | **STX17** | Progressive graying and increased melanoma risk | Not KIT |
| Cream | **SLC45A2** | Pigment dilution; no equivalent KIT developmental mechanism | Not KIT |

KIT and MITF/PAX3 are biologically connected in melanocyte development, but they are not interchangeable diagnostic categories. In particular, **deafness is an established concern for splashed white alleles, not a routine established consequence of KIT W/SB1/tobiano spotting.** [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

## Health and lethality: what remains uncertain

The phrase “homozygous lethal” is widely repeated for W alleles, but it needs precise interpretation.

### Reasonable current conclusion

Many high-impact W alleles are **suspected** to be embryonic lethal in homozygous form because:

- No living homozygotes have been observed.
- Stronger alleles often disrupt key protein domains.
- Historical matings of white horses have sometimes yielded approximately 2:1 white-to-solid live-born ratios rather than the expected 3:1, consistent with loss of a quarter of conceptuses.
- Complete KIT loss is lethal in comparative systems. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

The review specifically lists W1–W14, W16–W18, W21–W28, W30, W31, and W33 as not observed homozygously and **predicted** to be homozygous lethal. That is a useful risk-management list, but “not observed” is not the same scientific category as “experimentally proven lethal.” [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

### Why the certainty is limited

- Many W alleles are exceptionally rare and founder-line restricted. There may simply have been too few carrier-to-carrier matings to produce or recognize a homozygote.
- The W nomenclature groups variants with radically different molecular impacts under one historical label.
- Some alleles once considered potentially lethal were later found in viable homozygotes—W15 is an important example.
- Published datasets often lack systematic fertility, pregnancy-loss, neonatal, hematologic, and reproductive follow-up.
- Commercial test panels vary. A “negative dominant white test” may only mean negative for the W alleles on that particular panel, not negative for every KIT variant or every white-patterning locus. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

So the careful breeder/scientist language is:

> Certain high-impact KIT variants are **presumed or suspected** to be nonviable in homozygous or severe compound states. That presumption is biologically plausible and should guide conservative mating decisions, but allele-specific proof and combination-specific outcome data are incomplete.

## The principal controversies

### “Dominant white” is a poor umbrella term

The historical term suggests that every W allele is dominant, dramatic, and lethal when doubled. That is false. Some W variants are subtle, some are incompletely dominant, some are viable in homozygous form, and some may be invisible without interacting variants. “KIT white spotting” is more accurate for the molecular category. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

### W20: real effect, overstated certainty

W20 is sometimes dismissed as meaningless because many carriers appear solid, while other discussions treat it as a guaranteed “white booster.” Neither extreme is correct.

The evidence supports an association with increased spotting and interactions with other variants, but its standalone penetrance and visible extent are variable. W20 can be present in a horse with nearly no white, and a flashy W20 carrier may owe part of that phenotype to other known or unknown loci. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

### “Sabino” is not a one-gene visual category

**Sabino 1** is a specific KIT allele. **Sabino-like** is a phenotype description. Numerous W alleles, other unidentified KIT variants, and non-KIT mechanisms can produce jagged leg/face/belly white that people call sabino. A horse can look highly sabino but be SB1-negative; that does not disprove its phenotype, only the claim that it has SB1. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

### Roan remains unresolved

The “roan locus” was historically treated as a KIT trait and sometimes as homozygous lethal. Modern data support a KIT-region association in several populations but do not establish one causal sequence variant across breeds. Reports conflict by breed; potential markers fail in some roan populations; and true roan may include more than one genetic mechanism or phenocopies. This makes blanket use of commercial “roan” tests scientifically precarious outside the population in which their marker was validated. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

### Allele count and genotype reporting

A two-slot format—such as “W20/W22”—can be misleading because W22 has been found linked to W20, and several other variants form complex haplotypes. Good reporting should show every tested variant and, ideally, phase when known: for example, \(W20W22/n\) rather than implying those two named changes must be on opposite KIT chromosomes. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854588/)

### Commercial panels lag discovery

There are more biologically relevant variants than are included in every routine color panel. Results are only as complete as:

- The loci tested.
- The mutation types detectable by the assay.
- The reference genome/coordinates used.
- Whether haplotyping or merely unphased variant calls are provided.
- Whether the particular breed has been studied.

A negative panel does not prove “no genetic explanation” for unexplained white; it may mean an unknown KIT change, a non-KIT white gene, a structural variant not on the assay, or a phenotype produced by multiple modifiers.

## What future research needs

The highest-value next steps are fairly clear.

- **Whole-genome and long-read sequencing** of unexplained white, sabino-like, and roan horses, especially parent–offspring trios, to find structural and regulatory variants missed by short targeted panels.
- **True phasing** of KIT haplotypes. Long-read sequencing and family-based haplotyping can reveal which variants sit together on the same chromosome.
- **Allele-specific functional assays.** Researchers need to measure KIT transcript abundance, splicing, receptor trafficking, ligand binding, dimerization, kinase activity, and melanoblast behavior for each important variant—not infer all effects from mutation class.
- **Prospective breeding and reproductive data.** Carrier-by-carrier matings, with ethical oversight and careful pregnancy monitoring, could distinguish genuinely lethal combinations from merely rare unobserved genotypes.
- **Systematic phenotype databases.** Standardized whole-horse photos, skin/eye pigmentation records, base-color genotypes, and environmental context would be much stronger than informal phenotype labels.
- **Health surveillance.** Hematology, fertility, foal growth, immune status, and gastrointestinal outcomes are poorly documented for many rare W and multi-variant genotypes.
- **Breed-aware roan studies.** Classical roan needs deep sequencing and phenotype validation in multiple independent breeds, rather than assuming a marker from one breed is universal. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

## Bottom line

KIT is best understood as a **melanocyte distribution and survival locus**, not a red/black pigment locus. It removes both pigment types from affected skin and hair, leaving the base color visible only where pigment cells remain. The recognized KIT landscape includes tobiano, SB1, dozens of W-series variants, and an unresolved roan region. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10854722/)

The well-established facts are strong for **tobiano**, **Sabino 1**, many individual W mutations, and the central developmental role of KIT. The uncertain territory is equally important: actual lethality of many rare W/W or high-impact compound combinations, full health/reproductive consequences of complex KIT haplotypes, the true causal basis of classical roan, and the identity of still-undiscovered regulatory/modifier variants. The most responsible approach is allele-specific, phase-aware, and phenotype-aware—not treating every white horse, every “sabino,” or every W-number as biologically equivalent.
