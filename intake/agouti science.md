The equine agouti locus is **ASIP** (*agouti signaling protein*). Its firmly established job is not to decide whether a horse can make black pigment—that is primarily the job of *MC1R* / Extension—but to control **where black pigment (eumelanin) is deposited** in a horse that can make it. In modern equine molecular genetics, the only well-established, routine-testable ASIP alleles are functional **A** and loss-of-function **a**; proposed **A+** (“wild bay”) and **At** (“seal brown”) are not accepted as validated ASIP alleles in horses. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)

## The pigment system

Horse hair color begins with two pigment types:

- **Eumelanin**: black to brown-black pigment.
- **Pheomelanin**: red/yellow pigment.

The two central loci are often taught together:

| Locus | Gene | Main question it answers | Basic outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extension / Red Factor | *MC1R* | Can the horse produce black pigment? | \(E\_\) horses can produce eumelanin; \(e/e\) horses make red pigment instead |
| Agouti | *ASIP* | If black pigment is available, where is it deposited? | \(A\_\) restricts it to points; \(a/a\) permits widespread deposition |

A horse that is **\(e/e\)** is chestnut/sorrel-based, because its melanocytes cannot effectively switch into eumelanin production. That makes agouti visually hidden: a chestnut can genetically be **A/A**, **A/a**, or **a/a**, but it will still appear red because there is no black pigment for ASIP to distribute. A chestnut’s agouti genotype becomes relevant when predicting foal colors with a non-chestnut mate. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)

## What ASIP actually does

*MC1R* sits on pigment-producing cells, melanocytes. When stimulated, it promotes eumelanin production. The ASIP protein counteracts that signaling: in the appropriate body regions and hair-growth stages, it reduces MC1R-driven black pigment production, allowing the coat to grow red/brown while black remains in the mane, tail, legs, ear margins, and often other point-associated areas.

That simplified “ASIP turns black off on the body” explanation is useful, though biology is more spatial and time-dependent than a literal on/off switch. The important practical result is:

- Functional ASIP in a black-pigment-capable horse produces a **bay-pattern base**.
- No functional ASIP produces a **black-pattern base**.

The conventional terms “bay” and “black” in this genetic sense describe pigment distribution before the effects of dilution, gray, white spotting, roan, leopard complex, seasonal fading, sun bleaching, and other modifiers. A black-based horse can therefore look smoky, brownish, gray, white-patterned, silver-diluted, or otherwise unlike a textbook black horse.

## The established alleles

### A: functional agouti

**A** is the normal, functional form of equine *ASIP*. It produces agouti signaling protein capable of restricting much of the horse’s eumelanin deposition to the points.

In a horse with at least one functional Extension allele:

| Extension | Agouti | Basal pigment result |
|---|---:|---|
| \(E/E\) or \(E/e\) | \(A/A\) | Bay-pattern base |
| \(E/E\) or \(E/e\) | \(A/a\) | Bay-pattern base |
| \(E/E\) or \(E/e\) | \(a/a\) | Black-pattern base |
| \(e/e\) | Any agouti genotype | Chestnut/red base; ASIP is not visibly expressed |

Crucially, routine laboratories generally report **A/A** as “no detected copy of the tested non-agouti deletion,” not proof that every sequence element across *ASIP* and its regulatory landscape is identical. In ordinary breeding practice, though, \(A/A\) means the horse carries two copies of the tested functional/non-deletion form; \(A/a\) means one; and \(a/a\) means two copies of the established non-agouti mutation. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)

### a: non-agouti / recessive black

The established equine **a** allele is an **11-base-pair deletion in exon 2 of *ASIP***. It causes a frameshift and loss of normal ASIP function. In an \(E\_\) horse, two copies—**\(a/a\)**—mean ASIP cannot appropriately restrict eumelanin on the body, producing the classical genetically black base. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)

This is why the allele is recessive phenotypically:

- **A/A**: enough functional ASIP → bay-pattern distribution.
- **A/a**: one functional copy is sufficient → bay-pattern distribution.
- **a/a**: no functional copy of the usual ASIP protein → black-pattern distribution.

A genuine \(E\_\,a/a\) horse can still fade in sunlight, show seasonal reddish/brown tones, have pangaré-like pale areas, or be altered by other loci. Fading does not turn an \(a/a\) horse into a bay genetically, and a very dark bay is not automatically black genetically.

### What commercial agouti testing detects

The standard test is fundamentally a test for that exon-2 11-bp deletion:

- **A/A**: deletion not detected on either allele
- **A/a**: deletion detected once
- **a/a**: deletion detected twice

That test is highly useful and well validated for distinguishing the ordinary functional-ASIP versus deletion-associated non-agouti state. It does **not** identify every genetic contributor to shade or every potential modifier of black pigment distribution. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)

## Why A+ and At were proposed

Older coat-color literature often presented an allelic series:

\[
A^+ > A > A^t > a
\]

with:

- **\(A^+\)** = “wild bay” or “wild-type bay,” supposedly restricting black still more strongly than ordinary bay
- **\(A\)** = ordinary bay
- **\(A^t\)** = seal brown / brown, supposedly allowing more extensive black than bay but retaining tan/red around the muzzle, eyes, flanks, elbow, and stifle
- **\(a\)** = black

This model was largely based on **phenotype, breeding observations, and analogy to agouti systems in other mammals**, especially species where multiple ASIP alleles really do exist. It was a reasonable historical hypothesis. It gave breeders a simple Mendelian story for why some bays appeared minimally pointed and some horses appeared nearly black except for “mealy” tan regions.

The problem is that a useful phenotype classification is not the same thing as proof of an allelic series at a particular gene.

## A+: why “wild bay” is not accepted as a proven ASIP allele

“Wild bay” is commonly used for horses with a relatively light red/tan body and black that appears tightly confined to the mane, tail, ear edges, and lower portions of the limbs—sometimes ending around the fetlock rather than extending high up the leg. It is a **real-looking phenotype category**, but its genetic assignment to a separate \(A^+\) allele is unproven.

### The evidence problem

The functional \(A\) allele and the 11-bp non-agouti **a** deletion are known molecularly. By contrast, no reproducible, causative *ASIP* variant has been established that:

1. Segregates consistently with the proposed wild-bay phenotype,
2. Is distinct from ordinary functional \(A\),
3. Produces the phenotype in controlled family or association datasets after accounting for other loci and environment, and
4. Has credible functional evidence showing altered ASIP expression or protein function.

Therefore a horse visually called wild bay usually tests simply **\(A/A\)** or **\(A/a\)** on a standard agouti panel. That does not mean its appearance is imaginary; it means current evidence does not support calling the appearance a separate, molecularly validated *ASIP* allele.

### Why it may look like a separate allele

A “wild bay” appearance could arise from one or more of the following:

- Polygenic variation in red versus black pigment quantity
- Regulatory variants affecting *ASIP*, *MC1R*, or other pigmentation genes
- Pangaré/mealy-related effects and ventral/muzzle lightening
- Dun or non-dun primitive-marking background
- Seasonal coat, age, sun exposure, nutrition, grooming, and photographic lighting
- Breed-specific selection and linked modifier variants
- Subjective labeling, especially where breed registries define terms differently

So the correct modern statement is not “A+ has been proven not to exist anywhere in the genome.” Science almost never proves a broad negative that strongly. The accurate conclusion is: **no distinct equine \(A^+\) allele at ASIP has been molecularly demonstrated, so it should not be used as a confirmed genotype in breeding predictions or simulation genetics.**

## At: why seal brown is not accepted as a proven ASIP allele

### The phenotype called seal brown

“Seal brown,” often just “brown,” generally describes an extremely dark brown-to-nearly-black horse with lighter red/tan areas concentrated around the muzzle, eyes, flanks, inner limbs, elbow, stifle, or underside. The phenotype is difficult because it overlaps substantially with:

- Sun-faded black
- Very dark bay
- Black with pangaré/mealy expression
- Dark bay modified by shade polygenes
- Silver-related alterations on black pigment where silver is present
- Gray or roaning at various ages
- Individual seasonal and coat-condition differences

In other words, “seal brown” is visually meaningful to many horse people, but visual boundaries among brown, dark bay, fading black, and related phenotypes are often not clean.

### The historical At hypothesis

The hypothetical **\(A^t\)** allele was supposed to sit between bay and black in a dominance series:

\[
A > A^t > a
\]

Under this idea, \(A^t\) would permit broad black pigment over the body but retain agouti-controlled reddish/tan “soft points” on the muzzle, flanks, and underparts. This resembles black-and-tan agouti patterning in species such as dogs, but cross-species resemblance is not evidence that the same alleles or mechanisms operate in horses.

### What undermined At as an equine ASIP allele

The central issue is **genotype–phenotype mismatch**. Seal brown horses routinely test as ordinary agouti-positive horses—\(A/A\) or \(A/a\)—rather than as a reproducible third ASIP genotype. Studies and modern veterinary-laboratory materials recognize functional **A** and the 11-bp deletion **a**, while treating shade among agouti-positive horses as incompletely explained rather than as evidence for a validated \(A^t\). [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)

There was also a commercial “brown” test marketed from a marker near/in the agouti region. It was not accepted as a reliable causal test after inconsistent results, including results in Miniature Horses that did not match expected brown phenotype inheritance. A linked marker can be associated with a look in one selected population without being the causal variant—and it can fail when recombination or different genetic backgrounds break that association. That history is an important cautionary example: **a patented or marketed test is not automatically a scientifically validated allele test.** [equinetapestry](https://www.equinetapestry.blog/base-color-variation-mealy-and-seal-brown/)

Thus, saying “At has been decided not to exist” needs precision:

- It has **not** been demonstrated as a causative, independently testable *ASIP* allele in horses.
- Existing evidence makes the classic simple \(A > A^t > a\) equine ASIP model unsupported.
- The seal-brown/brown phenotype may still have a genetic basis, potentially involving ASIP regulation, another pigment locus, multiple modifiers, or several distinct pathways.
- Future evidence could identify an ASIP regulatory haplotype or a different locus with a robust effect—but that would be a new discovery, not confirmation of the old textbook \(A^t\) assumption.

## Where controversy comes from

The controversy is not mainly whether bay, black, chestnut, or brown-looking horses exist. It is about **what level of evidence earns the label “allele.”**

### Phenotype versus genotype

A horse can unambiguously look dark brown or wild bay while its standard test says **A/A** or **A/a**. That does not make either the observation or test fraudulent. It means the conventional agouti test only explains the major bay-versus-black distribution mechanism, not all shade and pattern variation within agouti-positive horses.

### Breed terminology

Color names have different historical and registry meanings. “Brown,” “seal brown,” “dark bay,” “mahogany bay,” “fading black,” and “black” may be assigned differently by owners, registries, judges, breeders, and laboratories. A study based only on owner-reported color names will have classification noise—particularly for dark phenotypes.

### Oversimplification of the base-color model

The standard \(E/e\) plus \(A/a\) framework is excellent for the core base-color logic, but it is deliberately simplified. UC Davis notes that variation in shade among basic colors remains incompletely understood and that many mammalian pigmentation genes exist whose equine contributions are not yet defined. [vgl.ucdavis](https://vgl.ucdavis.edu/resources/horse-coat-color)

A useful way to frame it is:

\[
\text{visible color} =
\text{MC1R} +
\text{ASIP} +
\text{dilutions} +
\text{white/gray/roan/leopard effects} +
\text{modifiers} +
\text{environment}
\]

The first two terms establish the main red/bay/black pigment framework. They do not supply a complete rendering engine for every visual variation a horse can show.

### Apparent exceptions

Large genotype–phenotype datasets show that coat color is not perfectly predicted from the simple two-locus model once phenotypes such as brown, dark bay, gray, and white are included. For example, one 709-horse study found some horses classified as brown, dark bay, bay, or white despite being homozygous for the known ASIP deletion; the authors concluded that additional genes are likely involved. That study does not overturn the established effect of the 11-bp ASIP deletion—it reinforces that visible phenotype can be masked or modified and that color classification can be complex. [pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6447268/)

## Well-established versus speculative

| Claim | Current status | Best interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| *ASIP* is the equine agouti gene | Well established | It regulates distribution of black pigment in conjunction with *MC1R* |
| Functional **A** produces bay-pattern distribution in \(E\_\) horses | Well established | Black largely restricted to points |
| The 11-bp exon-2 deletion **a** is a loss-of-function non-agouti allele | Well established | \(E\_\,a/a\) gives the classical black base |
| Agouti is visually hidden in \(e/e\) chestnuts | Well established | The genotype can still be inherited and tested |
| Standard testing distinguishes A/A, A/a, and a/a | Well established for the known deletion | It is a major-base-color test, not a complete shade test |
| Wild bay is caused by a separate \(A^+\) ASIP allele | Unvalidated | A recognizable phenotype, but not a confirmed molecular allele |
| Seal brown is caused by a separate \(A^t\) ASIP allele | Unvalidated and presently unsupported | The phenotype may be genetic, but the old ASIP allele assignment has not held up |
| All bays, dark bays, browns, and blacks can be diagnosed perfectly from *MC1R* and *ASIP* alone | False | Additional genes, modifiers, and phenotype ambiguity matter |
| A+ and At can never be found in future work | Not a scientific conclusion | They are not currently validated; future sequencing could reveal new variants or mechanisms |

## Future science

The productive question is no longer simply, “Which old agouti letter should we assign?” It is: **which causal variants and pathways explain residual variation among genetically agouti-positive horses?**

The next advances will likely require:

- Large, well-photographed cohorts with standardized color scoring across seasons and ages
- Pedigree-based studies that distinguish causal inheritance from breed association
- Whole-genome sequencing rather than testing only the known 11-bp deletion
- Examination of *ASIP* promoters, enhancers, untranslated regions, and other regulatory DNA—not just protein-coding exons
- Genome-wide association studies for clearly defined dark-bay, brown/seal, and wild-bay-like traits
- Functional work showing how a candidate changes ASIP expression, MC1R signaling, pigment switching, or regional pigment deposition
- Modeling interactions with known loci such as gray, cream, dun, silver, champagne, mushroom, leopard complex, roan, and white-patterning alleles

For a horse-genetics simulator, the most defensible present-day implementation is:

1. Model **Extension** as determining whether eumelanin is available.
2. Model **ASIP** with only validated **A** and **a** alleles.
3. Use \(E\_A\_\) for a bay-pattern base and \(E\_a/a\) for a black-pattern base.
4. Make “wild bay,” dark bay, brown/seal-brown-like expression, countershading, and degree of black extension **polygenic/modifier-driven phenotype parameters**, not confirmed \(A^+\) or \(A^t\) Mendelian alleles.
5. Keep those modifiers probabilistic and allow breed-linked frequency differences if your game needs breed realism.
6. Label any future \(A^+\)/\(A^t\)-like mechanic as a hypothetical simulation abstraction rather than established equine molecular genetics.

That approach preserves the proven biology while leaving room for the very real, visually important variation that the current two-allele ASIP test does not explain.
