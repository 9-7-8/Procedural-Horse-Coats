Natural gene / phase 1

KIT (white spotting)

The white-patterning neighbourhood on equine chromosome 3, and the mod’s widest locus. Sabino 1 and the numbered W series are all KIT variants, so they are alleles of one gene — which means a horse can be sabino or dominant white, and never both. It replaces the old separate horsegenetics.white and horsegenetics.sabino genes. Most of what it does to a population is not the dramatic part: four mild, viable, additive boosters — W35, W32, W34, W20 — are between them the commonest thing at this locus by a wide margin.

Crossing two of them

Viability: what canOccur means here

The alleles UC Davis lists as “homozygosity thought nonviable” — W22, W13, W23, W10, W5 — get canOccur false. Those are embryonic lethals, so there is no such horse — no catalogue entry, nothing in totalGenotypes(), nothing in the founder table. It is the same statement the sex locus makes about Y/Y, and it is a different statement from lethal white, which is a foal that is born.

Compound heterozygotes are allowed. W5/W13, W22/W10 and the rest are real horses. The evidence is about an allele paired with itself, and the breeding advice is to test a prospective mate for the same exact variant — so the rule in the model is one allele twice, not two strong alleles.

“No homozygote has been found” and “a homozygote has been found” are different claims, and the table says which is which. For most of the strong alleles the first is true and the model treats it as a lethal, which is what a breeder is told to do. For W15 the second is true — a W15/W15 horse is on record and it is all white — so its homozygote is allowed and lands on near-white. W4’s has no evidence either way, and picking “allowed” is the choice that does not invent a lethal the source never claims.

Breeding cannot make an impossible foal (since 2026-09-13)

breedWith still draws alleles without consulting canOccur, so two W5 carriers can still draw W5/W5. What happens next changed. genetics.Conceivable is now checked beside the viability check on both breeding paths, so that draw is an embryonic lethal. Golden carrots give no foal, and a pregnancy is lost early, both with the “could never have become a horse” miscarriage line (gap 225). KIT itself still declares no lethal condition, which is why the random splice carrot can still hand over a W5 carrier. A hand-typed W5/W5 in the spawn egg is still accepted and still shows as viable.

White finds white

The painter reads the coat handed to it and raises its own strength by 0.35 × however much of the horse is already de-pigmented. That is what makes W20 a booster rather than a decoration, and it is why a KIT pattern over a tobiano or a frame reads louder than the same pattern on a solid horse. One line in the painter, no interaction table anywhere. Full reasoning on the pipeline page.

Still simplified

Pink skin is not rendered (the model has no skin layer, only hair). The W1–W4, W6–W9, W11–W19 and W21 alleles are not modelled — their published descriptions do not separate them from the eight that are, and each would add a row nobody could read on a horse. Adding one is a line in alleles(), a row in expressionOf and a frequency.

Its gene carrot

Blue eyes — but only from broad white upward

This is the locus that has to draw a line. A plain sabino with four socks and a blaze has ordinary dark eyes — the white on it never reached the head — so only broad-white and above claim a blue iris through the eye-colour channel. That is the difference between this locus and splash, where even a modest marking comes with blue.

One or two blue eyes. A depigmenting claim frequently fails to reach the whole of both irises: 62% of blue-eyed horses get two whole blue eyes, 22% get exactly one (complete heterochromia, the other eye keeping whatever colour the horse's pigment genes gave it), and 16% get a wedge in one or both. It is one roll off this locus's own stored values, inherited with the allele copy — see the spread.

A horse can still qualify the other way: past 55% of the coat depigmented, the eyes follow however the horse got there — which covers a sabino that is also something else.

Two all-white alleles, and why they are two outcomes

W22 beside a booster and W4 paint the identical horse: pure white, pink skin, every other coat gene hidden and still inherited. (One W22 copy alone is a broad sabino-like white, following the 2024 review; the owner's call, 2026-09-15.) “No pigment cells reached the coat” has one appearance, so they could have shared an outcome. They do not, and the reason is that they differ in the only thing a breeder can act on:

  • W22’s homozygote is thought nonviable. Two W22 parents throw a quarter fewer live foals, and no W22/W22 horse exists.
  • W4’s is not known to be. Claims about which dominant-white variants are embryonic lethal are allele-specific, and the ones made online frequently are not. Where the reference is silent this mod allows the homozygote, exactly as it does for W23.

W4 is also a named line rather than a generic white horse. It traces to one stallion, Sultan, born about 1912 and bought at the California State Fair in 1921 by Adolfo Camarillo, who bred him to Morgan mares; the descendants were parade horses for sixty years and the herd was dispersed at auction in 1987. That is why the Morgan is the one breed here carrying it, and why the gene dictionary says “Camarillo white” instead of throwing the fact away.

White from birth is not the same as white with age

Four things in this mod can render a white horse and only one of them is this. A grey is born coloured and loses it, over dark skin; a double dilute is born pale cream with pink skin and blue eyes; an extensive splash or sabino is a pattern that happens to have run out of room. A W4 or W22 horse is white because the pigment cells never populated the coat at all, and it is that way the day it is born.

The boosters are most of what this locus does

W35, W32, W34 and W20 are one group and behave as one. All four are mild; all four have viable homozygotes; all four range from “no visible white beyond ordinary markings” to frank sabino-like spotting; and all four add white beside any other variant here.

W20 is the famous one — detected in twenty-five of twenty-eight surveyed breeds, and reaching an allele frequency of 0.34 in sampled Thoroughbreds, 0.33 in Gypsy Cobs and 0.21 in Paints. But in the one large commercial dataset that measured the newer alleles, W35 was commoner still at 0.168, with W32 at 0.061 behind it. That is the correction this page used to need: the mod carried W20 alone and treated the rest of the locus as a museum of rare founder alleles.

The founder frequencies here are that ordering at roughly half the magnitude, because a commercial colour-testing dataset is a population selected for having something to find. The breeds that genuinely carry them — the stock horses for W35 and W34, the Thoroughbred and the Paint for W20, the Quarter Horse, Paint, Standardbred and Welsh Pony for W32 — say so in their own tables.

Three things this page deliberately does not model

Haplotype phase. Several KIT changes can sit on the same physical chromosome — W22 is usually reported linked with W20, and one 2024 dataset found horses carrying six variants across their two copies. A horse here has two alleles at this locus and no more, because an allele pair is the model. Linkage is a system, not a number.

W34 × MC1R. Its effect is reported as possibly stronger on a chestnut background. “Possibly” is doing a lot of work there, and buying it would cost W34 an outcome of its own.

The other twenty-odd named alleles — W1–W3, W6–W9, W11, W12, W14, W16–W19, W21, W24–W28, W30, W31, W33. Almost all are one founder or one family, described in terms an allele already here covers, with frequencies that round to zero. The owner does want the full set eventually, redundant alleles included, so this is a “not yet” rather than a “no” — see the roadmap, which also notes the combinatorial cost of each one.

The combination table

Every pair of alleles, nine outcomes, written as a cascade: the strongest allele first, then what the second copy adds. There is no dose arithmetic and deliberately so — this locus holds a real viable dose series (SB1), a booster that is subtle alone (W20), and several alleles that are “dominant with variable expression”, where one copy is already effective and the phenotype is not neatly intermediate. How it paints

Every painted outcome is the same WhitePattern.sabino shape at a different strength: white grows inward from the extremities — legs, belly, face — with ragged, roaned margins, and past about strength 0.42 the body follows in torn patches, colour retreating last to the ears and crest. That is what “sabino-like” means throughout the W series, and writing a bespoke painter per allele would be writing the same painter eight times and pretending the differences were principled.

Measured coverage through the real pipeline, averaged over three epigenomes: W20/N 5.7%, W20/W20 11%, SB1/N 15%, SB1/W20 25%, W13/N 73%, SB1/SB1 94%, W22/N 100%. WhitePatternGenesTest pins that the ladder is monotone at every step, because a ladder whose rungs a player cannot tell apart is a ladder whose descriptions are decoration.

Dominant white is a different painter, not the top of the ramp: it zeroes both pigments everywhere and masks. All-white is not a great deal of sabino, it is the absence of pigment cells altogether — and the model has one true epistatic result rather than an asymptote.

The face

The face marking comes from the shared face-marking vocabulary every white locus shares, at the outcome’s own strength and with a jag of 0.42 — so a KIT marking has the same torn margin on the face that it has on the body. Strength picks the distribution rather than the marking, which is what finally makes the weak end of this locus mean what its description has always said: W20/N is “a star and a sock”, and about a third of the time it is now literally a star — a detached patch on the forehead, with about another third of horses carrying nothing on the face at all. Up the ladder it becomes a star and stripe, then a blaze, and SB1/SB1 is a bald face three times in five.

Verified

Founder white is measured, and fine (2026-09-09, re-measured 2026-09-18). FounderWhiteRateTest walks a founder population: about three founders in five wear white from the marking loci, mean coverage a few per cent of the sheet, and only a small minority are loudly marked. Counting white directly reads 91%, an artefact of pale coats (cremello, grey, palomino) on a flat chart, so each founder is composed twice, as rolled and with these loci forced wild, and the difference is taken. The band is deliberately wide, and it is the instrument to tune MarkingsGene.frequencies(), MitfGene.WILD_SW1_PERCENT and KitGene.frequencies() against.

…and the locus list is part of the instrument. The test measured only mitf, pax3 and kit until 2026-09-18, which stopped being the right set the moment markings took ordinary white over and SW1 was deliberately dropped 55% → 6% to suit. It was then measuring the leftovers, read 42%, and tripped its own floor — a real signal about the measurement rather than the population. With markings in the list the share is back mid-band. A new locus that hands out everyday white belongs in MARKING_LOCI, or this quietly starts reporting on the wrong thing. Widening the band would have buried exactly the signal the band exists to raise.

Genetic identity

InheritanceAutosomal, dominant to incompletely dominant, with strongly variable expressivity

This locus does not choose red versus black. It determines where pigment-producing cells successfully reach and persist, so it makes areas unpigmented — white hair over pink skin — while the base genotype still colours whatever is left. PMC10854722

The mechanism is a migration failure

The cell that matters is the melanoblast: a neural-crest-derived precursor that migrates during embryonic development and matures into a melanocyte. KIT signalling, driven by its ligand, supports the survival, proliferation, migration and differentiation of that lineage, and interacts with MITF, a major pigment-cell transcription factor. Reduce or alter KIT activity and some regions are never colonised; those regions grow white hair over pink skin.

MC1R, ASIP and modifiers → which pigment, and what shade
KIT and the other spotting genes → where there is no pigment cell at all

So a KIT-patterned chestnut is red where it is coloured, a KIT-patterned black is black there, and a bay keeps its black points wherever melanocytes survived. An all-white KIT horse is not albino — true albinism is not an established viable equine category. It retains ordinary pigment genetics in whatever regions still have melanocytes, and its eyes are generally normally pigmented. That is also why base colour becomes impossible to read visually on a maximal-white horse: the programme is there with almost no hair left to display it.

What is at this locus, and what only looks like it is

This is the distinction the whole restructure turns on. Only alleles at exactly the same locus share a gene. SB1 and every numbered W are defined KIT variants, so they compete for the same two chromosome slots. Two patterns that sit right beside them do not:

The W series is not one mutation with strength levels

Each numbered W is a distinct DNA change, usually founded in one family or breed, and the numbers reflect discovery order rather than severity. They fall into rough phenotype groups:

GroupTypical result
High-impact, all-white tendencyOne copy often produces extremely extensive depigmentation, or an all-white pink-skinned horse
Sabino-like to very extensiveVariable leg, face and belly white with jagged edges; some heterozygotes are near-white
Mild “booster” variants — W20, W32, W34, W35Little or no visible spotting alone; reliably increase white beside another variant

W20 is the most misunderstood variant in horse colour genetics, pulled between two wrong poles. It is not meaningless — the association with increased spotting is real — and it is not a guaranteed white booster either. The accurate description is a probabilistic white-patterning enhancer: a W20 horse can be solid-looking, ordinary-marked, or sabino-like, and a flashy carrier may owe part of that to other loci entirely. W35 makes the same point from the molecular side: it is a 5′UTR regulatory variant, so KIT expression can be altered without touching the protein sequence.

SB1 is a well-characterised incompletely dominant allele — an intron-16 splice-site variant causing partial exon 17 skipping. One copy gives uneven stockings, face white, belly spotting and roaned or speckled jagged borders; two are viable and usually nearly or completely white. Note that “sabino” the look is far broader than SB1 the allele: many visually sabino horses are SB1-negative, which disproves the label and not the phenotype.

Why two horses with one genotype look different

  • Stochastic embryonic migration. If reduced signalling leaves development near a threshold, chance differences in how many melanoblasts reach each region produce markedly different horses from the same genotype. This is the big one.
  • Allele severity. A nonsense, frameshift, large deletion or critical splice disruption usually outweighs a subtle missense or regulatory change.
  • Dosage, notably for SB1 and the viable mild variants.
  • Allelic combinations, including more than two named variants — see below.
  • Other spotting genes, and background genetics, breed selection and photograph-based phenotype scoring.
The recommended implementation is the one this gene uses

The review’s advice for a breeding simulator is to treat white extent as a probability distribution conditional on genotype rather than a fixed pattern sprite — a major allele with a high probability of near-total white, a booster homozygote with a broad distribution centred much lower, and combinations shifting the distribution upward without guaranteeing a geometry. That is what the combination table plus a per-horse strength roll does here, and why every outcome is the same sabino shape at a different strength rather than a set of drawings.

Multi-variant haplotypes, and the two-slot problem

A common belief is that a horse cannot carry more than two KIT white variants because it has two copies of chromosome 3. That is wrong. One chromosome can carry a haplotype containing several named variants. W22 is consistently reported linked with W20; viable multi-variant haplotypes such as W32W35, W34W35, W20W32, W32SB1 and W19W34W35 are documented; and in a dataset of 8,334 horses, 434 carried three or more KIT variants, with some carrying six. PMC10854588

The consequences are real for breeders: a report reading “W20/W22” hides phase — whether the two sit on one chromosome or opposite ones — and phase decides what the horse transmits. Good reporting writes W20W22/n when it knows. It also means a rare strong allele beside mild ones is not automatically lethal: viability depends on the functional total of the two chromosome copies, not on counting labels.

Health

KIT is not pigment-only biology. It has developmental roles in melanoblasts, blood-cell development, mast cells, germ-cell biology and the interstitial cells of Cajal in the gut. At the extreme, near-complete loss of KIT function is expected to be developmentally catastrophic, with prenatal or perinatal lethality and anemia in comparative systems — which is the biological reason many high-impact equine variants are suspected nonviable in a damaging two-copy combination. PMC2065884

What is documented in horses is narrower and more reassuring: SB1/SB1 is viable and healthy; tobiano homozygotes are viable and fertile; W20, W32, W34, W35, W19 and W15 have all been seen homozygous. No KIT-W syndrome comparable to lethal white foal syndrome has been established for heterozygous white-patterned horses.

The one genuine everyday consequence is pink skin under white, which raises sun sensitivity — a predictable result of missing melanin rather than a KIT disease, but a real management burden. And KIT is emphatically not responsible for the health risks attached to other white patterns: lethal white is EDNRB, deafness risk belongs to the splash alleles, night blindness to leopard complex, and melanoma to grey.

“Homozygous lethal” needs reading carefully

The review lists a long set of W alleles as not observed homozygously and predicted homozygous lethal. The supporting arguments are good ones: no living homozygotes, disrupted protein domains, historical white × white matings yielding roughly 2:1 rather than 3:1 live-born ratios, and the comparative lethality of complete KIT loss.

But “not observed” is not “proven lethal”, and the caveats matter. Many W alleles are rare and founder-restricted, so there may simply never have been enough carrier-to-carrier matings. The W label groups variants of radically different molecular impact. And some alleles once presumed lethal turned up in viable homozygotes — W15 is the standing example. Published datasets rarely carry systematic fertility, pregnancy-loss or hematologic follow-up.

Breeds and frequency

Breed distribution is the wrong frame for this locus. Most numbered variants are founder-line events, often traced to a single de-novo animal and confined to its descendants, so a variant is where its founder’s line went rather than where a breed is. The boosters are the exception and are widespread. A negative panel therefore means negative for the alleles on that panel, not negative for KIT — commercial testing lags discovery, and an unexplained white horse may carry an untested KIT change, a structural variant the assay cannot see, a non-KIT white gene, or several modifiers at once.

Where the mod departs

  • Haplotype phase is not modelled, and cannot be. A horse here has two alleles at this locus and no more, because an allele pair is the model — so W20W22/n is inexpressible, and the linked pairs the literature documents become ordinary compound heterozygotes. This is the largest single departure on the page. Linkage is a system rather than a number, and the mod settled against it deliberately.
  • The allele list runs past this source. The review tabulates the series to W35; this gene ships every published variant beyond it as well, from later reports, with the two numbers that were retracted left out. A reader comparing the two should not “correct” the gene down to the review — the review is one snapshot of a series that is still growing.
  • W34’s reported interaction with MC1R — possibly stronger on a chestnut background — is not modelled. “Possibly” is doing too much work in that sentence to buy an outcome with.
  • Pigment lost with age is not modelled, on this locus or anywhere: aging is out of scope, so the variants that depigment progressively render as their adult state.
What needs verifying here

Nine W alleles have never been spawned, but the half that can be wrong invisibly is whether two different boosters still count as two, whether W31/W31 is refused as nonviable, and whether W23/W23 really left the designer.
