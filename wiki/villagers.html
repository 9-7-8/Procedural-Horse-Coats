Gameplay / villagers

Villagers & transfer papers

The people who trade in horses, and the item that lets a horse be sold without any of them touching it. The cowboy lives in a barn on the edge of a plains village, never gets off their horse, and sells their herd as transfer papers; the four equestrians are ordinary villager professions — a leatherworker, a scientist, a supplier and a metalsmith — each selling their own part of a horse operation from a post of their own. This page is the source of truth for all of them. The items themselves are listed on Items & recipes; the genetics of the horses the cowboy breeds are ordinary breed founders.

Not verified in game

Everything on this page compiles, registers, and boots a dedicated server clean. None of it has been seen in a world. The checklist is verification §0-AC.

Transfer papers

A horse changes hands by paper, not by proximity. There are two items and three moments.

A signed paper is drawn as the horse it names — the mod's HD horse mesh in that horse's own coat, shrunk into an item slot (client/TransferDeedRenderer), in the merchant window and everywhere else the item appears. That is what makes a bearer instrument usable: the paper is a claim you have to walk out and collect, and six papers that differ only by a name in a tooltip are six papers you cannot tell apart from the animals they name. The deed carries the horse's genetic and epigenome codes for it, so the picture needs neither the horse loaded nor the server asked, and survives the horse being sold on or shot.

MomentWhat happensWhere
Crafted Eight paper around one horse hair makes one blank transfer paper, and the bench stamps the crafter's name and UUID onto it. TransferPaperHandler.onCrafted
Signed The bound player right-clicks a horse they currently own; the blank becomes a signed paper naming that horse. TransferPaperHandler.sign
Redeemed Whoever is holding the signed paper right-clicks the horse it names. The horse becomes theirs; the paper is spent. TransferPaperHandler.redeem

Why redemption is a walk, not a click

A signed paper is a bearer instrument. It carries no binding at all — the binding is on the blank, so that only someone with the standing to write a paper can write one — and redeeming it does not require being the person who signed it, or the person who bought it. Buying one from the cowboy moves nothing: the horse is still standing in their paddock, and you have to go out and find it.

That is the whole reason the item exists in this shape rather than as “pay the cowboy, receive a horse”. A claim the bearer has not collected yet is an object worth trading — it can be handed to another player, sold on at a markup, or lost in a creeper hole with the horse still standing there waiting for whoever picks it up.

What a transfer never changes

bredBy is untouched by every path in the system. A horse bred by Jesse Calloway is still bred by Jesse Calloway after four owners, and HorseRecord.attribution() — which prefers the breeder over the tamer — keeps saying so in the family tree. The signed paper carries a snapshot of the breeder purely so a buyer can read whose line the animal comes from; it is never written back.

tamedBy is only ever filled in when it is blank. It records who first got a rope on the animal, which also does not change when the animal is sold. In practice the only horses it gets written to are the cowboy's, because their stock has never been tamed by anyone.

The genome, the names, the pedigree, the generation and the breed label are not touched either. Ownership is the only thing that moves.

The cowboy

A horse breeder who is generated with a barn on the outskirts of a plains village, and who is always on a horse. Their name is drawn from frontier given and family name tables (PersonNameGenerator.cowboys()), and that name is what the game says when it says who bred one of their horses.

Why they are not a villager profession

They are a custom AbstractVillager, not a VillagerProfession. A profession is acquired from a job site by a villager whose brain runs vanilla's daily schedule, and what makes this character themselves — home is a barn and not a bed, stock is a live herd and not a trade table — is a fight with that brain. Extending AbstractVillager keeps the parts that are worth keeping (the merchant screen, the villager silhouette, the trading goals) and none of the schedule.

The original case rested partly on his being permanently mounted — vanilla's schedule would have dismounted him every morning to go and stand at a workstation, which was the sharpest version of the argument. He came off the horse a day later, so that half of it is gone. The split outlived the argument that produced it, because the other two halves were the load-bearing ones all along.

The four equestrians are real professions, for the opposite reason: a shopkeeper who walks to a workstation, restocks, gossips and goes to bed is exactly what that brain is for.

Where they set up

The structure places them bare in the middle of the barn and CowboyHandler builds them on their first server tick. Their home is that spot, but nothing walks them back to it any more — the dusk ride and the door sweep that both used to read it went with the mounted cowboy, so it is a remembered address rather than a routine. (CowboyDoorGoal still opens doors they bump into; it does not know about this.) They do not found there. The barn's interior is eleven blocks by five with walls on every side and a horse is nearly a block and a half wide, so a string of them made around the barn's centre came out with half its members standing in the walls.

So they walk out first, to a paddock 12–24 blocks off, and the herd is made around them there. The bearing is taken from the village bell to the barn and sampled in a half-plane around it, so “out of town” is literal: they set up on the grass past the last house rather than in somebody's turnip field. If that half-plane has no room in it the search widens to the whole circle rather than fall back to the barn.

A candidate has to be ground, and the surface heightmap is not enough to know that: in a village the highest solid block in a column is very often a roof, which is where the first cowboy to walk out ended up standing — on a house that was not even theirs. So a paddock must also sit within 3 blocks of the barn's own height and have 3 blocks of solid ground beneath it. Either test alone is fooled: the height test passes a low porch or a wall top, and the depth test passes the flat top of a hill fifteen blocks up. Together they mean “ground, at about the height the barn is at”, which is what a paddock is.

Every placement — their and every horse's, at founding and at every restock — now asks the level whether a horse-sized box fits, rather than whether a one-block column is clear. A clear column next to a wall still puts a horse's shoulders through it, which is exactly how the barn filled up with embedded horses.

Their herd

  • Four to ten horses. All are made on their first server tick, in the barn. That roll is also their stockTarget — the number they are restocked back up to for the rest of the world's life, so one cowboy is a four-horse outfit and the next is a ten-horse one, for good.
  • Every one is a foundation horse of a real breed — no parents, generation 0, and never feral_mixed.
  • Half their string is one breed — the one they are known for. A preferredBreed is settled at founding (drawn from the biome's pool, weighted the way a wild herd is weighted), and the first horse of the string is one. Every horse they breed after that is their breed until half the target is their breed, and drawn from the biome pool after that — so a breeder's yard reads as a yard with a speciality rather than as a random sample of the county. CowboyHandler.nextBreedFor counts rather than flips a coin, because a coin flip on a string of six lands on six-out-of-six often enough to be seen, and it self-corrects: sell three of their Fjords and the next three they breed are Fjords.
  • Every one carries bredBy = their name.
  • The herd's lead is the man himself. Every other herd in this mod leads with a horse; this one has had none to lead with since they stopped riding, so each member carries the cowboy's UUID as their HorseCareAttachment.herd rather than another horse's. The browser and the info panel only need the ids to agree, and they do.
  • They are branded, not owned. A CowboyBrand attachment holds the cowboy's id. Vanilla lets a player tame any untamed horse by climbing on it until it stops bucking, and their whole herd standing there untamed would otherwise be a free stable rather than a shop — so a branded horse refuses every interaction except a transfer paper. Kill the cowboy and the brand is cleared: their horses go feral and can be tamed the hard way, while keeping their name as their breeder.

What they sell

One offer per herd horse. A horse costs one to three emeralds — HorsePrices.DEFAULT_PRICE, and what almost every horse in the game costs. They are the early-game purchase this mod is built around: you buy a horse before you have a farm, not after you have a trading hall.

Which breeds they can deal in at all is the breed's own source checklist: they draw from the breeds of the country round the barn that allow cowboy, and falls back to every breed that allows it when the local list is empty. So “no dealer has ever had one” is a thing a breed file can actually say, and it is a different claim from “none run wild here”.

A breed that should be dear names its own range in its file ("price": [min, max]); anything that does not gets the default. A range rather than a number, because two horses of a breed are not worth the same and a stall listing six papers at exactly two emeralds each reads as a vending machine — the roll inside the range is taken from the horse's own UUID, so it is stable across every look at the merchant screen. The default and the roll live in server/HorsePrices, deliberately on the Minecraft side and off common/, for the same reason RarityItems is: how much a horse costs in emeralds is a gameplay-economy decision. What a breed gets to say is only “mine are dear”.

This replaced a table priced off the breed's Commonness tier that ran from ten emeralds to forty, which made the cowboy a shop you walked past for a week. No breed sets a range yet, so every horse they sell is currently 1–3.

The offer list is rebuilt every time someone looks at them, because the herd is a live thing — a horse can be killed by a wolf between one customer and the next. A horse leaves the list for good the moment its paper is bought, whether or not that paper is ever redeemed. They are not a levelling villager: no XP, no tiers, no vanilla restock timer — what they do instead is breed replacements.

Restocking and rotation

CowboyHandler.restock looks over their string about once a minute (jittered, so two cowboys loaded on the same tick do not stay in lockstep) and does two slow things.

  • Tops up to stockTarget, one horse per look. Horses leave — bought, tamed, eaten by a wolf — and without this a cowboy you have traded with once is a man standing next to an empty field.
  • Rotates a full string: roughly one look in seven, one horse retires and the top-up replaces it next time round. Without it a player who did not like the six horses on offer never has a reason to come back, because they would be the same six horses forever.

A horse is never retired with a player within 48 blocks of it — the rotation is meant to be something you notice by coming back to a different string, not something you watch happen; a horse vanishing in front of you reads as a bug however well it is documented. And “their string” here is exactly the set the merchant screen offers (alive, loaded, untamed, not already papered), because “how many have they got?” and “how many can you buy?” have to be the same number or they restock to fill a shelf nobody can see. Tamed horses are dropped from the herd list entirely, so it cannot grow without bound over a long world.

The arcane dealer

About one barn in twelve is founded by a man who deals in magic rather than in a breed. He is the same entity, the same barn, the same paddock, the same restock clock and the same papers — one saved flag, Cowboy.isArcane(), changes what he is carrying and what he is called. CowboyHandler.ARCANE_CHANCE is the roll, and it happens before the breed is picked, because it is the thing everything else about him follows from.

Why he exists Magical genes sit near a 0.1% founder weight in the wild. A player can play for a very long time without meeting one, and the wild population is not a realistic way to collect them — which made a large part of the registry something you could read about and never obtain. He is the answer to that: a dozen showing loci per horse, restocked and rotated, so go and buy the locus you are missing is a thing a player can actually do.
  • He stocks no breeds. Every horse is BreedLineage.MIXED over a feral_mixed founder roll. He keeps no preferredBreed, so he has no region, which is exactly right: he is not from anywhere.
  • One showing gene from each magical family — coat, yield, behaviour, emission, body-stat, ground and strong white, fields and regions, spots and rings, speckle and dust, lines and strokes, and mane and tail — plus a colour modifier on a quarter of them. So eleven or twelve magical loci on every animal.
  • Showing, never carried. Every one of those is an expressing pair, so a player can see what they are paying for. The rest of the horse stays wild type, which is what keeps eleven magical genes legible rather than a smear.
  • No two horses in his string share an allele combination. Keyed on the pair and not the gene: two of his horses may both show galaxy if one is homozygous and the other is not, because those are two different animals to breed from and a player collecting loci wants both.
  • He never keeps a gelding. The ordinary cowboy gelds half his colts because he is selling something to ride; this man is selling something to breed from, and a gelding cannot pass a gene on. The rule that makes his neighbour's string useful would make his worthless.
  • Twelve to twenty-eight emeralds (HorsePrices.ARCANE_PRICE), against one to three for an ordinary horse. The markup is a fact about the man, not about the animal — see below.
What you are buying is a gene bomb He breeds willy-nilly, and it shows. A dozen unrelated magical loci on one animal is almost never the single thing a player went there for, and isolating it is the work: cross out to a plain horse and half the bomb goes each generation, keep the foals that still show what you were after, and breed those back together until it runs true. No two of his horses are alike, so a second purchase is a second set of genes rather than a spare copy of the first. That is the intended shape of the feature — he sells raw material, not finished horses — and both the filter box and sneak-and-use exist so a player can tell what they are getting into before they pay.

Reading a horse you do not own

Sneak and use, with an empty hand, on any horse you have not tamed — wild, a stable's stock, or one of his — and the horse information screen opens on it. Without this the cowboy does not work: a player was being asked to pay twelve to twenty-eight emeralds for an animal whose genes are the entire product, with no way to see them. Buying blind is a raffle, not a purchase.

It stops short of “any horse” in one place, deliberately: a tamed horse keeps vanilla's behaviour, because sneak-and-use is how the saddle and armour slots open and taking that over would cost a player the ability to tack up. A tamed horse already reaches the same screen through the i button on that inventory. Reading is not owning: the barn-name box is hidden on a horse you do not own and a name tag refuses with a message, both on the screen and again on the server, so a crafted packet cannot rename a stranger's horse either.

The filter box, on every cowboy's window

A cowboy's window is a column of names and prices, and a name says nothing about the animal. Liveable when a cowboy sold six horses of one breed; not liveable for a dealer whose ten horses carry a hundred-odd magical loci between them and whose entire product is the genes. So there is a box above the list: type a breed, gene or allele and the string narrows to what answers it. Several words mean all of them, the way every search box a player has used behaves. HorseSearch matches breed label, the horse's own name, gene name and registry key, allele token and label, and the name of the expression the pair produces — so dapple finds every horse showing one, whatever gene put it there. Loci the horse is wild type for are not matched, or every horse would answer every query.

Why the server does the filtering A trade is sent back to the server as an index into the merchant's offer list. A client that quietly drew fewer rows would make row three mean one horse on screen and a different one on the server, and the player would buy the wrong animal — at twelve to twenty-eight emeralds, silently, with nothing anywhere reporting an error. So the box only ever sends what was typed (CowboyFilterPayload); the server rebuilds its own list in Cowboy.updateTrades and sends it back. One list, so the indices cannot drift apart. The filter is not saved and not synched: it belongs to one open window, and a new customer starts on the whole string.

He is also out on the road

A village is the odd place to meet a man who is not from anywhere, so he turns up away from one as well. ArcaneWandererHandler rolls near each player every ten seconds at SPAWN_CHANCE — roughly one dealer every two or three hours of play — places him on open ground 48 to 96 blocks off, and founds him through the same path the barn uses, minus the paddock walk he has no barn to take. Never a second one within 256 blocks of a player: one dealer is an event, three is a shop.

And he moves on again A Cowboy sets setPersistenceRequired() in its constructor, which is right for one that came with a structure and wrong for one that spawned on a hillside — every dealer the world ever rolled would still be standing in it, each with up to ten horses that also never despawn. A wanderer carries a wanderExpiry instead: after a day and a half he and his unsold string leave together, and only once no player is within 64 blocks, so he is never seen to vanish. A horse you have bought and redeemed is tamed, has lost its brand and has left his herd, so it is never taken.

Hostile mobs ignore every cowboy now (LivingChangeTargetEvent). Cowboy extends AbstractVillager, which vanilla zombies target by class — that is why Cowboy.HEALTH is two hundred in the first place. In a village that is survivable, because there are golems and walls and lit ground; a man stood alone on a hillside with ten horses has none of them. The failure that mattered was not him dying, since he mostly does not, but his string dying, and a dealer found alive beside his dead stock reads as a broken feature rather than a bad night. His two hundred health stays, because none of this stops a creeper, a fall or a player.

He does not stock the genes that make a noise

ArcaneStock.NOISY holds five keys, and they are named one at a time rather than detected, because nothing on a gene says “this is audible”. Up to ten horses in one pen, all carrying eleven magical loci, turns a gene that is a pleasant surprise on one animal into a paddock a player cannot shop in: meowing is a cat every fifteen seconds, singer plays the opening of a music disc every minute, and base_alarm is dominant and shouts at anything hostile. echolocate (only when tamed and ridden) and ender_echo (only when hurt) are the mild two, and the first to reconsider. None of them is otherwise changed — all five still breed, and still turn up in wild magical herds.

The limit of a list A third-party noisy gene will not be caught by this, and the honest fix for that is a flag on the gene rather than a longer list here. Genes that make no sound themselves but gather noisy mobs — spawner, pack_leader, the bait expression of magic_mob_aura — are deliberately still stocked. That is a different complaint, and a different fix if it turns out to be one.

The pools are generated, never listed

ArcaneStock builds a family's pool by walking Genes.magicalOrder() and asking GeneFamily.of, so a gene added by a data pack or another mod is in his string the moment it registers, with no edit anywhere. The only hard-coded thing in the class is which families a horse must cover, and a family is an enum constant rather than a list of genes. Four kinds of locus are held back, the same four MagicalVariant holds back and for the same reasons: the body-stat loci (owned by the breed's stat bands), feral-only genes, sex-linked genes, and disorders.

The order the families are drawn in is computed per horse rather than written down, scarcest first. The families are wildly different sizes — the shipped registry has one with three times another's stock — and a cramped family drawn last is the one that runs dry. Drawing it first costs nothing and means the horse that misses out is from the family with plenty. That also re-sorts itself when somebody drops in a folder of mane genes, which a hand-written order would not. ArcaneStockTest asserts every required family has room for a whole ten-horse string, so a pack that strips one bare fails a test rather than quietly shipping a dealer with a gene missing.

The markup is on the man, not the horse §4 is that nothing in the model scores a horse — no rarity tier, no “this is the good one”. So the price lives at the counter, like every other price: emeraldsFor(record, arcane) takes the dealer as an argument. A horse with eleven magical loci that a player bred themselves is worth nothing in particular, and that is correct.

He is named from arcane-alpha.txt and arcane-beta.txt — witch and wizard names, both halves off those tables. Not a Region: PersonNameGenerator keys on a plain string precisely so that something which is not a place can still have names, and the drop-in folder widens his tables the same way it widens a region's. He is also skipped by the village horse family in both directions — he neither founds a surname nor joins one, or the stable hand ends up a Blackthorn.

Their day

They do not ride

They did, for about a day, and it cost more than every other part of this character put together. A mob rider cannot steer, so the itinerary had to live on the horse; the horse's own goals all exist for the “someone is on my back” case and fought it; a villager has no seated pose, so they had to be sunk into the saddle to stop looking like they were standing on it; and getting a mounted man and eleven horses through a barn door at dusk was never made to work at all. Five rounds of fixes, each exposing the next.

The herd follows the man now. They are an ordinary villager who walks, and CowboyHerdGoal points their horses at them instead of at a lead horse. That deleted CowboyMountGoal, CowboyRemountGoal, CowboyRoutine, CowboyDoors, HerdCollision, the saddle sink, the patrol, the shelter and the flee — roughly a thousand lines for a character nobody could tell had them. They keep ten times a villager's health (Cowboy.HEALTH), which is what the night cost in the end.

So there is no routine to document. They float, trades, panics, strolls and looks at players — the goals every villager has, plus one of their own below. Their horses trail them at 14 blocks and settle at 7 by day, tightening to 4 after dark so the string gathers on them rather than strung out across a field.

They can work a door, in daylight, with both hands

CowboyDoorGoal. Until it existed they could not open a door at all — not for want of trying, but because a mob needs two unrelated things to use one and they had neither. The goal is what swings it; the navigator's canOpenDoors is what makes the pathfinder score a shut wooden door as a way through rather than as wall. On its own, either is useless: no goal and they plan a route they cannot take, no flag and they never plan the route at all.

  • Both leaves. Vanilla's OpenDoorGoal opens the one door block the path runs through, and every opening in the barn is a pair. One leaf is a one-block gap, and the ground pathfinder rounds a mob up to floor(width + 1) blocks across — so a one-block gap is not a gap at all to a horse, and the whole point of the man walking to the barn is the string walking in behind them. The partner leaf is the neighbour along the wall with the same facing and the opposite hinge.
  • Daylight only, on Level.isBrightOutside() — the same test the gene effects use for their day trigger, so the mod has one definition of daytime. It reads sky darkening rather than the clock, so a thunderstorm counts as night.
  • They never close anything. OpenDoorGoal's close-behind would be wrong here twice over: the herd is following them and would be shut out, and the barn doors standing open is what the doorway headroom in bake-barn.py was cleared for.

None of it is a routine — they open a door they have walked into, which is vanilla's own cheap trigger, and nothing sends them at one on purpose. What it buys is that the barn is now a building the outfit can actually get inside. Gap 63 is the part that is designed rather than measured.

They do not go indoors, and does not need to

They cannot. Walking into a house and getting into a bed lives in vanilla's villager brain, and a Cowboy is an AbstractVillager — the same base as a wandering trader, chosen precisely so that no schedule drags them away from their horses. A wandering trader does not go indoors either. Giving them somewhere to sleep is what the entire barn-shelter subsystem was, and it is written up in gap 55 as the thing that could not be made to work.

So the night is survived rather than avoided, on two numbers and no behaviour:

  • Ten times a villager's health (Cowboy.HEALTH).
  • Nothing hunts them. CowboySafetyHandler cancels LivingChangeTargetEvent whenever an Enemy is about to pick them, and that event fires from Mob.setTarget — the one door every way of acquiring a target goes through, whether that is a nearest-attackable goal, a raid's orders or retaliation for being hit. They are never anybody's target, so nothing paths to them and nothing swings at them.

Enemy rather than Monster on purpose: it is the interface that means “this thing is hostile”, and it catches the few hostiles that are not Monster subclasses. Players are not covered and can still hit them. Their horses are not covered either — they are ordinary animals, a wolf getting one is a thing that happens, and that is what the restocking is for.

The barn, and how it lands on the outskirts

cowboy_barn.nbt is the whole homestead in one piece: a hand-built stable — hay-bedded floor, water cauldrons, two double doors at each end, the cowboy generated at its centre — with the house behind it along the street, and a walk running down the front of both.

The walk, and the course it lies in

Worth knowing before editing the bake, because it is the one thing about the piece's geometry that is not obvious from looking at it. Layer 0 of a building is not the ground — it is the course that rests on the ground. The street's road block is the layer below it, outside the piece entirely, which is why a plains house sits one block proud of the road with a step up into the doorway.

So a path laid in layer 0 is a block-high curb you walk over, which is what the lane between the two buildings was. The piece now carries a ground course beneath the foundation: everything lifts by one and the jigsaw lifts with it, so nothing about where the homestead lands changes, and the new bottom layer falls in the road's own course. The walk runs down it.

It has a column of its own, west of both buildings, because it has to be in front of the steps and there is nowhere else for it to go. One column further west is the street's own connector block; a piece whose box reaches over that overlaps the street piece and is thrown out by the placer, so the homestead would simply never generate. And it cannot run under the steps either: DirtPathBlock will not survive a solid block overhead and ticks back to plain dirt, so a walk beneath the stair skirt would be a walk made of dirt. In front of the steps, nothing is covered and nothing converts.

Terminators are the edge of the village

The barn is appended to minecraft:village/plains/terminators. A terminator is what the village generator places when a street connector has nowhere left to go: both village/plains/streets and village/plains/houses name it as their fallback. So the terminator slots are, by construction, the ends of the village — the last piece on a road that ran out of room. Putting the barn there is the closest the jigsaw system comes to expressing “on the outskirts” without hand-placing the building and owning terrain fitting ourselves.

The homestead carries three minecraft:street jigsaws, all of them in the walk column down its west edge: one facing west in the step in front of the barn's north pair of doors, one facing north at the top of the path, and one facing south at its foot. The generator rotates the whole piece so that one of them faces back at whatever it attached to.

Why three, and what one was costing

A jigsaw pair fixes a piece's rotation completely. The generator turns the candidate until its connector faces back at the street connector, and for a piece with one connector exactly one of the four rotations does that — so the homestead always landed the same way round relative to the road: sixteen blocks straight out, with two blocks of its width on one side of the road's line and fifteen on the other, every single time. If those fifteen blocks had a village house standing in them, the piece failed the free-space test, the slot fell through to a vanilla terminator, and there was no second arrangement for the generator to try.

With a connector on three faces, three of the four rotations attach, so the generator gets three attempts at the same slot and keeps whichever one clears the ground it has. JigsawPlacement shuffles the rotations before trying them, so which of the three it settles on is not biased.

They are all in the walk column because the walk is the approach. Whichever end the road meets, it meets the path that runs down the front of both buildings, past the hitch, the door, the four posts and the bench — head-on at the barn doors, down from the top of the walk, or up from its foot with the house first. A connector in the middle of the north or south face instead would be a road running into the barn's own wall with no way in; the walk column is the only column of the piece a street may legitimately arrive at.

Two things checked in JigsawPlacement before adding them

The box does not grow. Villages set use_expansion_hack: true, which inflates a candidate's bounding box to make room for children — but only for a connector whose forward neighbour is inside the candidate's own box. All three of these face out of it, so the expansion is zero and the fit is tested against the piece as drawn.

No stray jigsaw is left behind. Only one connector is used; JigsawReplacementProcessor runs over the whole piece and swaps every jigsaw block for its final_state, so a placed homestead has none of them in it. All three resolve to air over the path, which is what the walk needs — a solid one would sit on the path and tick it back to dirt.

And all three at y=0

They sit at y=0 — the barn's own foundation course — and getting that wrong is what sank the building. A jigsaw pair lands its two blocks at the same world height, and the street connector it meets is at the street piece's y=1, one above the road block. So whichever local layer carries this jigsaw is the layer that ends up resting on the ground. It was y=1 at first, on the reasoning that vanilla terminators put theirs one above their ground block — which is true of terminators and the wrong piece to copy. The result was the foundation landing in the road's own layer: the stair skirt round both doorways was buried flush with the ground instead of stepping up onto it, and the barn read as half-dug-in.

Vanilla houses are the shape to copy. A plains house puts its building_entrance jigsaw in its own foundation layer, and so sits one block proud of the road with a step up into the doorway. At y=0 the barn does the same, and its steps are steps again.

Each jigsaw block replaces whatever the bake had already laid in its cell, so the bake reads that block back out of the grid and writes it as the jigsaw's final_state — no hand-copied block state to go stale, and re-drawing the piece with something else in one of those spots just works. In the walk column that block is air above the path, and the bake asserts it: a solid final_state there would be a block standing on the walk, and DirtPathBlock does not survive a solid block overhead.

The same 265 villages both times — seeds 1–40 — so the difference is the piece and not the sample.

Both numbers are a surprise, and the first one is the important one. Everything written here before said the opposite: that a piece the better part of a chunk square, squeezed into a slot vanilla sizes for a two-by-three stub, must fail the bounding-box check on most of the draws that picked it — and therefore that the homestead was in danger of having become rare, or of having stopped generating at all when the house was deepened for the four equestrians. It never was. Several slots at three-sevenths each is simply a great many chances, and a slot lost to a neighbouring house costs the village nothing as long as another slot takes the piece.

So the extra connectors are worth having — they closed fifteen of the eighteen villages that had none — but they are an improvement and not a rescue, and the weight was never doing the work it was raised to do. It is also not free to raise: the pool is shared with vanilla's four terminators, and crowding them out changes the look of every plains village rather than just ours. Reach for the bake before the number, and for the census before either.

The connector split on that run was north=88 south=102 west=72. That is not a measure of which connectors were needed: JigsawPlacement shuffles the rotations, so the three are drawn evenly and whichever is tried first and fits wins. What it does show is that all three are live — two thirds of homesteads now stand a way round the piece could not manage that morning.

How it is counted, and how to count it again

worldgen/HomesteadCensus, run from the headless test homestead_still_generates: ./gradlew :neoforge-26.1.2:runGameTest. It generates no chunks. Structure.generate is the village generator — it runs the whole jigsaw placement, every bounding-box test included, and hands back the finished piece list without touching a world — so a few hundred villages is seconds, and the answer is exact rather than an impression formed while walking. Seeds are 1..n rather than random so that two runs can be compared, which is what makes a before-and-after possible at all.

The sample is small by default to keep the suite quick. PHC_CENSUS_SEEDS and PHC_CENSUS_VILLAGES are read from the environment, so the numbers above are one run with two variables in front of it. The test asserts a floor of 75% rather than a rate: it is there to catch the thing collapsing, and a worldgen number pinned exactly gets edited rather than read.

What it does not answer: only whether the piece wins its slot. Whether the homestead then looks right where it landed, whether the villagers claim their posts, and whether the ground under it is somewhere you would want to live are all still a walk — see verification.

Why the pool is edited in code

A datapack can only replace terminators.json, never add to it. Replacing it would freeze today's four vanilla terminators into this mod and silently delete any other mod's additions to the same pool. BarnPoolInjector appends instead, on ServerAboutToStartEvent, which needs the live pool object's element lists — and that is what this repo's first access transformer is for.

The cowboy comes with the building

They are carried in the structure template's entities list, which is the same mechanism vanilla uses to put villagers in village/plains/villagers/*.nbt. A template can only place an entity, not roll one, so they arrive bare — nothing but their id — and CowboyHandler builds them on their first server tick: name, home, herd.

That founding runs on EntityTickEvent.Post and not on the join event, for exactly the reason HorseFoundingTickHandler documents at length: founding spawns horses, spawning a horse ends in an Attributes.SCALE write, and a scale write runs a collision scan that can force-load a chunk. Doing that from the join event or a server.execute task re-enters the chunk system's ticket pass and crashes it.

One cowboy per village

The jigsaw generator has no way to say “at most one of this element per structure”, so a village that rolls two barns gets two barns. The second one is just a barn: of the cowboys within 128 blocks of each other exactly one founds, and the rest discard themselves before spawning anything. The tie-break is the lowest UUID — the same rule HerdManager uses to elect a wild herd's lead, and for the same reason: every candidate has to reach the same answer without talking to the others.

Re-baking the piece

Two sources, one output. tools/barn/cowboy_barn.source.nbt is the raw structure-block save of the stable; tools/barn/cowboy_house.source.nbt is a byte copy of vanilla's village/plains/houses/plains_small_house_1. Both are kept only so the buildings can be edited in game and re-exported; the mod never reads either. After a re-export, run: 

python neoforge-26.1.2/tools/barn/bake-barn.py

which adds everything a structure-block save cannot carry — the ground course, the jigsaw connector, the headroom over the doorways, the reshaped house, the walk along the front, the work posts and the villagers — and writes data/horsegenetics/structure/cowboy_barn.nbt. Commit all three.

The four equestrians

The roadmap’s “Horse Master” shipped as one villager, and one villager sold tack, carrots, armour, papers, eggs and tickets across five tiers. That made him a shop rather than a tradesman, and left no room for any of it to get better as he did. He is retired outright — no alias, no transitional profession, so a villager who held the old job comes back unemployed — and four ordinary villager professions take his place, each with a post, a POI, a hat colour and five tiers of its own. All four run on vanilla’s ladder: Novice, Apprentice, Journeyman, Expert, Master — not the design document’s, which invented an “Adept” rung that does not exist.

The hats are generated, not drawn four times. neoforge-26.1.2/tools/villagers/bake-profession-hats.ps1 takes one piece of source art — the old horseman’s overlay, kept the way tools/barn/*.source.nbt is kept, as an input to a bake rather than as an asset — and retints the hat in each of the four colours, leaving the belt across the jacket brown in all of them. One outfit, four hats: they are meant to read as one family. The retint keeps each texel’s brightness relative to the hat’s mean rather than flooding it, so the shading the art was drawn with survives.

The script writes a fifth file, and that is the point of it being a script at all: textures/entity/villager/cowboy.png, the same art with the hat left its own brown. The cowboy used to read the horseman’s profession PNG straight, which was the cheapest possible guarantee that the two could not drift. That stopped being possible the moment the horseman became four files in four colours — there is nothing at that path any more, and picking one of the four would have handed him somebody else’s hat. The guarantee is now held by the bake instead of by a shared path, which is the stronger of the two.

None of the four may be purple

Owner’s call, and it is a constraint rather than a preference. Purple is taken: CowboyOverlayLayer.ARCANE_HAT tints the arcane dealer’s hat, and he is the one villager in this mod a player has to be able to pick out of a crowd at a glance. A fifth purple hat would cost him that.

Five posts, one texture

Four blocks rather than one shared post, because a POI hands out exactly one profession — vanilla picks the job from the job site claimed, not the other way round — so four jobs need four blocks. With the Cowboy Hitch that is five posts, and they are the same block five times: one model, one three-face texture set, five names and five jobs. Making each look like the trade it belongs to is open work.

All five are shapeless, and all five are distinct. Every one takes two horse hair and two fences of any kind; what is left of the seven slots is the part that says which post it is — leather for the leatherworker, glass bottles and paper for the scientist, two more hair and a carrot for the supplier, iron for the metalsmith, and three more hair for the hitch. neoforge-26.1.2/tools/check-recipes.mjs refuses two recipes that claim the same inputs, which is what keeps that true as they are retuned.

BlockHands outHow
The four *_post blocks the four equestrians, one each Ordinary job sites. Each backs a POI of its own name, and all four are added to vanilla’s acquirable_job_site and village tags — tags merge across datapacks, so adding to vanilla’s is safe where overriding a worldgen file would not be. One villager per post, so a second scientist needs a second Scientist’s Post.
Cowboy Hitch the cowboy Not a job site at all. A cowboy is an entity, not a profession — they have a herd, a stock target and a merchant screen that sells papers rather than goods, none of which fits on a Villager — so there is nothing for a profession to point at and no POI to claim. server/CowboyHitchHandler looks for the block instead: every two seconds, an unemployed villager within 6 blocks of a hitch that has no cowboy within 32 is taken on.
Why a post each and not one post that alternates

One block tried to do two jobs, handing out a cowboy first and a shopkeeper second by alternating. It could not. Converting the villager meant either discarding them — which silently holds their job-site ticket for ever, because Villager.releaseAllPois is private and runs on death, so the post produced one cowboy and then nothing while the rest of the village stood unemployed — or demoting them and trusting vanilla to re-assign them, which is two more moving parts than a second block.

A post each is far less clever and simply works, and it is the same answer the POI system forces for the four professions anyway. Discarding the villager is safe at the hitch for exactly the reason it was not safe at the shared post: they never claimed the hitch, because the hitch is not a job site.

One cowboy per hitch, guarded by looking rather than remembering: a hitch with a cowboy already within 32 blocks is spoken for. That is self-correcting — kill them and the next villager past the hitch takes over — where a “used” flag on the block would need saving and would go stale the moment they died.

All five drop themselves when broken. Worth writing down because for a while one of them did not: cowboy_hitch shipped with no loot table at all, so breaking one destroyed it. The four new posts were given theirs at the same time the hitch got its.

All five stand at the house

They run down the house’s frontage in the order you meet them walking in from the street: the hitch first, then the doorstep, then the four trade posts, then the bench at the far end. The hitch comes first because the cowboy is the one who has to exist before the rest of the homestead means anything, and the bench comes last because nobody is employed by it. A villager stands on the walk in front of each post — one per post, none for the bench — and takes the job behind them.

They were on the barn, and moving them is what fixed the house. Not a question of where they look right: the house has to exist before any post does, and while the house was built at runtime by the cowboy, the posts had to be somewhere the cowboy already was. That ordering is what finally sank the runtime house — the posts are what make a cowboy, so a cowboy cannot be what builds the thing the posts stand on. With both buildings in one baked piece there is no order left to get wrong, and the posts can go where they belong: on the doorstep of the house whose family works them.

Baked into the structure rather than placed at runtime, because a job-site POI is indexed off the block — putting the block in the world is the whole mechanism — and because a shop front is architecture. None of the five villagers is given a profession, and that is the point rather than an oversight: each has to claim its own post the ordinary way and come out a cowboy, a leatherworker, a scientist, a supplier or a metalsmith. Letting them take the jobs themselves is the only test there is of the part that has ever been in doubt — whether every POI registered and whether the tag merged for all of them — so a row of nitwits standing there for ever is a real answer to a real question, and one that names which post is the broken one.

Nothing about the frontage is counted by hand. bake-barn.py reads the west wall’s own foundation course off the grid it has just built and takes that as the run of columns a post may stand in — a post past the end of the wall would hang in the air over the walk, and the eave reaches further than the building does, so “is there something overhead” is not the test. Every post is then asserted to front wall and to land on an empty cell before it is placed, so moving the door moves the whole frontage with it rather than quietly eating the doorstep.

One family per village

Whoever is hired first names the family, and everyone after joins it. Both handlers ask the same question — EquestrianHandler.familySurname — which looks round the village for a cowboy or an equestrian who already has a surname and takes theirs; finding nobody, it coins a new one. So a village reads as one outfit in the horse trade: Wade Hargreave at the hitch, Frank Hargreave behind the leatherworker’s post, and the scientist, the supplier and the metalsmith Hargreaves down the same frontage.

Every profession check in that class asks ModVillagerProfessions.isEquestrian rather than naming one key, so a fifth equestrian added later joins the family by existing and cannot be left out of it by somebody forgetting the file.

And the name it coins comes from the same place the horses do. Both halves are drawn from the region the outfit is from, so the man selling Fjords is a Halvorsen and the man selling Andalusians is an Olivares. The cowboy reads his region off the breed he is known for, which is why CowboyHandler.found now settles that breed before it names him — naming him first threw the connection away. An equestrian has no breed of their own, so they take the region from the nearest cowboy who has one; in a village with no cowboy at all the first one hired is the founder and their region is simply rolled.

The name tables are drop-in, like breeds and genes. Every .txt in .minecraft/phc/names/ adds names to one region — scandinavia-alpha.txt for given names, -beta.txt for family names — and the folder is made with a README in it on first launch. The format is the shipped one: comma-separated, with newlines counting as ordinary whitespace, so one name per line reads exactly the same as one long line. It adds rather than replaces, deliberately: a drop-in holding ten names would otherwise make every trader in that region draw from ten, and widening a region is the thing worth doing without a rebuild. A file naming a region that does not exist, or ending in neither half, is reported in the log rather than silently ignored — and it must be saved as UTF-8, or accented names arrive as question marks with nothing to warn you.

Order-independent on purpose. The hitch and the four posts hand out their jobs entirely separately, and which villager gets round to which first is down to where they happen to be standing — so no one role can be the one that owns the name. An earlier version had the cowboy coin it and the horseman inherit, which is fine right up until the horseman is hired first and there is nobody to inherit from.

The nearest namesake wins, which only matters in a village with two outfits in it: a second hitch across town founds the Adamses, and a post put up beside it joins the Adamses rather than the Hargreaves at the far end. It counts the roles this mod names and nothing else — a player who renames their own farmer “Bob” has not founded a horse family.

Why a poll and not an event. Taking a job is not something NeoForge fires an event for: it happens deep in vanilla’s brain, when a villager’s AcquirePoi behaviour claims a ticket, and the only outward sign is that their VillagerData now names a profession. So EquestrianHandler watches for that — one profession comparison per villager tick — and announces it. That line is also the cheapest answer to the hardest thing about these four to test: when nothing happens there are three indistinguishable reasons (the POI did not register, the tag did not merge, or they simply have not got round to it), and the announcement separates the third from the first two.

They keep vanilla's brain, and get a house

Nothing in this mod touches how an equestrian behaves. They are completely ordinary villagers with a profession, so going indoors at night, panicking in a raid, hiding from a zombie, restocking and gossiping are all free — that is the whole reason they are professions where the cowboy is not.

What that brain needs is a bed POI to claim, and a barn on the outskirts can easily be out of range of the nearest house's. Beds went into the barn first and came straight back out: the barn is a building made of doors. Four double doors and a two-block gap at either end, and a villager only ever shuts the one they walked through — so a bed in there is a bed in a corridor with three doors standing open.

So they get a house, and it is part of the same structure piece as the barn. It is a copy of vanilla's plains_small_house_1, copied rather than referenced because it needs changing and because a vanilla template is not ours to rely on keeping its shape. tools/barn/bake-barn.py reshapes it:

  • The two jigsaw blocks come out. They are placement machinery and nothing resolves them for a template read straight out of the data pack, so left in they would stand in the finished building as jigsaw blocks. The one in the doorway becomes air, the one in the floor becomes floor.
  • One column wider, and three rows deeper, both by duplicating a cross-section of the plan rather than by drawing a new house — so every wall, corner, eave and roof stair stays exactly what vanilla drew. Vanilla’s three across is a single-file squeeze past the end of a bed; four is the bed and an aisle. The depth is set by the frontage rather than by the floor space: the west wall has to carry the hitch, the doorway, four posts and the bench standing side by side, which is more wall than vanilla’s house has.
  • A bed per sleeper against the wall opposite the door, heads to the wall and feet to the aisle. Five of them, because the frontage hands out five jobs: a villager who claims a job site and has no bed to go home to never sleeps and never restocks, so the bed count is the other half of the post count and the bake asserts the two agree rather than trusting them to.
  • Two chests, one in each end of the one interior row the beds do not take, drawing on horsegenetics:chests/cowboy_house: feed by the armful, a lead or a saddle now and then, and one roll in four or so at a piece of this mod's own kit, so a chest can teach you an item exists. Nothing in it should make a player feel they can skip the shops — the saddle is the only real prize. Both are asserted to have an empty cell to stand in and open from.
  • The stair beside the beds comes out. It is a chair in the vanilla house and it reads as a headboard once there are beds against that wall.
  • A step outside the front door, with the five posts and the bench down the frontage from it. The house sits a block proud of the ground the way every vanilla plains house does, so without the step the threshold is a ledge you jump at.
  • Bark back on the corner posts. They are stripped oak in vanilla; nothing else about a homestead beside a stable is planed.
The door has to be lifted out before the house is deepened

Which row is duplicated is not free choice. At the top course of the roof, vanilla's three rows are a south-sloping stair course, the ridge, and a north-sloping stair course — and only the ridge tiles. Copy it and the ridge simply gets longer; copy either neighbour and the house grows a flat band of stairs all sloping the same way where the roof should be. The same row is the only one that repeats at every other height too, and it is also the door’s row.

So the door is duplicated along with everything else unless something is done about it, and the west wall grows one door per copy. bake-barn.py lifts both halves out first, fills the hole with the wall vanilla drew one row along, deepens the house, and sets the same two states back down at the doorway — read out of the source rather than written in, so the hinge and facing are whatever the export says and the door that goes back is the door that came out.

Where the inside of the house is is read off the finished grid rather than counted up from the two duplications by hand: vanilla floors the room in oak planks and nothing else in the building is oak planks at the foundation course, so the plank footprint is the interior by construction, however the bake is retuned.

It is set down beside the barn with a lane of dirt_path between them, the same block the village's own streets are made of, running along the front of both and up to each door. Two buildings on the edge of a village with nothing between them read as two things that happen to be near each other.

It used to be built at runtime and it never once appeared. CowboyHouseBuilder hunted the far side of the barn for a patch that was flat, clear and untouched — every column of the footprint agreeing on its surface height — on the reasoning that no house is a great deal better than a house dropped through somebody's roof. In a real village that test never passed once. Baking the house into the piece answers the same worry better than the test did: the jigsaw generator's own bounding-box check is a far more careful version of “is there room here”, and it runs before anything is placed at all. The price is a deeper piece, paid for in pool weight — and the four equestrians have just made it deeper again.

The cowboy will not use a bed — an AbstractVillager has no schedule at all — which is why they get ten times the health and hostile mobs that ignore them instead. The fifth bed is his all the same; it is the count that has to match the posts, not the sleeping.

The tiers that list four trades rather than three are the ones where that matters most. Hair buying is still the early-game emerald tap, and it is now the supplier's alone — hair, bundles, rope and cloth all pass over his counter, which is what makes him the one a player meets first and the number most likely to want retuning.

The metalsmith is deliberately thin, and with vanilla alone cannot easily be otherwise. Vanilla has three horse armours above leather, which is not five tiers of anything. So his ladder is the same three armours getting cheaper and more plentiful as he rises, against ingots and diamonds getting dearer to sell him — a smith who gets better at his trade rather than one who learns new tricks. The alternative was giving him someone else's stock to pad him out, which is the thing the split was for.

What fills him out: other mods' metals

The thinness was always a vanilla problem, and it stops being one the moment anything else in the pack adds a metal. This mod generates one horse armour per modded ingot or gem, and the metalsmith stocks every one of them — so a pack with tin, silver, lead and ruby in it gives him four more armours to sell than the three he shipped with, without a line of trade authoring.

Which tier sells which is the armour's own strength ladder, not a second opinion. A generated armour's protection is worked out once (their own chestplate if it is readable, else a guess from the common tag and the item's rarity, else iron) and its tier is read straight off that number by GeneratedArmour.tierFor — weakest at Novice, strongest at Master, with the cut points in that one method and nowhere else. The prices sit inside the shipped armours' own range, and the expensive tiers restock less. A player who levels him up for the good stuff should find that the good stuff is good, so the shop's ladder and the armour's ladder have to be the same ladder.

His tier files are replaced, and they carry the shipped trades

minecraft:trade_set is a datapack registry, not a tag. Registry files do not merge across packs — the higher pack wins outright — and the generated pack sits at the top, so a generated level_3.json is the metalsmith's third tier, not an addition to it. It therefore has to carry the shipped trades as well as the new ones.

It gets them by reading the shipped file off our own classpath rather than by keeping a second copy of the list in Java. Edit a tier in data/horsegenetics/trade_set/equestrian_metalsmith/ and the generated one follows on the next launch; it cannot go stale. If that read ever fails, the generated trades are written on their own — which loses the vanilla armours from that tier and keeps the modded ones, on the grounds that a tier missing three trades beats a tier that is empty for everybody.

None of this has been seen in a shop. The generated trades have only ever been exercised against a synthetic test mod on a dedicated server, where what was confirmed is that the files are written and the datapack parses with no errors — not that a metalsmith offers them, not that the tiers are sane, and not that a tinted armour draws in a trade window. See §0-FK.

A random carrot, without a custom listing

Villager trades are datapack registries in 26.1.2 (minecraft:villager_trade and minecraft:trade_set), and a trade's given_item_modifiers are loot functions that run when the offer is generated. That is exactly the semantics the roadmap argued for: “a random rare carrot” is rolled once per restock and then sits in the window at a fixed price, rather than re-rolling under the player's cursor. So the whole “custom listing that rolls when the offer is generated” requirement is met by a stock vanilla mechanism plus one loot function: 

{ "function": "horsegenetics:set_random_gene",
  "target": "carrot",          // or "paper"
  "min_rarity": "uncommon",
  "max_rarity": "uncommon" }

The pool is computed, not listed: a gene is a candidate if it is registered, has a gene carrot, and its GeneRarity falls inside the window. A third-party gene therefore joins automatically, and legendary and mythic are never sold because no window reaches them — a comparison rather than a blacklist that can go stale. Within a window, draws are weighted by GeneRarity.lootWeight(), the same weighting the chest-loot papers use, so the price ladder and the find-it-in-the-wild ladder agree.

An empty window returns an empty stack, which VillagerTrade.getOffer reads as “no offer” and drops — so a rarity tier with no genes in it costs a trade slot rather than putting a blank item on sale.

The carrot that breeds true

The scientist's master tier adds one optional field: 

{ "function": "horsegenetics:set_random_gene",
  "target": "carrot",
  "min_rarity": "rare",
  "max_rarity": "rare",
  "homozygous": true }

A known gene splice names both alleles of the gamete rather than carrying a flag, so “the carrot that breeds true” is not a separate concept — it is the pair X/X where an ordinary one is n/X. Which of the two a carrot gets is normally the gene's own call (Gene.geneCarrotHomozygous); this overrides it upward, and the foal is then certain to get the gene rather than getting it on a coin flip. That is what a fifty-emerald carrot has to be selling.

It cannot force a pair the gene forbids. A locus whose homozygote is lethal — or that otherwise fails Gene.canOccur — would make an inert carrot, which is worse than not selling one, so those genes are skipped at the draw rather than sold and silently doing nothing.

Dyed tack, on a ladder you can see

The leatherworker's five tiers are the same trick again, with a function of their own: 

{ "function": "horsegenetics:set_tack_tint",
  "palette": "flowers" }

Five palettes — flowers, garden, kiln, deep, lapis — ordered by what the dye costs to get rather than by taste. At his first tier he deals in what you could dye a thing with on the walk to his post, bone meal and whatever is growing; at his last he is selling lapis on diamond fittings. A flat random roll cannot express that, because the expense of the dye is the progression. Seat and bridle roll separately within a palette, so a piece reads as somebody's choice rather than as one flat colour.

This is not vanilla's dyed_color. That component reaches the blanket of leather horse armour and nothing at all on a saddle. This writes the mod's own three-zone tack_tint, which is what the equipment assets this mod ships actually read — so it colours the saddle too, and it sets the metal fittings, which no vanilla component can express.

That is why the dyed-saddle trade is back. It was written and withdrawn once, for selling a fourteen-emerald saddle indistinguishable from the eight-emerald one; with three tinted zones on the saddle it now sells something you can see, and the objection has expired rather than been overruled.

The older horsegenetics:set_random_dye — no parameters at all, stamps one of the sixteen dye colours onto whatever it is given — is still registered and is still what a datapack wants for a plain dyed item. The sixteen dyes rather than a free RGB roll, because the point is an item that looks like one somebody dyed, and a random 24-bit colour mostly lands on muddy shades no dye can make.

Breed eggs, and the region filter

Breed spawn eggs are the same trick with a third function, horsegenetics:set_random_breed, which takes a commonness window instead of a rarity one: 

{ "function": "horsegenetics:set_random_breed",
  "commonest": "rare",
  "rarest": "very_rare" }

The membership test is the breed's own source checklist — a breed is a candidate if it allows spawn_egg — so a breed a player drops into .minecraft/phc/breeds/ joins the scientist's stock by being registered, and one that opts out disappears from it, with nothing edited either way. Within a window, draws are weighted by the breed's own spawnWeight. Three functions rather than one parameterised thing, for the reason there are two loot modifiers: a paper is knowledge and an egg is an animal, and a datapack ought to be able to retune one without the other.

The window is then narrowed to the scientist’s own part of the world. He has no region of his own, so he takes one from the nearest cowboy in town — the same nearest-wins scan his surname uses — and stocks only breeds from that region’s countries. With no cowboy in town there is no connection and so no filter, and he sells the world; a dungeon chest is unfiltered for the same reason, having no villager to ask. Nothing is stored on him, so a cowboy who moves in or dies changes what is on the counter at the next restock.

A region with nothing in a window costs him that trade. Three of the ten regions have no breed at all in the commonest band, so their scientist has no common breed egg to sell: the function returns an empty stack and vanilla drops the offer, which is the same path a player switching most breeds off already takes. That is the owner’s call over the two alternatives, and the reason is that both of them lie — widening the band sells an uncommon breed at the common price, and falling back to the world pool puts a Marwari on a Scandinavian counter, which is exactly the case a player would notice.

What the roadmap asked for and this does not do

  • No random genotype vials, and no buying player-made ones back. Both need the assisted-reproduction / collectedFrom work that is still unbuilt. The scientist's buy row is a stallion seed jar instead, which is a real player-made item today.
  • No custom gossip type. HORSE_TRADER was flagged as unverified; all four use vanilla gossip, which costs nothing and works.
  • The four posts are still four names on one block. None of them does anything beyond marking a spot, and none of them looks like the trade it belongs to. A hitch you tie a horse to and a bench you work leather at are open work.
