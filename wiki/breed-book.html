Gameplay / who the horses are, and where they came from

The breed book

Nearly every wild horse in this world belongs to a breed — a real one, with a name, a shape, a set of colours it runs to and a country it came from that is not here. This page is the field guide to every one of them, and the story of how they arrived. The breeds page is the machinery: weights, stat curves, founder rolls, crosses. This one is for reading.

Nothing here is native

Start with the thing the game never says out loud: these are not wild animals that happened to evolve into a Clydesdale. A Shire is a thousand years of somebody choosing which colt to keep. A Falabella is a deliberate, patient argument about how small a horse can be. Every breed in this book is an artefact — the surviving output of a civilisation that had time to care about the difference between a carriage horse and a plough horse, and no longer exists.

They were brought here. Not by the people who made them, as far as anyone can tell, and not recently. Somebody else — a race nobody has a name for and nobody living has met — gathered up the stock of a collapsed world and moved it, wholesale, into this one. Whole breeding programmes, lifted and set down. Then that race went away too, or stopped coming, or is still here and simply not introducing itself.

What is left is a world full of horses that were designed for jobs that no longer exist, doing extremely well. The draughts have nothing to pull. The coach horses have no coaches. The desert breeds are hundreds of leagues from any desert their ancestors would recognise, and they are fine — the herds are healthy, the foals are strong, the lines have not collapsed into one muddy brown average the way an abandoned population is supposed to.

They are also, unmistakably, still fond of people. A wild Shetland that has never seen a human being will come and eat out of your hand if you crouch and wait. That is not how a feral animal behaves. Whatever was bred into these horses over all those centuries, being glad to see us was part of it, and it did not wash out.

What you actually meet

A horse that spawns in the wild is almost always a member of a breed, not a random draw from the whole gene pool. The world picks a breed appropriate to the biome, then rolls a founder within that breed — so a herd on the badlands is a Mustang herd, and it looks like one, and its foals will look like one.

That is why the wild population has structure instead of soup. It is also why a breed is worth learning to recognise: the name tells you what the horse is likely to be carrying, roughly how big it will grow, and what it is good at, before you have laid a hand on it. A minority of wild horses are Feral Mixed — the descendants of horses that got out and interbred — and those are the genuine grab bags.

See herd spawning for how a band is assembled, and crosses and mixes for what happens when you put two breeds together yourself.

The things that still work

The horses are not the only thing that got left behind.

The hay portal is the clearest case. A door made of hay bales, built to a pattern nobody taught you, that opens onto somewhere else — and the somewhere else is full of horses. It is not a natural feature. Somebody made it, for a reason, and the reason had something to do with moving stock. Whether it is magic or machinery is a distinction that may not have meant anything to whoever built it. How it works is not knowable from this side. That it works is not in question: you can walk through it.

The breeding carrots are the same story in a smaller package. Feed one to a mare and the foal she carries is different, in the specific way the carrot said it would be. That is not husbandry. That is something reaching into an animal and adjusting it, and then being eaten by a horse, and the horse being perfectly happy about it. The recipes survive — the gene splices, the research papers, the whole apparatus of the carrot workshop — and they still do exactly what they were built to do, centuries after the last person who understood them stopped writing anything down.

The honest position on all of it is: it works, and nobody knows why. The mod does not explain the mechanism and is not going to. That is deliberate — a leftover you understand is just a tool, and the interesting thing about these is that they are older than the explanation.

The cowboy and the horseman

There are two people in this world who know more about horses than they have any business knowing.

The horseman keeps a workstation, deals in genes, and can tell you what a horse is carrying by looking at it. The cowboy turns up with stock to sell, from somewhere, and the stock is always good. Neither will say where they learned. Neither is surprised by anything you bring them.

They may be the last of the race that brought the horses here. They may be perfectly ordinary people who inherited a trade and a set of instructions they follow without understanding. The mod does not settle it, and you should not expect it to — but it is worth noticing that they are the only two who treat a hay portal as unremarkable. See the villagers page for what they actually do.

The breeds

Grouped by where they turn up. Heights are in hands; rarity is the wild-spawn weight, not a judgement. The mechanical detail for each — colour genes, stat scores, disorder risks — is in the breeds table.

The open country

Quarter Horse 14.5–16.0 hh · extremely common
The horse you will meet first and most. Bred for a quarter-mile and nothing else, and it shows — a standing start out of a Quarter Horse is closer to being fired than to accelerating. So common now that it is effectively the wild default across the grassland, which is what happens when a breed is both useful and easy.
Thoroughbred 15.5–17.0 hh · very common
Every one of them descends from three stallions, and the old world kept the paperwork proving it for three hundred years. The paperwork is gone. The horses are still fast, still thin-skinned, and still faintly ridiculous about weather.
Standardbred 14.5–16.5 hh · very common
Built to keep one gait, at speed, for an hour, in front of a cart. There are no carts. It does it anyway, on its own, across an empty field, and it is difficult not to read something into that.
Hanoverian 15.8–17.3 hh · very common
A committee horse — centuries of an actual registry deciding which stallions were permitted. Athletic, sane, and slightly institutional about it. Wild bands of them are unusually orderly.
Morgan 14.3–15.5 hh · very common
Descended from one stallion who was, by every account, better at everything than he had any right to be. Compact, willing, and famously difficult to discourage.
Tennessee Walking Horse 14.8–17.0 hh · very common
Bred for a gait that is comfortable to sit for a whole day. The people who needed to sit for a whole day are gone; the gait is not.
American Paint 14.5–16.0 hh · very common
Loud, patterned, and carrying more white-marking genes than is entirely safe — a Paint herd is the one place you are most likely to see the lethal white cross go wrong. Beautiful and a genuine breeding hazard, in that order.
American Miniature 7.0–8.5 hh · very common
A full-sized horse in every proportion and none of the scale, carrying practically every dilution and pattern gene the mod has. Somebody spent a very long time making these, for no reason anyone can now reconstruct.
Missouri Fox Trotter 14.0–16.0 hh · common
Bred by people who had to cross the Ozarks and would rather not be thrown about doing it. The fox trot is a walk in front and a trot behind, which sounds like a fault and is the whole point: the horse goes all day and the rider’s hat stays on. Comes in every colour anybody has ever asked for, because nobody was ever choosing it by looking.
Belgian Draft 16.0–17.0 hh · common
Among the strongest things that has ever walked. It has nothing left to pull and grazes placidly through a field it could have ploughed in an afternoon.
Percheron 15.5–17.8 hh · common
A draught horse that moves like a much smaller one. Greys out with age almost without exception, so an old Percheron band is a field of white.
Gypsy Vanner 14.0–15.5 hh · common
Bred by travelling people to pull a home and be safe around children. Feathered legs, enormous mane, unbothered by anything. Wild bands drift rather than range — the habit outlasted the wagons.
American Saddlebred 15.2–16.7 hh · moderate
Carries its head as though it has been told something important is about to happen. Kentucky bred it out of colonial riding stock to be comfortable over distance and then spent a century making it spectacular instead. Some of them have two extra gaits and some do not, and the ones that do are not obviously prouder about it, though it is hard to be certain. Comes in every colour there is, the registry having declined to have an opinion on the subject.
Friesian 15.0–17.0 hh · uncommon
Black, and only black, and dramatic about it. Nearly died out twice in the old world and was rescued both times by people who could not stand to let it go. It is still here. They would be pleased.
Andalusian 15.0–16.5 hh · uncommon
A war horse and a parade horse at once, which was always the point. Thick arched neck, heavy mane, and an opinion of itself that is not entirely unearned.
Lipizzan 14.5–16.2 hh · uncommon
Born dark and turning white over years. Trained, in the old world, to do things above the ground that no horse needs to do. Occasionally a wild one will do something in a field that looks a great deal like practice.
Hackney 14.5–16.0 hh · uncommon
Enormously high knee action — bred to look impressive in front of a carriage. Wildly impractical, entirely committed.
Knabstrupper 14.5–15.5 hh · uncommon
Spotted, from a single spotted mare, and every one since has been an argument about how to keep the spots. Circus stock in the old world, which is the sort of provenance that does not survive a collapse and did.
Falabella 6.0–8.0 hh · uncommon
The smallest horse there is. Not a pony — the proportions are all horse, shrunk. It is hard to look at a wild Falabella band and believe nobody made them on purpose, because somebody did.
Oldenburg 16.5–17.5 hh · uncommon · never wild
Among the tallest things here you could still reasonably call a riding horse. It began as a carriage and artillery animal and was rebuilt, deliberately and over about a century, into something that could jump. The studbook that did it had one rule, which was that quality was the only rule — no preferred colour, no closed ancestry, nothing but whether the horse was good. So an Oldenburg may be a sober bay or may be a pinto, and both are equally correct, which is a rarer attitude than it sounds.
Pony of the Americas 11.5–14.0 hh · uncommon
Spotted, always, without exception — the only breed in this book where that is true, because the registry would not have you otherwise. It was invented in Iowa in 1954 around one crossbred foal called Black Hand, for children, and it is built like a small stock horse rather than a pony. Painted white over the hindquarters or leopard from nose to tail or merely frosted, but never piebald: the club was emphatic that spots and patches are different things and it was right.
Selle Français 15.2–17.3 hh · uncommon · never wild
France had a dozen regional half-bred horses and in 1958 decided they were one breed, which they then became. Bay or chestnut, mostly, with a good deal of white on the legs inherited from the Norman side, and bred for a single question: can it leave the ground. It can. Of everything in this book it is the one most clearly designed backwards from a fence.
Budyonny 16.0–16.7 hh · rare · never wild
Built to order in the 1920s out of Don mares and Thoroughbred stallions, for an army that wanted cavalry and got tanks instead. Tall, chestnut four times in five, with a gold sheen to the coat that the old accounts go on about at length and that is not modelled here. The only horse in this book you will never find running loose — it only ever existed because an institution kept it existing, and the cowboy and the stables are what is left of the institution.
Cleveland Bay 16.0–16.5 hh · rare
Bay, always, with no white to speak of — the oldest English breed and the most stubbornly uniform. Rare here as it was rare there.
Suffolk Punch 16.0–17.5 hh · rare
Chestnut without exception, because every one descends from a single stallion foaled in 1768 and chestnut is all he had to give. A closed book of a breed, still reading out the same answer.
American Cream Draft 15.0–16.0 hh · rare
Cream-coloured with amber eyes and pink freckled skin, and every one carries it — but the colour is gold champagne on a chestnut, in one copy or two, not the cream dilution the name suggests, which the breed does not carry.
Colorado Ranger 14.5–16.0 hh · rare
Two stallions arrived as a gift from a sultan to a president in 1878, and everything here descends from what they left behind. The registry that grew out of it was adamant on one point: it registered pedigrees, not colours — so a Rangerbred may be a plain bay or may be covered in spots, and both are equally the breed. About half of them carry the spots. Nobody is checking the pedigrees now, which is precisely the thing the registry existed to check.
Gelderlander 15.5–17.0 hh · rare
A big chestnut carriage horse with white socks and a trot that lifts. Bred in Gelderland for two jobs at once, pulling a plough on a weekday and a good carriage on a Sunday, and it never quite decided which it preferred. The modern sport horse was built on top of it and very nearly absorbed it entirely; somebody had to write a separate studbook in 2019 to keep the old shape from being bred out. Six hundred mares left, and every one of them looks like a painting of a horse rather than a photograph of one.
Danubian 15.3–16.2 hh · rare
Assembled at a Bulgarian state stud out of local mares and imported Hungarian stock, for an army, and finished in 1951. It is the plainest horse in this book and was meant to be: dark, broad, steady, and built to pull a cart down a farm road for a working life. Six paternal lines were kept on paper and the paper mattered enormously to somebody. What is left is a sensible horse that will never be mistaken for a racehorse.
Jutland 15.5–16.1 hh · rare · never wild
A wide red horse with a white mane, which is the whole of Denmark’s picture of a horse. It ploughed the place. Then the tractors came and there were fewer than three hundred left, and every one you meet here is the result of somebody deciding that was not acceptable.
Nonius 15.8–16.7 hh · rare · never wild
Descended from a stallion taken off the French in the Napoleonic wars and brought to a Hungarian state stud in 1816, where he turned out to be worth rather more than the war. Dark — black, dark bay, brown, and effectively nothing else, which after two centuries of selection reads less like a colour than like a policy. Big, plain, heavy-headed and enormously willing. It pulled artillery and it pulled ploughs and it would clearly still do either if asked.
Novooleksandrivsky Heavy Draught 14.8–15.8 hh · rare · never wild
A Ukrainian draught horse that is not very large, which is the whole trick of it: all the pulling and none of the feeding. Chestnut, usually, often with a pale mane, and built close to the ground on short heavy legs. Bred from 1868 at a stud in what is now Luhansk and recognised as Ukraine's own only in 1999, which is late for a breed that had been doing the work for a hundred and thirty years.
Orlov Trotter 15.5–16.2 hh · rare · never wild
Grey, more often than not, and the greyness is traceable: a count bought an Arabian stallion in 1774, and rather a lot of this horse is still him. Built to pull a carriage at a fast trot across real Russian roads for real distances, which is a harder job than a racetrack and produced a bigger, tougher animal than the trotters that eventually beat it for speed. The dark coat underneath the grey is still there in the foals, and then it goes.
Kladruber 16.0–17.0 hh · very rare · never wild
Bred for four hundred years to pull an emperor, in two colours: grey for the court and black for the church. Nobody has told them the empire is over. They are enormous, they are aware of it, and they still move as though something ceremonial is about to be required of them.
Anglo-Arabian 15.4–16.7 hh · uncommon
An Arabian and a Thoroughbred, held at the right proportions for long enough that it became its own thing. France wrote the rules down in 1880 and the rules were the breed — not a colour, not a build, a pedigree fraction. Nobody is checking fractions now, so what is left is the horse the rules were aiming at: rangy, grey more often than not, and able to keep going after the Thoroughbreds have stopped.
Australian Stock Horse 14.0–16.5 hh · moderate
Descended from whatever came off the ships and survived the continent afterwards, which is a harsher selection than any registry ever ran. The standard accepts every colour, because arguing about colour was never the point. It was bred to work cattle all day in heat and turn hard when one broke away, and it still does the turning, at nothing, in an empty paddock.

The high and windswept country

Welsh Pony 11.0–14.5 hh · very common
Hardy, quick, and far cleverer than is convenient. The wide height range is real: the breed was four separate types in the old world and they have been reconverging ever since nobody was left to keep them apart.
Haflinger 13.5–15.0 hh · common
Chestnut with a flaxen mane, every time, like a decision. Mountain stock — sure-footed on ground that makes bigger horses think.
Irish Draught 15.5–17.0 hh · uncommon
A farm horse that could also hunt, because the farm could only afford one horse. That double duty is bred right through it, and it is why the breed crosses so well with everything.
Connemara Pony 13.0–14.5 hh · uncommon
From a coast with almost nothing on it, and it shows: small, tough, astonishing over a jump. Wild bands take the worst ground available and seem pleased with the arrangement.
Domestic Mountain Pony 12.8–14.3 hh · uncommon
Bosnia's own, and the local name is the plain one: the domestic mountain horse. Small, dark, hard-footed, and capable of carrying an absurd load up ground you would rather walk. Almost all of them are black or a bay so dark it reads as black, and the occasional one is dun. Of everything in this book it is the least interested in being looked at and the most likely to get you home.
Fell Pony 13.0–14.0 hh · uncommon
The Dales pony's neighbour and near-twin, off the other side of the same hills, and it carried lead out of the same mines. Black now, almost always — though that is recent, and a century ago you would have met as many browns and bays. It shares something less welcome with the Dales too: a recessive that kills foals, carried by better than a third of the breed. Here it does not, because the gene has not been built. That is a kindness the real horse never got.
Highland Pony 13.0–14.5 hh · uncommon
The biggest of the British natives and the one most likely to be dun, in a range of duns the Scots bothered to name individually — mouse, yellow, fox, oatmeal, biscuit. Bred to carry a shot deer down off a hill, which is a specific job requiring a specific temperament, and it has it: patient to the point of looking bored. Do not let it near good grass. It was built for a diet of almost nothing and it has no idea when to stop.
Hequ 12.8–14.8 hh · uncommon
From the great bend of the Yellow River, two miles above sea level, where the air has a third less oxygen in it and the winter is long. It is far tougher than its height suggests and recovers from work at an altitude that would flatten a bigger horse. Three regional kinds exist and the difference you will actually see is grey: the heavy Gansu type greys out far more often than the other two.
Dales Pony 13.5–14.5 hh · rare
Bred to carry lead ore down out of the hills. Black, feathered, and absurdly strong for its size. There is no ore to carry and it still walks the same routes.
Dartmoor Pony 11.5–12.5 hh · rare
Small, level-headed, and old — on its home moor long before anyone was writing breeds down. Of everything in this book it is among the least obviously designed, which may be why it took to being wild so easily.
Exmoor Pony 11.5–12.8 hh · rare
The most ancient-looking thing here: mealy muzzle, hooded eye, a winter coat in two layers. Nearly gone in the old world — a handful of animals at the worst of it — and now running loose in numbers on hills it has never seen.
American Bashkir Curly 14.3–15.2 hh · rare
Named for a place it probably never came from, and known for a coat of tight curls — mane, tail, and the inside of the ears. It comes in every colour there is, because nobody breeding it ever cared about colour; they were selecting for the curl, and the curl was enough. Here it is not curly. The two genes that do it have not been built, so what runs on these hills is the rest of the horse: sound, unbothered by winter, and colour-wise the least predictable breed in the book.
Hucul Pony 13.0–14.6 hh · rare
The Carpathian pony, from a mountain range that has been divided between four countries and several empires without anybody asking the horses. Low, deep, short-legged, and footed well enough that most of them never needed shoes. Its reputation is for primitive dun colouring and the reputation is louder than the genetics: when somebody finally counted, only about a quarter of them carried it. The breed had been telling a slightly flattering story about itself for a long time.
Kaimanawa 13.1–14.9 hh · rare
Feral in the middle of New Zealand’s North Island, on volcanic tussock nobody else wanted, and rounded up every so often because the herd outgrows the country. The grey ones all come from the south, and all of them come from one mare a long way back — which is the sort of thing you only ever learn by counting.
Montenegrin Domestic Hilly Horse 13.0–14.1 hh · rare
The same horse as the Bosnian mountain pony, on the other side of a border that means nothing whatever to either of them. Carries a hundred kilos over bare limestone without comment. Greyer than its cousin, which is the only way to tell them apart and is not a reliable one.
Pindos 11.8–13.8 hh · rare
The horse you take when the road ends. Off the Pindus mountains in northern Greece, where the paths are limestone and the summers are hot and the winters are not, and it carried packs, wood, crops and ammunition through all of it. Small, narrow, a little cow-hocked in a way that turns out to be useful on bad ground, and fitted with hooves that several sources describe as being like flint. Bay, mostly, or grey, and entirely uninterested in being admired.
Karakachan Horse 12.4–13.6 hh · very rare
It belongs to shepherds who walked their flocks between summer and winter pasture, and it is built like the country they did it in: small, bay, and out in it all year. Almost every one of them is the same colour, which in this world is rarer than any spotted coat.
Guoxia 8.5–9.8 hh · rare
The name means “under-fruit-tree horse” and it is not a metaphor — it is short enough to walk beneath the orchard branches while you pick. Second-smallest thing in this book, and unlike most very small horses it is not a miniature of anything: it is a working mountain pony that happens to be the size of a large dog, with a startling amount of mane. Nearly forgotten entirely until somebody found a thousand of them in 1981.
Debao Pony 9.2–10.1 hh · rare
From the same limestone hills as the Guoxia and descended from the same old tradition, but a separate breed and a fraction larger. It hauled fruit up and down tracks too narrow for anything else. Its smallness was arrived at entirely independently of every other tiny horse in the world, which is a strange thing to be able to say about an animal. One was found in 1981 standing 92 centimetres, and the finding of her is why the breed still exists.
Riwoche Horse 11.5–12.5 hh · very rare
Found in 1995 in a Tibetan valley behind a pass three miles up, and immediately announced as a living relic of the prehistoric horse — beige, dark-striped, bristle-maned, exactly like the animals on the cave walls. Then somebody tested the blood. It is an ordinary domestic horse. That is the more interesting answer: what it actually demonstrates is that a small enough population, left alone for long enough, will look several thousand years older than it is. The people who had been riding them all along were not surprised.

The cold country

Shetland Pony 8.0–10.5 hh · very common
Pound for pound the strongest horse in the world and entirely aware of it. Bred on islands with no shelter and no spare food, which produced an animal that is very difficult to kill and very difficult to argue with.
Trakehner 15.5–17.0 hh · common
The old world nearly lost these in a single winter, walking them out of a war across frozen water. A few hundred made it. Everything you see in the pine forest is descended from those.
Clydesdale 16.5–18.0 hh · uncommon
The tallest thing in the book, with white feathered legs and a walk you can hear coming. Draught power for a country of hills.
Norwegian Fjord 13.5–14.5 hh · uncommon
Dun, with a dorsal stripe running up into a mane that grows two-toned and stands upright on its own. One of the oldest breeds anywhere, and it looks like the horses people painted on walls.
Icelandic Horse 13.0–14.0 hh · uncommon
Has two gaits nothing else has, is never called a pony by anyone who keeps one, and comes from a place that banned imports for a thousand years to keep it clean. That isolation is still legible in the genes.
Finnhorse 14.6–15.5 hh · uncommon
Finland's only native horse, and it is chestnut with a pale mane nine times out of ten — not by accident but by decree. When the studbook closed in 1907 the breeders decided which colours were properly Finnish and which were foreign, and bred the foreign ones out. Greys, palominos and piebalds all went. A few survived anyway, in ones and twos, and those are the interesting ones to find: a black Finnhorse is about one in eighty.
Gotland Pony 11.3–12.8 hh · uncommon
The forest pony of a Baltic island, called the little horse of the woods and once, better, the forest ram. Dun, mostly, with the stripe down the back. In 1922 the entire breed came down to seven mares, one young stallion and six youngsters — everything alive today came through that needle. It is a natural trotter and will out-trot things twice its size, apparently for its own entertainment.
Canadian Horse 14.0–16.0 hh · rare
“The little iron horse” — small, dark, and effectively indestructible in cold that stops other breeds. Nearly extinct twice. Doing better here than it ever did at home.
Dølahest 14.6–15.4 hh · rare
Norway's largest native horse, from the Gudbrandsdal valley, and the working answer to the Fjord standing next to it. Where the Fjord is dun and only dun, this one comes in black, brown, chestnut, palomino, buckskin and every shade of grey — it is the same country and entirely the opposite decision about colour. It hauled timber, pulled sleighs, worked fields and was ridden home afterwards, and there is a lighter branch of the family that does nothing but trot.
Newfoundland Pony 11.0–14.5 hh · rare
Shaggy, feathered, hard-hooved and built by three centuries of an island with not much on it. It hauled wood and nets and fish and children, on the grounds that a proper draught horse was an extravagance nobody there could justify. Bay, black, brown, chestnut, dun, grey or roan — never patched, the registry was firm about that. It came within a few hundred animals of ending, and is only still here because a province passed a law about it.
Sanhe 14.8–15.5 hh · rare
From the three-rivers grassland of Hulunbuir, and deliberately made: Mongolian mares crossed with imported Russian and Thoroughbred stock through the twentieth century to get something taller and faster that could still stand a winter reaching minus forty. It worked. Chestnut or bay, almost always, and noticeably bigger than the steppe horses it came from without having given up anything they were good at.
Kiso Horse 12.1–13.9 hh · rare · never wild
Honshu’s own horse, and it very nearly stopped existing. The war took the stallions; by the seventies there were thirty-two animals left, and all of them traced back to one stallion a shrine had quietly declined to hand over. Two thirds of them wear a stripe down the spine. Nearly all of them are bay.

The dry country

Arabian 14.5–15.8 hh · common
The oldest documented breed there is, and the one in the ancestry of nearly everything else in this book. Dished face, high tail, one fewer vertebra than it ought to have. Bred by people who kept their mares inside the tent.
Anadolu 12.2–14.0 hh · common
Not a breed so much as a country's worth of ordinary horses that all ended up the same shape. Small, broad, hard-footed, and bred by nobody in particular for carrying things up hills nobody wanted to walk up. It is the least designed animal in this book after the Exmoor, and the most useful.
Mongolian Horse 12.0–14.0 hh · common
There are more of these than of almost anything else on four legs, and no registry has ever had an opinion about them. It lives outside through winters that stop machinery and comes out the other end looking for grass. Any colour at all, though dun keeps winning.
Mangalarga Marchador 14.5–16.0 hh · common
Brazil’s answer to the question of how to ride for six hours without standing up in the stirrups. The marcha always keeps a foot on the ground, so the horse floats and the rider does nothing at all. Loud colours, grey everywhere, and strong feelings about being left behind.
Criollo 13.7–15.2 hh · moderate
The gaucho's horse, off the Pampas, and the toughest thing in this book by a margin that is not close. Spanish stock turned loose in 1535 that multiplied, went feral, and got sorted by drought and distance for four centuries before anybody thought to write it down. Most of them are dun — a dark stripe down the spine and bars across the legs — which is the oldest colouring a horse comes in and looks it. It will outlast anything you own.
Akhal-Teke 14.5–16.0 hh · uncommon
Thin-skinned to the point that the coat throws back light like metal — the sheen is real and is not currently modelled, which is a known loss. Bred for desert distance on very little water.
Mustang 13.5–15.5 hh · uncommon
Not a designed breed at all — escaped stock that went feral and got sorted by the country instead of by people. Which makes it the closest thing here to what happened to all of them, run in fast-forward.
Lusitano 15.0–16.0 hh · uncommon
Bred to stand its ground in front of a bull. Brave in a way that is difficult to describe and immediately obvious to ride.
Barb 14.3–15.3 hh · uncommon
Older than almost everything it is standing next to, and responsible for a good deal of it. Carried raiders and cavalry across country that does not forgive a horse needing much. Greys out so reliably that a settled Barb band reads as a pale smudge from a distance — which was presumably never the intention and is now the only way to spot one.
Boerperd 14.5–15.8 hh · uncommon
“Farmer's horse”, and it meant it: one animal expected to ride, plough, pull, herd, and get someone home. Nearly bred out of existence in a war it was conscripted into, then put back together on purpose. Most of them have a fifth gait that is comfortable to sit for a whole day; you cannot feel it here, which is a known loss.
Chilean Corralero 13.4–14.6 hh · uncommon
Short, broad, and built entirely around stopping a cow that has decided otherwise. The oldest stud book of any horse in the Americas, kept unbroken since 1893 by people who were very clear about what they wanted and wrote it down. Dark, plain and immovable. Wild ones still plant themselves and turn at nothing in particular.
Karabair 14.8–15.5 hh · uncommon
An oasis horse from Uzbekistan, quick and neat and bred where the desert stops. Its name means “black horse”, and about a fifth of them are grey, which tells you how much attention anyone was paying to the name.
Karabakh 14.0–15.0 hh · rare
Mountain-desert stock with a golden chestnut coat that is nearly a breed marker on its own. Very old, very rare, and quietly one of the ancestors of a great deal else.
Kiger Mustang 13.0–14.5 hh · rare
A pocket of feral horses that turned out to be carrying old Spanish blood almost undiluted — dun, striped, primitive-marked. An accident that preserved something on purpose.
Nokota 14.0–17.0 hh · rare
Blue roan, mostly — a black horse shot through with white hairs until it reads as smoke, with the head and legs staying dark. They were the feral horses of the North Dakota badlands, fenced into a national park more or less by accident and then sold off out of it in the eighties, at which point one family bought as many as they could and started writing things down. Angular, alert, and carrying frame overo, which is beautiful and is also the gene that kills foals when two carriers meet.
Caspian Pony 9.0–11.5 hh · rare
Assumed extinct for a thousand years, then found again in a handful of villages. Tiny, fine-boned, and built like a miniature Arabian because it is very probably where the Arabian started.
Przewalski's Horse 12.0–14.0 hh · rare
The one entry that does not belong in a book about breeds. It was never domesticated, never designed, and has a different number of chromosomes from everything else here. Whoever moved the others brought this too, which raises a question nobody can answer: why take the one horse that was never yours?
Murgese 14.8–16.5 hh · rare · never wild
Black. That is the rule, and the only exception is a blue roan the old books called iron grey, which happens about once in fifty horses and looks like smoke. Kept half-wild on a limestone plateau in Apulia, and unimpressed by weather, ground, or people.
Sorraia 13.8–14.8 hh · very rare
Mouse-coloured, with a black stripe down the spine, bars across the legs, a dark muzzle and a cobweb across the forehead — every single one, because there is no other kind. A Portuguese zoologist found a few dozen living wild in the river lowlands in 1920 and spent the rest of his life keeping them. Everything alive now came through that, which is why there are so few and why they look so alike. People keep wanting it to be the ancestor of the Iberian horses. It may not be. It is remarkable regardless.
Azteca 14.3–16.0 hh · uncommon
The youngest breed in this book by a wide margin — started in 1972, recognised in 1982, built deliberately out of Iberian horses and Quarter Horses to be Mexico's own. It is the one entry whose whole existence is documented, and the documents are gone. Two kinds turn up: the solid dark or grey horse the Mexican standard insisted on, and occasionally a patterned one, which the registry on the other side of the border allowed and the one that made the breed did not.

The wet and wooded country

New Forest Pony 12.0–14.5 hh · common
Semi-wild even in the old world — owned on paper, loose in practice. Of everything here it made the smallest adjustment to the new arrangement.
Appaloosa 14.5–16.0 hh · uncommon
Spotted by a gene that also mottles the skin, whitens the sclera and stripes the hooves, so you can identify one at rest. Bred by people who were very good at it and had the breed taken from them.
Shire 16.5–17.8 hh · uncommon
The other contender for tallest. Bred to move impossible weight at a walk, and gentle out of all proportion to its size.
Camargue 13.5–14.5 hh · uncommon
Born dark, greys out to white, and lives in salt marsh. Foals are almost black; the adults are the colour of the sky over the water they stand in.
Baise Horse 11.0–11.5 hh · uncommon
A southern Chinese village pony from the Baise hills, and nothing at all like the famous horses of the northern steppe — short, broad, dense-boned and entirely unbothered by heat that would stop a bigger animal. Bay, black, chestnut or grey, and that is the whole list. Bronze horses cast two thousand years ago in the same region look remarkably like it, which proves nothing and is hard to ignore.
Kentucky Mountain Saddle Horse 11.0–16.0 hh · uncommon
Appalachian people needed something that could get up a hollow and back without jarring their teeth out, and this is it: a horse that ambles instead of trotting, in every size from small pony to substantial. Any colour you like, so long as it is only the one colour.
Rocky Mountain Horse 14.0–16.5 hh · uncommon
Not from the Rocky Mountains. From a hollow in eastern Kentucky, named after the community rather than the range, and the famous ones are chocolate brown with a mane and tail the colour of cream. It ambles instead of trotting and will do it all day without spilling anything. The chocolate is worth knowing about: the same change that makes it also malforms the eye, mildly in most and badly in the ones that inherit it twice, so the prettiest thing about the breed is the thing careful breeders are trying not to double up on. Here it costs nothing, which is a mercy and a lie.
Konik 12.8–13.8 hh · uncommon
Every one of them is the same grey-brown as a winter field, with a black stripe down the back. Turned loose into marshes across Europe to eat them into shape, which it does happily and for nothing. People keep calling it a surviving wild horse. It is not, and it does not care.
Marwari 14.5–15.5 hh · rare
Ears that curve inward until the tips touch — not modelled yet, and the most missed omission in the book. A war horse from a hot country, and exported almost never.
Paso Fino 13.0–15.5 hh · uncommon
A four-beat gait so even you could carry a full cup at speed. Bred over four centuries by people crossing an ocean and needing to stay comfortable once they arrived.
Puerto Rican Paso Fino 13.0–15.0 hh · rare
The island line of the same idea, kept separate and finer-boned. Two breeds that share a name and an ancestor and have not been the same thing for a very long time.
Banker Horse 13.5–14.5 hh · rare
Feral on barrier islands, drinking from holes it digs in the sand, descended from stock that came off wrecked ships. It has been surviving an abandonment for four hundred years, which makes it the most qualified animal in this book to have an opinion about what happened here.
Chincoteague Pony 12.0–13.5 hh · rare
A marsh pony off a barrier island, small because the grazing was poor and patterned because nothing ever selected against it. Once a year the people there swam the herd across a channel and sold the foals to pay the fire company, which is either the strangest arrangement in this book or the most sensible one. The swim is not happening any more. The ponies are still here, still loud with white, and still looking at you out of one blue eye.
Misaki Horse 12.8–13.5 hh · rare
Lives on a headland at the bottom of Kyushu, and has done since somebody rounded up the local horses in 1697. Nobody rides them. They were made a national monument along with the cape itself, which is a rare instance of protecting a horse and its view in the same document.
Croatian Coldblood 14.8–16.2 hh · rare · never wild
A broad chestnut farm horse out of the Croatian floodplains, bred to plough, to haul timber, and to stand patiently while somebody loaded it. Mechanisation made it nearly pointless and it survived anyway, in the hands of people on small farms in wet country where a machine was the wrong tool. There are no farms now and no timber to haul. It is still the most obliging animal in the book, and you will never find one loose — it only exists where somebody is keeping it.
Posavac 13.8–14.8 hh · rare
The Coldblood's smaller cousin off the same Croatian floodplain, and the one you will actually meet loose, because this is the one they turned out onto the wet meadows to keep them open. Broad-footed for ground that swallows a normal hoof, and content on grazing that would not keep a riding horse alive. It came down to about four hundred and fifty animals in the nineties and there are thousands again now, which is a rarer ending than it sounds.
Florida Cracker Horse 13.5–15.5 hh · rare
Off the Spanish ships in the fifteen-hundreds and running cattle through palmetto scrub ever since, named for the crack of the drovers' whips rather than for anything about the horse. Small, quick, and able to work all day on feed that sounds like a joke. It was nearly bred out of existence in the 1930s when the ranches wanted something bigger. The old line on it is that you cannot tell one by looking — you have to get on it.
Henson Horse 14.8–15.8 hh · rare
The youngest breed in this book by a wide margin: somebody in the Bay of Somme decided in the 1970s that what the tourists needed was a Fjord with longer legs, crossed for it deliberately, and had it recognised as a real breed in 2003. It worked. Bay dun every single time, with the stripe down the spine written into the rules, and calm enough to put a stranger on. A horse designed on purpose, for a purpose, and honest about it.
Medimurje Horse 15.3–16.2 hh · very rare · never wild
The Međimurje, out of the flat wet country between two rivers — which is in Croatia now and has been in several other things. A big quiet draught that was on every farm within living memory, and there are about forty of them left on the Croatian side. The horse did not change. The farms did.

The night country

One breed in this book was never anybody’s farm horse, and it only comes out after dark.

Dhampir 15.0–17.3 hh · rare · night only
Red-eyed, with whites that glow in the dark, and found only by people who were out at night when they should not have been. Nine in ten are a deep seal brown and merely a little stronger and faster than they have any right to be. The tenth is white from nose to tail, three times as hard to kill, burned by the morning sun, and unable to eat anything you can hold out to it — it keeps itself alive by biting, once, and then leaving the animal be until tomorrow. The brown ones carry every piece of the white one quietly, which means two of them in a paddock are a slow, deliberate bet. Whoever made them wanted something that could not be stolen by daylight. Nobody has worked out what for. See magic white, sun sensitivity and blood diet.

Reading the book against the game

None of the above changes a number. A breed's biomes, rarity, height band and gene weights are all in its JSON file, and that file is the truth — if this page and a breed file disagree, the file wins and this page is wrong. What the entries add is the reason a breed is shaped the way it is, which is the part the numbers cannot carry.

The lore is deliberately thin in one specific place: it never explains the mechanism behind the portal or the carrots, and it never confirms what the cowboy and the horseman are. Those gaps are load-bearing. Filling them in would turn a world with something older than you in it into a world with a backstory, and the first is worth more.
