Magical gene / dominant / grows geometry, paints nothing

Tusks

One gene for the growths of the mouth and muzzle, with a form for each allele: a narwhal horn, one long spiral tusk straight out of the front of the muzzle; boar tusks, a pair curling up from the corners of the lower jaw; and sabre fangs, a pair of long blades hanging from the upper lip past the chin. A horse with two different forms grows both. Each form’s length, thickness, curve and shape are written on its own allele. Their colour is a gene of its own, tusk colour.

Crossing two of them

One copy, either sex

Dominant: one copy of a form grows it, and a second copy of the same form does not grow a second set or a bigger one. It is not sex-limited: unlike the antlers, no allele grows only on stallions. Foals grow none; the tusks come in when the horse grows up, and stay.

The three forms

  • Narwhal horn (Nar): one horn on the front of the nose, in the middle, pointing forward along the line of the face. It does not sit where a unicorn’s horn does, so one horse can grow both.
  • Boar tusks (Tsk): a pair, one from each corner of the lower jaw. They leave the jaw sideways and curl up beside the nose.
  • Sabre fangs (Sab): a pair of flat blades from the front of the upper lip, hanging down past the chin.

All three move with the head, so they dip when the horse grazes.

Two forms on one horse

The forms combine. A horse whose two copies are different forms grows both: tusks and fangs (Tsk/Sab), tusks and a horn (Nar/Tsk), or fangs and a horn (Nar/Sab). A horse has two copies, so two forms is the most one horse can carry; there is no horse with all three. Each form is grown from its own copy’s numbers, so a line can be bred for long fangs and short tusks at once. One colour gene tints every form on the horse.

A horse with one copy of boar tusks reads n/Tsk on its gene list, not Tsk/n. It means the same thing; the order is only how the pair is stored.

Finding one

Very rare in the wild, and because one copy is enough, every horse that carries a form shows it. About one wild horse in five hundred has boar tusks, about one in seven hundred has sabre fangs, and about one in a thousand has a narwhal horn. No wild horse has two forms: that takes breeding. The skeleton horses can grow any one of the three in bare bone, as one of their horn-like parts.

What varies

The narwhal horn:

  • Length: from a short tusk just past the end of the face, through long, to a great one longer than the horse’s body is deep. Wild horses are short to long; a line bred for length keeps growing it, up to the ceiling.
  • Twist: a spiral or a tight spiral. A narwhal horn is always straight; it never curves.
  • Thickness.
  • Lift: how far the point is raised above the line of the face.

Boar tusks:

  • Shape: a short curl, a longer sweep, or a hooked tusk whose tip turns back on itself, either over the nose or back toward the eye.
  • Length: small, standard, long or great. A great tusk reaches over the top of the nose.
  • Curl: how far the tusk turns upward after it leaves the jaw.
  • Thickness.

Sabre fangs:

  • Shape: straight, curved back, or serrated, with small barbs down the back edge. The barbs’ pattern is the horse’s own.
  • Length: short, standard, long or great. Even a short fang reaches below the chin.
  • Lean: how far the points lean forward.
  • Thickness.

A pair is always symmetric: both tusks, or both fangs, are the same shape and size. Everything stacks: a horse can wear tusks, a unicorn horn, antlers and every body part at once.

Is it good for the horse?

It does nothing at all, for good or ill: no stat, no ability, no hitbox. It is a look.

Slice A of the tusks treatment

The narwhal horn is the cheapest form, so it came first and proved the gene, the new anchor and the colour locus. There is no new generator. PartKind.NARWHAL is HornGenerator in its two twisted styles (NARWHAL_STYLES: spiral and tight, never the bending smooth style) on its own ladder, NarwhalSize, from 10 to 30 model units, geometric, with the horn’s sixteen size buckets. No new render path either: the client change is the SNOUT anchor’s case in AttachedPartLayer.anchor. One call in GrownParts.of, dressed by its colour locus; the granting locus is in GrownParts.shapes’s every-part baseline.

Slice B: boar tusks and sabre fangs

Shipped 2026-10-08 (#161). Two alleles, two generators and four anchors; no new render path, render-state field or config key, and no change to anything saved.

  • TuskGenerator: a tapering chain of three to five boxes. The root leaves the jaw 66° outward from upright and every later box turns back toward upright, so the tusk curls up beside the nose. Three forms (CURL, short; SWEEP, long; HOOKED, whose last two boxes turn hard), three sweep buckets and two hook directions: TuskGenerator.styles() meshes a side. The sweep is baked in buckets, as the dragon horn’s splay is, because it is a turn about z that differs by side and the layer’s one free rotation is about x.
  • SabreGenerator: a flat blade of two boxes (three when curved), thin across the horse through PartNode.width. It is the third flat box after the membrane and the plate’s slab, and PartGeneratorTest allows it for PartKind.sabre(). Three forms (STRAIGHT, CURVED, SERRATED) and three serration patterns. A pattern shows only on the serrated form, so SabreGenerator.style folds the other forms to pattern 0 and they share a mesh.
  • The fang hangs because its root is turned over. Every mesh grows up its own -y. The fang’s root box carries a half-turn about x, in the mesh and not in the layer. That keeps the blade on the anchor’s y axis, so the layer’s stretch and tilt act on it unchanged and the two new anchor cases are only a translate. After the half-turn the mesh’s -z is the horse’s back: a positive rx curves the blade toward the throat, and the barbs sit on -z.
  • Sizes: TuskSize (2.5 to 9 units) and SabreSize (3 to 10), four classes each, geometric. Within a class the part is scaled uniformly, the dragon horn’s rule, with girth as the nudge across it.
  • Pairs are two kinds, one per side, mirrored box for box with rz negated and never with a negative scale. TusksGeneTest.theLeftOfAPairIsTheRightMirrored holds it.

One outcome per set of forms

expressionOf asks which forms the pair holds, not how many copies: narwhal, tusks, sabre, tusks_sabre, tusks_narwhal, sabre_narwhal, or wild. partsFor returns the horn, then the tusks right and left, then the fangs right and left, and GrownParts.of dresses all of them from the one colour locus. GrownParts.listed names a pair once (“Boar tusks”, “Sabre fangs”).

Each form reads its own copy

Every other part gene reads the expressed copy. This one reads the copy that carries the form, through TusksGene.copyFor: a Nar/Sab horse grows its fangs from the Sab copy’s numbers and its horn from the Nar copy’s, so the two are bred apart though they share a locus. Two copies of one form read the first copy (the antlers’ symmetric rule), not the higher-priority one.

A copy follows its own allele because of Epigenome’s alignment invariant: Copies.first() belongs to pair.first(), and Genome keeps them aligned when breeding. The treatment marked this unverified. TusksGeneTest breeds Nar/n with n/n both ways round and checks that every horned foal’s horn has its Nar copy’s length, never the empty wild copy’s. eachFormReadsTheCopyThatCarriesIt then sets the two copies of every mixed pair to different lengths and checks each form against its own. A one-copy boar is n/Tsk, so its tusks read the second slot while a one-copy narwhal reads the first: a mutant that always read slot 0 turned that test red (2026-10-08), as one that always read the second slot did for slice A.

The numbers are generic

Every copy carries the same five numbers whatever its form, and each form reads the ones it uses. style is a category of six so that each form’s own count divides it: every style equally likely, and two forms on one horse independent. The schema has been these five since slice A, so slice B changed nothing saved.

Why the order is Nar, n, Tsk, Sab

Slice A declared only Nar and n: a Tsk or Sab allele that grew nothing would have been a promise the game did not keep. Alleles are saved by token and a pair is stored lower order first, so slice B appended the two after n. No pair that already existed changed its order, so no saved horse’s copies moved to the other allele. The price is how a pair reads: n/Tsk, not Tsk/n. A breed file may write either; AllelePair puts them in order.

The skeleton breeds roll one of the three

Owner, 2026-10-08. The skeleton breeds’ horn count group holds one slot for this gene, and a founder that draws the slot gets Nar/Nar, Tsk/Tsk or Sab/Sab at equal weight, in bone (tusk_colour is pinned Bon/Bon). The slot’s share of founders did not move; the three forms split it. A group’s locus could only name one pair, so the format grew a choice: Breed.GroupLocus now holds a weighted list of pairs. A one-pair locus draws no extra randomness, so every other grouped locus rolls exactly as it did. UndeadBreedsTest.theTusksSlotRollsOneOfThreeForms holds the thirds and the share, and went red with the draw fixed to the first pair.

Why priority 334

The part loci fill 227–246, and 247 is the only slot left beside them. The next free run of two was 334/335. The MAGIC_PARTS override files the pair with the parts whatever band the number falls in, and nothing requires slots to be unique.

Invented

No horse grows tusks. The narwhal’s tusk is a real thing: a single spiralled canine tooth that grows forward through the upper lip, almost always on the left and almost always in males. This horn takes the look and not the biology. It is centred on the muzzle, not offset to one side (owner, tusks treatment), and mares grow it as often as stallions. It is fantasy and makes no claim about any real gene. The forms combine (a horse with two different forms grows both) because the parts already stack, so there is nothing to suppress.

What needs verifying here

All three forms have been photographed standing still (the narwhal horn 2026-10-05, the boar tusks and sabre fangs 2026-10-08); where the tusks and fangs sit and how big they are is the owner’s to judge, and grazing, the size gene, a foal, and head gear are still to be seen for every form.

Boar tusks and sabre fangs: what a photo shoot showed, and the look

2026-10-08, -PphotoShoot, twelve still frames, most on the new Framing.MUZZLE: each tusk form, each fang form, a Tsk/Sab and a Nar/Sab horse, and two great valley skeletons in bone. Every part drew, on both sides, with no gap at its root. The fangs hang from the front of the muzzle well past the chin; the curved pair bends back and the serrated pair shows its barbs. A red-to-blue tusk is red at the root. On the Nar/Sab horse the horn and the fangs do not touch.

For the owner to judge, since every number is a first proposal read from the muzzle box. The boar tusks root well back on the side of the muzzle and rise toward the eye, so they read as on the cheek more than at the mouth; moving ADULT_JAW forward (its z) or leaving the jaw more sideways (TuskGenerator.ROOT_OUT) would change that. The short curl is a small lump at wild sizes. The hook is hard to read from the side. The fangs’ length, and whether two blades this deep are too heavy for the face. None of this shows a great size, a foal, gear or the head moving.

What a photo shoot showed, and the look

2026-10-05, -PphotoShoot, four still frames of wild-length founders: ivory, Red/Blu, black beside a white unicorn horn, and a bone horn on a great valley skeleton. The horn roots in the middle of the front of the muzzle with no gap, runs straight along the line of the face and tapers to a point. The two-tone horn is red at the root fading to blue at the tip. Beside a unicorn horn the two never touch.

Two things for the owner to judge. Because the neck holds the head nose-down, a horn along the face points well below level; the lift number raises it by up to 0.45 rad, and a wider lift (or a fixed one) would change that. And the ceiling, a great horn of 30 units, has not been seen: the treatment asks for it to read as a long tusk without the horse looking like a unicorn on its face.

Grazing, the head, and the size gene

A horse with a great narwhal horn while it walks, tosses its head and grazes. The horn rides the head bone, so it should dip with the head; when the head is down to graze, say whether the horn driving into the ground is acceptable. The same for long sabre fangs, which hang below the chin and reach the ground first. Then a tiny and a huge horse (the size gene): every form should scale with the head, through the pose scale already on the stack, as the page records from reading the code.

A foal, and head gear

A foal with any form should show nothing, and grow it when it grows up. A bridle (and head armour, if any is worn) on a horse with each form: the part should not hide inside the gear or float clear of the face. The boar tusks root on the side of the muzzle, where a bridle’s noseband runs.
