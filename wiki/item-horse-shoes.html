Horse gear / hoof equipment

Horse shoes

Horse shoes are the first true hoof item in the accessories system: one item per hoof, each with its own material, enchantment and durability, yet all of them living under the same attachment-backed gear model the rest of the horse gear uses. They are not a special case on the horse; they are four independent stacks sharing one shared rule set. Any metal can be used, and the tier of that metal is what decides how much the shoe increases the horse's movement stats.

What they are

A horse shoe is a real piece of gear worn on a hoof rather than a cosmetic shape on the body. This means each hoof can hold its own piece and each piece can hold its own enchantment state, material profile and damage. The point is not that the horse wears four shoes as a set; the point is that a horse can wear one healthy front shoe and one worn hind shoe without the system treating them as one single state.

  • One shoe per hoof. Front left, front right, hind left, hind right.
  • Each shoe is independent. One can break, be enchanted or be removed without changing the others.
  • They are real equipment. They are in the gear attachment, not in a separate horse-only container, and they are not a flag on the horse.
  • All shoes walk on powered snow. Even a plain shoe grants the horse the ability to move across powered snow without sinking or losing traction.

Metal tiers and stat bonuses

Horse shoes are made from a metal material, and the metal's tier determines the baseline stat boost. Higher-tier metals add more flat bonus to speed and jump than lower-tier ones, while the actual value is derived from the material configuration rather than from a hardcoded switch on a single item. The point is that a common metal makes a modest improvement, while a rare or superior metal gives a larger, recognizably better hoof profile.

The stat bonus is intentionally a flat bonus, not a percentage multiplier. That keeps the system readable and easy to balance: a tier 1 metal gives a small bump to speed and jump, a tier 2 or 3 metal gives a larger bump, and the horse's existing movement stats remain easy to compare from one shoe to the next. Because the bonus is flat, the design is also easier to tune around breeding, load and terrain.

  • Tiered material bonus. Material tier determines the flat increase to jump and speed.
  • Any metal qualifies. The system should accept any material with a valid metal tier, not only a fixed shortlist of named metals.
  • Enchantments add special abilities. Some enchantments grant effects such as traction, impact reduction, or terrain-specific movement, while the core tier bonus remains the steady rule.

Why they are different from boots

Boots are a leg item and therefore sit in a broader leg slot model. Shoes are a hoof item and they need a different render target, a different effect scope, and a different durability profile. The important difference is that a boot is about the leg as a whole and a shoe is about the ground interaction at the hoof. The horse-side math therefore belongs on the hoof slot rather than the leg slot, even though they share the same equipment system.

Shared architecture

The shoe system should sit directly on top of the gear model already built in Horse gear: the nineteen slots. The horse stores four worn stacks in the same attachment-backed pattern; the slot roster names the four hoof slots; the item stack holds the material and enchantments; and a horse-side effect manager aggregates the four shoe states into a single effective result. This keeps the logic parallel to the rest of the mod instead of inventing a separate inventory toy that is only valid for shoes.

Slot layer

The slot layer is the first part of the implementation. Add the four shoe slot keys to HorseTackSlot as their own entries, each with its own item tag and its own hoof anchor on the paper doll. That is the part that decides the horse can accept a real shoe item and refuses all other gear; it is not the effect system itself.

Item layer

Each shoe should be a genuine item stack carrying a material identity and enchantments. The horse attachment stores the stack only for the one hoof slot, and any effect calculations read the item from that stack rather than from a hidden shoe state elsewhere. This gives the system the correct shape for item-level enchantments and keeps the worn item as the source of truth.

Effect layer

The effect layer should aggregate the four shoe stacks into a single readout for the horse. It should ask: which shoes are present, what material are they, which enchantments are on them, and what is the total effect at this moment? The calculation must be additive but bounded: one shoe should not silently turn off the other three, and one enchantment should not overwrite the effect profile of another simply because it was the last shoe to be read.

Render layer

A shoe should render from the underside of the hoof, with four independent render roots. It should be slightly larger than the horse model's hoof so the rim is visible, and it should extend down into the ground by a small, deliberate amount. That intersection is part of the silhouette: the shoe should read as weight-bearing hardware seated under the foot, not as a floating ring or a plate pasted over the hoof. This is not a body-layer feature. The first pass should prove one front shoe and one hind shoe before adding the full set, because the hoof scale, ground intersection and body overlap are the parts most likely to look wrong in a way that is easy to miss in code review.

Enchanting rules

This is the design call I would make explicit before the first shoe item ships: each shoe is enchantable as an item, and the enchantment belongs to that item rather than to the horse as a whole. One front left shoe can be enchanted for grip while the right front shoe remains plain, and the horse-side logic sums what the actual worn stacks say. That means the shoe effect model is read from the item stacks themselves, while the material tier continues to provide the base motion bonus. Some enchantments are purely special abilities layered on top of the basic speed/jump bonus, while others may modify the effect profile of the shoe around a specific terrain or use case.

  • Enchantments are per shoe. A horse has four separate shoe stacks, and each stack can be different.
  • Effect legality must be slot-aware. Not every enchantment should be legal on every hoof, and not every enchantment should be legal on every material.
  • Enchantments survive reloads. A shoe is data-bound to the horse attachment, not to a one-shot state flag, so the item stack remains the source of truth after a save and reload.
  • Powered-snow behavior is universal. The powered-snow walk trait is a base feature of all shoes, regardless of metal or enchantment, so even a plain shoe is useful and not only a stat stick.

First effects to implement

I would start with a very small, mechanically clear set and not build a giant pencil-box of abilities in the same pass. The first systems worth shipping are:

  • Base movement tier bonus. Each shoe grants a flat jump and speed bonus based on the metal tier, with the material profile defining the exact value.
  • Grip on slick ground. The first real utility effect for a shoe: it gives a safe traction path on wet, icy or muddy ground without making every ride a friction puzzle.
  • Reduced slipping. A modest horse-side effect that lowers slide and stabilizes footing on the same terrain families the mod already reasons about.
  • Durability cost. Shoes are consumable gear, not free decorations. The effect should be felt in the wear profile the same way armour or equipment wear is felt elsewhere in the mod.

This leaves space for a later expansion to stronger effect roots such as hot-ground protection, quiet movement, or a more explicit jumping / movement bonus if the project ever wants that level of mechanical complexity. In practice, the material tier already provides the base jump and speed boost, and enchantments layer on the special-case abilities without replacing the universal metal bonus.

Bench and crafting path

Shoe crafting should ride the same material-generation pipeline as the other tack items rather than be a separate one-off recipe list. The bench determines the material shape and style, the material scan decides what is legal, and the shoe item stack carries the result. That keeps shoe creation inside the same system as the rest of the gear architecture instead of making it a disconnected feature that only the horseshoe page understands.

Suggested implementation, not built

This is my complete proposal for adding horse shoes to the existing gear system. The central rule is to keep four real ItemStacks in the existing horse-gear attachment, then derive all bonuses and abilities from those stacks. Nothing shoe-specific should become a second inventory, a horse-level boolean, or a special replacement for the shared tack plumbing.

The implementation contract

A shoe is an item made from a metal material. A horse can wear one in each of four hoof slots: shoe_front_left, shoe_front_right, shoe_hind_left and shoe_hind_right. Every occupied slot contains an independent stack. The stack is the source of truth for material tier, durability, enchantments and appearance; the horse attachment is the source of truth for which stack is currently worn.

Compatibility requirement: shoes must leverage the existing generated-horse-armour engine, not introduce a shoe-only list of materials. The same ModdedMaterials and MaterialScan path that discovers and generates armour for metals supplied by other mods must discover those metals for shoes too. When a modpack adds a new metal, including a metal the base mod has never named, the shoe item family, material profile, tier lookup, bench output and render tint should become available through that existing generated-material path.

The effective result is calculated from the four worn stacks whenever horse movement or jump values are queried. Each shoe contributes its material's flat speed and jump bonus. Each shoe also grants powered-snow walking while present. Enchantments contribute additional abilities from the same stack, so one enchanted shoe never changes the enchantment state of its three partners.

Build order

  1. Extend the slot registry. Add four constants to HorseTackSlot, each with a hoof zone, paper-doll anchor and dedicated item tag. Keep the tags separate even if the first item is identical in all four slots; separate keys make the data model, UI, validation and future left/right rules explicit.
  2. Reuse the generated armour material engine. Add the shoe item family to the existing ModdedMaterials.armourMetals() / MaterialScan flow that discovers metal materials for generated horse armour. A modpack-added metal must automatically receive a shoe variant without a new Java class, manually registered item, or hand-written recipe. The shared profile should expose at least metal tier, durability, render material and the flat speed/jump contribution. Preserve the existing material-id collapse and load-order rules so two mods contributing the same metal do not create duplicate shoe registrations.
  3. Use the existing attachment without mirroring it. Store each worn stack through HorseGear.with(slotName, stack) and ModAttachments.HORSE_GEAR. Reuse HorseTackSlot.set and the existing TackSlotPayload server path for equip and remove operations. The server must validate ownership, distance, adult status, slot acceptance and the held stack before changing the attachment.
  4. Make the item tags the acceptance gate. A shoe is accepted only by its matching hoof slot. The tags must reject ordinary metal ingots, boots and shoes intended for another slot. If the design later decides that one shoe item can move between hooves, keep that choice in the validation helper rather than weakening the attachment or packet rules.
  5. Define the material-tier formula once. Add a common material profile or resolver that maps tier to flat speed and jump values. The horse stat integration should sum the four worn contributions, clamp the result according to the project's normal stat rules, and remove the contribution immediately when a shoe is removed or broken. Do not bake the bonus into the horse's genetics or permanently mutate its base stats.
  6. Hook powered snow into the horse movement check. Add one horse-side predicate such as HorseGearEffects.canWalkOnPoweredSnow(horse) that returns true when at least one required shoe condition is met according to the final gameplay rule. For the current proposal, any worn shoe grants the trait. Keep this separate from the stat resolver so powered snow remains unconditional across metal tiers and enchantments.
  7. Read enchantments from each worn stack. Do not copy enchantments into HorseGear or a horse capability. Register shoe enchantments as item enchantments, restrict them with item/slot predicates, and let an effect resolver inspect each stack in turn. Combine compatible effects deterministically; reject or define conflicts explicitly rather than allowing the last shoe processed to win.
  8. Render each stack from its hoof anchor. Add four entries to the shared gear render registry and use the horse model's hoof bones or equivalent transforms. Place the shoe under the hoof, scale it slightly larger than the model, and lower it just enough to clip into the ground naturally instead of floating above it. The renderer receives the slot and stack, so metal appearance, damage state and decoration are read from the same item that gameplay reads. First prove one front and one hind hoof, then enable all four.
  9. Share the item's material and enchantment appearance. The shoe model should use the generated metal's tint and surface treatment, matching the material colour and metallic shine used by the corresponding item. If the worn stack is enchanted, apply the same enchantment glint or equivalent animated shine that the item renderer uses; do not replace it with a generic blue shoe texture. The render layer should therefore consume a shared item appearance/material resolver so an item's inventory icon, dropped form and worn form cannot disagree.
  10. Route crafting through the generated-material bench. Generate a shoe from a discovered metal material and the shoe shape through the existing bench/material pipeline. The output stack must preserve the material profile and be enchantable before it is equipped. Avoid a recipe file for every metal and avoid treating an ingot alone as a valid worn shoe; the same compatibility path that makes generated armour work in a modpack must make generated shoes work.
  11. Add UI and feedback last. The existing Gear tab should discover the four slots from HorseTackSlot. Show the shoe's material tier, flat speed/jump contribution, powered-snow trait and enchantments through normal item tooltips or the gear screen; do not put a second editable shoe inventory in the screen.

Suggested code shape

public record HorseShoeProfile(int metalTier, double speedBonus,
        double jumpBonus, boolean poweredSnow) {
    public static HorseShoeProfile from(ItemStack stack) {
        return HorseMaterials.shoeProfile(stack);
    }
}

public final class HorseGearEffects {
    public static HorseShoeTotals shoes(AbstractHorse horse) {
        HorseShoeTotals total = HorseShoeTotals.ZERO;
        for (HorseTackSlot slot : HorseTackSlot.HOOF_SLOTS) {
            ItemStack stack = slot.on(horse);
            if (!stack.isEmpty()) {
                total = total.add(HorseShoeEffects.from(stack));
            }
        }
        return total;
    }
}

The names above are deliberately illustrative rather than a demand for those exact classes. The important boundary is that one resolver reads the existing attachment and returns derived effects; it must not mutate the horse record or duplicate item state. The concrete stat hooks should follow the existing horse-body/stat architecture once the owning attribute path is confirmed.

Integration code for the worn shoes

The existing renderer already gives this feature the correct extension points: register a custom RenderLayer in GeneticHorseRenderer, copy the attachment-backed stacks during extractRenderState, and submit each stack through the same ItemModelResolver / ItemStackRenderState path used by CutieMarkLayer. Because the resolver receives the actual enchanted stack, its normal item tint and enchantment glint remain available to the worn shoe. 

// GeneticHorseRenderState.java
public final ItemStack[] shoeStacks = {
        ItemStack.EMPTY, ItemStack.EMPTY, ItemStack.EMPTY, ItemStack.EMPTY
};

// GeneticHorseRenderer.extractRenderState(...), inside the GeneticHorseRenderState branch
HorseTackSlot[] shoeSlots = {
        HorseTackSlot.SHOE_FRONT_LEFT,
        HorseTackSlot.SHOE_FRONT_RIGHT,
        HorseTackSlot.SHOE_HIND_LEFT,
        HorseTackSlot.SHOE_HIND_RIGHT
};
for (int i = 0; i < shoeSlots.length; i++) {
    geneticState.shoeStacks[i] = shoeSlots[i].on(horse).copy();
}
// GeneticHorseRenderer constructor
this.addLayer(new HorseShoeLayer(this));

// HorseShoeLayer.java - the model part names and local offsets are the only
// values that need tuning against HdHorseModel/HdBabyHorseModel.
public final class HorseShoeLayer extends RenderLayer<HorseRenderState, HorseModel> {
    private static final float SHOE_SCALE = 1.08f;
    private static final float GROUND_CLIP = 0.025f;
    private static final float SHOE_LIFT = -0.015f;

    private final ItemStackRenderState[] itemStates = {
            new ItemStackRenderState(), new ItemStackRenderState(),
            new ItemStackRenderState(), new ItemStackRenderState()
    };

    public HorseShoeLayer(RenderLayerParent<HorseRenderState, HorseModel> renderer) {
        super(renderer);
    }

    @Override
    public void submit(PoseStack poseStack, SubmitNodeCollector collector, int light,
                       HorseRenderState state, float yRot, float xRot) {
        if (state.isInvisible || !(state instanceof GeneticHorseRenderState genetic)) {
            return;
        }

        HorseTackSlot[] slots = {
                HorseTackSlot.SHOE_FRONT_LEFT, HorseTackSlot.SHOE_FRONT_RIGHT,
                HorseTackSlot.SHOE_HIND_LEFT, HorseTackSlot.SHOE_HIND_RIGHT
        };
        ItemModelResolver resolver = Minecraft.getInstance().getItemModelResolver();
        Level level = Minecraft.getInstance().level;

        for (int i = 0; i < slots.length; i++) {
            ItemStack stack = genetic.shoeStacks[i];
            if (stack.isEmpty()) {
                continue;
            }

            resolver.updateForTopItem(itemStates[i], stack, ItemDisplayContext.FIXED,
                    level, null, i);
            ModelPart hoof = HorseShoePlacement.hoof(getParentModel(), slots[i]);

            poseStack.pushPose();
            hoof.translateAndRotate(poseStack);
            poseStack.translate(0.0f, SHOE_LIFT - GROUND_CLIP, 0.0f);
            poseStack.scale(SHOE_SCALE, SHOE_SCALE, SHOE_SCALE);
            poseStack.mulPose(Axis.XP.rotationDegrees(180.0f));
            itemStates[i].submit(poseStack, collector, light,
                    OverlayTexture.NO_OVERLAY, state.outlineColor);
            poseStack.popPose();
        }
    }
}

HorseShoePlacement.hoof(...) should be the only model-specific helper. It should map the four slot constants to the corresponding front/hind and left/right hoof parts, apply the underside orientation, and keep adult and baby model offsets together. The negative GROUND_CLIP is intentional: it lowers the shoe slightly into the floor, while SHOE_SCALE makes the rim visibly larger than the hoof. These values should be tuned from screenshots or an in-game test horse rather than guessed independently in four render branches.

No separate enchanted texture should be baked for this. The stack passed to ItemModelResolver already contains the metal identity and enchantments, so the normal generated-metal tint supplies the base colour and the normal item glint supplies the enchanted shine. If the shoe model needs a custom material pass for metal reflectivity, that pass should consume the same ModdedMaterials.Metal.colour() / item tint resolver used by generated armour; it must not maintain a second metal-colour table.

Required checks before calling it complete

  • Equip four different metal tiers and verify each stack contributes its own flat speed and jump bonus.
  • Remove or break one shoe and verify the other three bonuses and enchantments remain unchanged.
  • Equip one plain shoe and verify powered-snow walking works without an enchantment.
  • Put different enchantments on all four shoes and verify effects remain attached to their own stacks after save/reload.
  • Reject wrong-slot items, ordinary ingots, foals, distant horses and unauthorized horses on both client and server paths.
  • Verify the Gear tab, item drops, ownership transfer and full-inventory removal do not lose or duplicate a shoe.
  • Render front and hind shoes at adult, juvenile and varied horse-scale poses, confirming underside placement, slight oversizing, intentional ground clipping, metal tint, metallic shine and enchanted glint without z-fighting or left/right transposition.

Design reasoning

Shoes are a good fit for the mod because they are a piece of utility gear that is real and visible but still anchored to the horse rather than to a chest or a ride mode. They also create a clean dividing line between purely cosmetic tack and useful wearable items: a shoe is not decoration, it changes how the horse interacts with the world. That matches the mod's utility-over-decoration rule and keeps the shoe system from collapsing into a gimmick whose only value is a texture change.

The real concurrency risk is not the art, it is the state model. The design should keep each shoe as a separate stack with a separate enchantment profile and a separate render root, because the horse body is too rich to treat footwear as a single global flag. That is the core rule to keep when this page is expanded later.

What is still planned here

Horse shoes are planned as four independent metal item stacks: each shoe contributes a flat speed and jump bonus from its metal tier, every shoe grants powered-snow walking, and enchantments add special abilities without becoming shared horse state.

Implementation plan

  1. Model four distinct hoof slots. Add front-left, front-right, hind-left and hind-right entries to HorseTackSlot, each with its own item tag and hoof anchor. The slots must remain separate so an individual shoe can be equipped, removed, damaged or enchanted without changing the other three.
  2. Accept any tagged metal. The shoe item should be generated or configured from any material with a valid metal tier, not from a hardcoded list of named metals. The material profile should supply durability, appearance and tier rather than creating a separate Java item class for every metal.
  3. Apply the tier as a flat stat bonus. Each worn shoe contributes a flat bonus to horse speed and jump based on its metal tier. The values should be defined by the material configuration and aggregated from the four actual worn stacks, not stored as one horse-level boolean or percentage multiplier.
  4. Make every shoe a genuine item stack. The stack carries the metal identity, durability and enchantments. HorseGear stores the worn stack for that hoof and remains the attachment-backed authority for equipment state.
  5. Grant powered-snow walking unconditionally. The ability to walk on powered snow is a base shoe trait, so it must apply to every worn shoe regardless of metal tier, enchantment or durability. It should not be implemented as a special enchantment that a plain shoe can lack.
  6. Enchant each shoe independently. Enchantments belong to the individual item stack. A horse may have one enchanted shoe and three plain shoes, or four different enchanted shoes, and the effect manager must read and combine those four stacks without overwriting one with another. Enchantment legality should be item- and slot-aware.
  7. Layer special abilities over the base rules. Enchantments can add traction, reduced slipping, impact protection, quiet movement or other special abilities, but they do not replace the metal-tier speed/jump bonus or the universal powered-snow trait.
  8. Use the shared render and bench systems. Render each shoe from the correct hoof bone, route crafting through the equestrian bench and material scan, and reuse the shared gear attachment, validation, networking and decoration paths. The shoe system should not become a separate inventory or recipe architecture.
  9. Verify independent behavior. Test mixed metal tiers, four different enchantment profiles, powered-snow walking with plain shoes, one shoe breaking without the others, save and reload, ownership checks, and full-inventory removal without item loss.
