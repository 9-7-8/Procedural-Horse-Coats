Block / the yard

Jumps

A showjumping fence in three styles, and a real obstacle: like a fence, it cannot be stepped over. Place them side by side to widen it and on top of each other to raise it — there is no height setting, because the stack is the height. One of each style per wood, and the first thing in the mod that makes a horse’s jump stat something you can watch rather than read.

What it is for

A jump is one rail across the block with an upright standard at each end of the run. Put two beside each other and they become one continuous rail with a post at either extremity, the way a real fence line looks; the standards in the middle disappear on their own, so you never have to think about it.

Recipe (shapeless): 3 vanilla fences of that wood + 1 horse hair → 4 jumps.

Four to a hair on purpose. A course is the one thing you build dozens of, and a hair is sheared one to three at a time, once per horse per day — so a twelve-fence course costs three hairs, which is an afternoon with one horse rather than a reason not to build.

Three styles, and changing one costs something

Every jump is one of three shapes. None of them is more than one block deep — real courses put ground poles in front of an oxer, and we deliberately do not, because a jump that occupies two blocks stops being a thing you put in a row and starts being a structure.

StyleWhat it is
VerticalOne rail. The plain fence, and what a jump starts as.
OxerTwo rails with a spread, front and back, with wider standards to hold them.
CrossrailsTwo poles crossed in an X, lowest in the middle — the shape you school a young horse over.

A stick restyles it. Right-click a placed jump holding a stick and it becomes the next style, spending the stick.

A plank repaints it in that plank’s wood, spending the plank. That is also how you say which wood — there is no menu and no cycle order to learn, you hold the wood you want. Holding the wood it already is does nothing and costs nothing.

Neither is consumed in creative. Both are deliberately one item: a course is a thing you build rather than fiddle with, and the cost is meant to be a brake, not a tax.

Style changes nothing about the jumping. All three collide identically, so a stack of three is the same fence whichever shape you build it from. That is on purpose — see why style is cosmetic.

Height is the stack, and it is a measuring stick

A jump is a block and a half to clear, not a block. It is drawn one block tall but stops you like a fence does, which is the same bargain vanilla makes with every fence and wall: half a block of it is invisible. Without that a horse just steps over the rail, because a horse steps a full block without breaking stride.

Every block you stack on top asks for one more block of clearance, which makes a stack a ladder you can read off:

StackTo clearWhat it is
11½ blocksA low fence. Most horses manage, a bad jumper does not
22½A proper vertical — wants a horse bred for it
33½Beyond anything the natural loci reach on their own
4+4½Puissance. A horse carrying the magical jump locus

You cannot jump one on foot, any more than you can jump a fence, so a course is something you walk around and ride through. That is deliberate and it is the vanilla behaviour.

The point is not the number. It is that two horses that look identical on a stat screen are separated by a fence one of them stops at, which is the whole argument for the mod being about breeding rather than about rolling.

Is it dangerous?

No. A jump is a fence: a horse that refuses it stops, and one that hits it takes nothing. It has no effect on a horse’s health, its condition or its bond, and failing one costs nothing but the attempt.

Building a course

  • Facing is set when you place it, from the way you are looking, and the rail runs across that. A row placed while walking sideways comes out straight.
  • A jump placed on top of a jump takes the facing of the one below, so a stack lines up however you walk around it while building. Only facing is inherited — the style still comes from the item in your hand, so you can stack an oxer on a vertical.
  • A single jump is already a fence. There is no trot-pole height — the lowest thing you can build is one a horse has to leave the ground for.
  • A jump facing north and one facing south are the same fence, so they join up. You do not have to approach a row from a consistent side to build it.
  • Jumps join to jumps and to nothing else — they will not connect to a fence, a double gate or a wall.
  • An axe is the right tool, and a jump drops its own style — break an oxer, get an oxer back.

Jumps live in their own creative tab, “Horse Jumps”, rather than in Building Blocks where the double gates are. A gate is a building block horses happen to care about; a jump is horse equipment, useless without one. Every style of every wood is in there, grouped by wood, so the three oaks sit together rather than the twelve verticals.

Every wood has one, including woods other mods added — those are generated at run time from that mod’s own plank texture, so they arrive looking right without anybody drawing anything. See compatibility.

What it is not, yet

Three styles is not a course designer’s vocabulary. A wall is the next one and is a different problem: “wall” means cobblestone, brick, stone brick — a material axis that is not the twelve woods, so it waits on jumps carrying their material as data rather than as one block per wood. Beyond that, cross-country wants logs, brush, ditches, fillers and water, and none of it is built.

The standards and the rails cannot yet be different woods, which is the other thing waiting on that same change: two woods on one jump cannot be a block each. Nothing scores a round, times one, or notices that a horse knocked a rail, because a jump is currently scenery with a collision box. The roadmap is where that lives.

Where it lives

  • block/JumpBlock — the geometry, the connection rule, the VoxelShape.
  • block/Jumps — the per-wood registration, vanilla’s twelve then every modded wood. Deliberately the same shape as block/DoubleGates, down to the duplicate-id guard declared above the static block; that ordering is not stylistic, and the gates crashed mod construction with an NPE out of a class initialiser for getting it wrong.
  • tools/bake-jumps.mjs — every shipped asset, for vanilla’s woods, at author time.
  • compat/GeneratedJumps — the same files for other mods’ woods, at run time.

The collision box is taller than the model

JumpBlock.getCollisionShape returns the same boxes as getShape but topped at 24/16 rather than 15 and 16 — vanilla’s own fence number. The outline and the model stay tight to the rail, so the block still looks right; only what you bump into is taller.

Verified 2026-09-20, in play, and this is why it exists. The first build let collision follow the model, which put the rail under a horse’s 1.0 step height. Owner’s verdict: the jumps “look great, expand great”, but a horse walked straight over them and it “doesn’t feel immersive”. The fix is the fence trick and nothing else — no model change, no new state.

Two consequences worth knowing before changing it. The render and collision shapes are built by one function with two different tops, and the rail and the standards do not share a top in the drawn shape (15 and 16) — collapsing those into one number quietly lops a pixel off every post. And the tall box applies to players as much as horses, so a course cannot be crossed on foot. That is intended, and it is what a fence does.

The crossrails are not UV-locked

The X is drawn with model element rotation, which vanilla caps at ±45° about one axis — exactly enough for a crossrail and no more. A 16-long bar turned 45° projects to about 11 across, so it stays inside its own block. That is why crossrails do not run together the way the other two styles do: each is its own X. Intended, not a limitation waiting to be fixed.

UNVERIFIED, and reasoning rather than observation: the crossrails blockstate sets uvlock: false where the other styles set true. UV-lock re-projects a face’s texture against the block axes after the blockstate’s y rotation, and on an element that is itself rotated 45° the two rotations fight and the grain shears. The axis-aligned styles lock cleanly. Worth looking at a crossrail facing each of the four ways before trusting it.

The geometry is written three times

This is the thing to be careful about. The same shape exists as boxes in bake-jumps.mjs, as a VoxelShape in JumpBlock, and again in GeneratedJumps for modded woods. Change one, change all three.

Each drift fails differently and none of them logs. Model against shape gives you a jump you can see in one place and collide with in another. Baker against generator gives you a modded jump that is a purple cube while every vanilla one is fine.

Left, right, and the suffix that was wrong

LEFT and RIGHT are fence-style connection flags: true means another jump continues the rail on that side, so the standard there is not drawn. Left is facing.getCounterClockWise(), the same definition DoubleGates.partnerDirection uses — keep the two families agreeing about which way is left.

Verified 2026-09-20, by check-block-models.mjs, on its first run. The blockstate composed its model suffix as (left?"_l":"") + (right?"_r":""), giving _l_r, while the models were written _lr. Only the both-connected case differs, so a lone jump and a row of two were perfect and the middle of every row of three was a purple cube.

Both generators now derive the suffix from one function rather than composing it, and the check is a repo tool: node neoforge-26.1.2/tools/check-block-models.mjs, which walks every blockstate, item definition and model parent and asserts the file is there. It covers the shipped assets only — the run-time generated pack is not on disk and is still guarded by nothing but the class docs.

The axe tag is shared, so it merges

mineable/axe holds the double gates as well, and a datapack has exactly one file per tag. Both bakers therefore merge into it rather than writing {values: ours}, and GeneratedGates.mergeBlockTag does the same for the run-time pack so it does not matter which generator runs first.

Getting this wrong is worth describing because of how quietly it fails: the losing family keeps working, mines slowly, and drops nothing without an axe. That reads as a balance decision, not a bug, and could sit there for months. After running either baker, check both families are still in the tag.

Unverified

The look and the connection behaviour were confirmed in play on 2026-09-20 — they “look great, expand great”, and the row-of-three case that was broken drew correctly. The height ladder has not been ridden. It follows from a 1.5-block collision box and a horse’s 1.0 step height, and which rung a given horse stops at is exactly the thing still to be measured — along with whether it agrees with HorseUnits.jumpMetres. See verification.

Why stacking, and not a height property

A height state was the obvious design: right-click to cycle 1–4, like a repeater. It was rejected because it makes the jump assert a height, and the block has no business knowing one. Stacking makes height a consequence of geometry instead — the horse is answering a question about the world, not about a number somebody typed into a blockstate.

It also costs nothing. No property, no cycling interaction, no lang keys, no extra variants, and the ladder comes out evenly spaced for free because blocks are one block tall.

Why style is cosmetic

An oxer is a harder fence than a vertical in life, and it would have been easy to make one here. It is deliberately not. The height ladder is the mod’s one honest reading of a horse’s jump genetics, and it stays readable only while a stack of three means the same thing whatever it is built from. A style that jumped differently would make the measurement depend on decoration, and you would no longer be able to compare two horses without also comparing the fences you happened to build for them.

So difficulty lives in the one axis that is honest about itself — how high you stack it — and style is what the course looks like. That also means a rider can rebuild a course to taste without changing what it tests.

What a stack can actually measure

This matters more than a decoration has any right to. Nothing else in the mod makes jump visible: the debug yard’s stat pens measure it by printing Attributes.JUMP_STRENGTH, which is reading the answer rather than asking the question.

And there is a claim waiting to be checked. common/trait/HorseUnits.jumpMetres converts jump strength to metres through a cubic — it is what the info screen prints as “x.x m” — and its own class doc admits the fit is unverified against this mod’s horses, assuming scale 1.0 and normal gravity. A stack of jumps is the instrument that can finally test it: breed for a known strength, build the ladder, and see which rung the horse stops at. If the cubic is right, the rung and the metres agree.

That is a genuine experiment the mod could not previously run, and it is the reason this family was worth building before the prettier obstacle types.

Why the art is ours

Three existing Minecraft projects cover this ground — DragN’s XC Jumps, EquestrianMod’s obstacles, and the Showjumping Craft CIT pack. All three are All Rights Reserved for their assets, and the one that splits its licences (MIT code, reserved art) reserves precisely the half that would have been worth having. The author policy behind the first is explicit that models and textures may not go into a standalone mod; the exceptions are texture packs and dependent addons, which we are not.

Our own licence makes it worse rather than better: this mod is CC BY-NC 4.0 and its jars go to outside testers, so shipping someone’s reserved art would mean sublicensing it, which needs written permission and not a friendly nod. So the geometry here is drawn from the real-world object. A showjumping vertical is a rail between two standards; that is not anyone’s invention, and building it from the same vocabulary is clear.
