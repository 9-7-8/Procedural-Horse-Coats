Block / the yard

Jumps

A showjumping fence in three styles, and a real obstacle: like a fence, it cannot be stepped over. Place them side by side to widen it and on top of each other to raise it — there is no height setting, because the stack is the height — or set one directly, anywhere from a ground pole to double. One item: its height, its style, its two woods and its two coats of paint are all chosen after you put it down, by right-clicking it. And it is the first thing in the mod that makes a horse’s jump stat something you can watch rather than read.

What it is for

A jump is one rail across the block with an upright standard at each end of the run. Put two beside each other and they become one continuous rail with a post at either extremity, the way a real fence line looks; the standards in the middle disappear on their own, so you never have to think about it.

Recipe (shapeless): 3 vanilla fences of one wood + 1 horse hair → 4 jumps of that wood.

There is one jump item. The wood rides on the stack, which is why an oak jump and a birch jump are two stacks with different names rather than two items; the style is not on the item at all. Three fences of mixed woods do not craft — put the jump down and swap a half in its screen instead.

Four to a hair on purpose. A course is the one thing you build dozens of, and a hair is sheared one to three at a time, once per horse per day — so a twelve-fence course costs three hairs, which is an afternoon with one horse rather than a reason not to build.

Three styles, two woods and two coats of paint

Every jump is one of three shapes. None of them is more than one block deep — real courses put ground poles in front of an oxer, and we deliberately do not, because a jump that occupies two blocks stops being a thing you put in a row and starts being a structure.

StyleWhat it is
VerticalOne rail. The plain fence, and what a jump starts as.
OxerTwo rails with a spread, front and back, with wider standards to hold them.
CrossrailsTwo poles crossed in an X, lowest in the middle — the shape you school a young horse over. One crossed pair spans up to three blocks: build a row of them and you get one wide X, not a row of little ones.

Right-click a placed jump and it opens. Two slots for the rails and the standards, a button per style, and a height you step up and down. Everything about a jump that is not its position is changed here, which is why there is only one item.

A plank in a slot re-woods that half. One plank is spent and a plank of the wood it used to be comes straight back to you, so re-dressing a built course costs nothing at all and you can put it back exactly as it was. Shift-click a stack of two or more and both halves change at once.

A dye in a slot paints that half — and the dye is gone for good. The paint is a wash over the wood, not a replacement for it: the colour reads as the colour you chose, and the grain and shading of whatever wood is underneath still show through it.

The only way to strip paint is a fresh plank in that half, which pops the old plank out and takes the paint with it. So a coat of paint is a decision and a change of wood is not — you can always get a jump back to bare wood, and you can never get the dye back.

Height and style both apply to the whole fence, not to the block you clicked — a fence has a height and is one kind of fence; each block of it is neither. To build a mixed or stepped line on purpose, break the run and set the halves separately. Ten rungs, from 0.1 (a ground pole) to 2.0 (double), and the screen shows both the height and what a horse has to clear.

The style and height buttons are free. Right-clicking a jump opens the window whatever is in your hand, another jump included — sneak to place instead, the same thing you already do to put a block against a chest.

Style changes nothing about the jumping. All three collide identically, so a stack of three is the same fence whichever shape you build it from. That is on purpose — see why style is cosmetic.

Height is a measuring stick

A jump is an eighth of a block taller to clear than it looks. It stops you like a fence does, and a sliver of it is invisible — without that a horse simply steps over the rail, because a horse steps a full block without breaking stride.

That sliver does not scale, which is what makes the whole range work without a special case. A jump is height + ⅛ blocks to clear, so what you have to clear is never far from what you can see:

HeightTo clearWhat it is
0.10.25 blocksA ground pole — schooling, not an obstacle
0.50.625A trot pole. Still walked over
0.750.875The last walkable rung
1.01.125A low fence, and the first real one. Most horses manage, a bad jumper does not
1.51.625Wants a horse bred for it
2.02.125Beyond anything the natural loci reach on their own

The line falls where you can see it. Everything under a block is walked over and everything from a block up has to be jumped — a rule you can read off the fence rather than one you learn by failing at it.

And you can still stack. A jump on top of a jump adds its own height to the one below, so the range above 2 is reached the way it always was.

You cannot jump one on foot, any more than you can jump a fence, so a course is something you walk around and ride through. That is deliberate and it is the vanilla behaviour.

The point is not the number. It is that two horses that look identical on a stat screen are separated by a fence one of them stops at, which is the whole argument for the mod being about breeding rather than about rolling.

Is it dangerous?

No. A jump is a fence: a horse that refuses it stops, and one that hits it takes nothing. It has no effect on a horse’s health, its condition or its bond, and failing one costs nothing but the attempt.

Building a course

  • Facing is set when you place it, from the way you are looking, and the rail runs across that. A row placed while walking sideways comes out straight.
  • A jump placed on top of a jump takes the facing of the one below, so a stack lines up however you walk around it while building.
  • And its style — from the jump below it, or failing that from either jump beside it along the rail. There is one item, so a new jump has to get its style from somewhere, and continuing the run you are building is the answer that needs no clicks. The first jump of a course is the only one you have to dress.
  • A single jump is already a fence. There is no trot-pole height — the lowest thing you can build is one a horse has to leave the ground for.
  • A jump facing north and one facing south are the same fence, so they join up. You do not have to approach a row from a consistent side to build it.
  • Jumps join to jumps and to nothing else — they will not connect to a fence, a double gate or a wall.
  • A run is posted every three blocks, with an upright at the join rather than a T through the middle of a block. So three in a row has uprights at its two ends and nothing between them; six has one more, where its two halves meet.
  • A crossrail run makes one X up to three blocks wide, and the poles get shallower as it widens. Three in a row is always one three-wide X; a run of five is a three and a two; a run of seven is a three, a three and a single. The two poles sit at slightly different depths and pass each other, the way real ones rest in their cups.
  • And only to jumps of the same style. An oxer beside a vertical is two obstacles, not one fence, so each keeps its own standards. They are genuinely different shapes — an oxer's rails are front and back where a vertical's is down the middle, and its standards are deeper to hold them — so sharing a standard left an oxer's two rails running into thin air with nothing holding them up.
  • An axe is the right tool, and a jump drops its own style — break an oxer, get an oxer back.

The jump lives in the horse tab with the carrots and the papers, as one oak item. It was filed into vanilla’s Building Blocks for one build, beside each wood’s fence, on the same argument the double gates use — and the owner’s call was that the argument does not carry: a gate is a building block horses happen to care about, and a jump is horse equipment, useless without one. It then had a tab of its own for a day, back when there were thirty-six of them. There is one now, and a tab holding one thing is worse than a row.

Every wood has one, including woods other mods added — those are generated at run time from that mod’s own plank texture, so they arrive looking right without anybody drawing anything. See compatibility.

What it is not, yet

Three styles is not a course designer’s vocabulary. A wall is the next one, and the thing that was blocking it is now gone: “wall” means cobblestone, brick, stone brick — a material axis that is not the twelve woods — and a jump now carries its material as data rather than being one block per wood, so a second axis costs a list and some models rather than another dozen blocks. Also waiting on nothing but the work: crossrails spanning up to three blocks as one crossed pair rather than a row of separate X’s, and half and third height jumps as walkable ground poles. Beyond that, cross-country wants logs, brush, ditches, fillers and water, and none of it is built.

A warning for whoever builds the short ones. Stacked clearances do not add on a block grid. A third-height jump on top of a full one clears exactly what the full one did, because the obstacle is the block column, not the sum of the rails in it. The height ladder below only reads honestly while every jump is one block.

None of it costs an item any more, which is the point of there being one. Style, wood and paint are all chosen in the screen, so a new style is a fourth button rather than a thirty-seventh item, and the roster stops growing however much vocabulary the courses gain.

Nothing scores a round, times one, or notices that a horse knocked a rail, because a jump is scenery with a collision box. The Roadmap tab is where that lives.

Where it lives

  • block/JumpBlock — the geometry, the connection rule, the VoxelShape, and the two use overrides that decide between stacking and opening the screen.
  • block/JumpBlockEntity — the two woods, the client sync, and the implicit components that survive a break-and-replace.
  • block/JumpMaterials — the pair itself, and the only place that knows it is two components on an item.
  • block/JumpWoods — every wood a jump can be, the plank that stands for each, and what to call it. Read by the model, the screen, the item names and the tab.
  • block/Jumps — the one item, and the placement hint. It registered twelve blocks and thirty-six items until 2026-09-20.
  • menu/JumpMenu + client/JumpScreen — the two slots, the plank exchange, the dye, and the three style buttons. The menu carries the block’s position and reads it; see below for why it is not a ContainerData.
  • client/JumpTintSource — the paint, as a block tint per half and an item tint per half.
  • client/JumpModel — the custom block model that reads the two woods per position. See the API notes, because everything written before 1.21.4 is wrong about how one of these is built.
  • tools/bake-jumps.mjs — every shipped asset, for vanilla’s woods, at author time.
  • compat/GeneratedJumps — the same files for other mods’ woods, at run time.

The woods are data, and the item carries two components

Verified 2026-09-20, in play. The screen opens on a right-click, the style buttons change a placed jump, and a plank in a slot changes that half’s wood — owner, on the first build that had it: “the right click menu works (changing jump type, changing the color with new wood)”. Same session: “it works that the different jumps no longer connect”, so an oxer beside a vertical keeps both sets of standards.

Still unseen: a jump whose two halves are different woods, which is the thing the whole rework exists for, and every part of the paint. See the Verification tab.

The owner’s ask was that the standards and the rails be independently coloured. As blockstate properties that pair is 12 × 12 × everything else — 13,824 block states, every one allocated at registry bootstrap on the server as well as the client. So the woods are JumpBlockEntity data and the model reads them per position, which is what collapsed twelve blocks into one.

On the item they are two separate string components, horsegenetics:jump_rails and horsegenetics:jump_standards, rather than one pair-valued component — and the reason is the icon. minecraft:select on minecraft:component matches the whole value of one component, so a pair would need a case per pair: a hundred and forty-four per style, for a sixteen-pixel picture. Split, the icon selects on the rails alone in twelve cases and is never wrong about the half it shows.

Both are always present, oak included, on every jump the mod hands out — the recipes write them, the creative tab writes them, the loot table copies them off the block entity. An item with no components and an item saying “oak” are the same jump but do not stack, and a creative-tab jump that will not stack with a crafted one gets reported as an inventory bug.

The round trip is four pieces and all four are needed. collectImplicitComponents puts the woods on the way out; minecraft:copy_components with "source": "block_entity" in the loot table is what asks for them; setPlacedBy puts them back into the block entity on placement; and removeComponentsFromTag discards the block-entity copy so the item does not carry the same woods twice and let the stale copy win. Miss any one and a birch-railed jump comes back oak — which reads as “the screen did not save”, not as a loot bug.

Paint is a tint, and here that is the right tool

The wood could never have been a tint — multiplying a colour over birch grain does not produce jungle grain, which is the reason the woods became block-entity data and a per-position model in the first place. Paint is the opposite case. It is a colour laid over a wood that still shows through, which is exactly what a multiply does, so the mechanism vanilla dyes leather with is the honest one here.

It also cost almost nothing, because the two halves were already separate model parts. Tagging every rail face tintindex: 0 and every upright face tintindex: 1 is the entire model change.

The tint index is a list index, in three places that nothing checks against each other. RegisterColorHandlersEvent.BlockTintSources.register takes a list of BlockTintSource and its position is the model’s tintindex — not the per-index lambda every older version and every tutorial has (coding notes). So the order in JumpTintSource.BLOCK_SOURCES, the RAILS_TINT/ STANDARDS_TINT constants in bake-jumps.mjs, and the tints array in items/jump.json are one contract in three languages. A swap shows as a jump whose rails take the colour you painted its uprights, and logs nothing.

colorInWorld runs on chunk-meshing worker threads, exactly as JumpModel.collectParts does and against the same region snapshot, so it reads the colours through getModelData rather than through getBlockEntity. That is the same reason JumpMaterials is an immutable record.

The screen reads the block, because a ContainerData is 16 bits

The menu carried a three-int ContainerData — style, and two indices into the wood list — and it worked until paint arrived and it could not. ClientboundContainerSetDataPacket reads and writes its value with readShort/writeShort, so every value in a ContainerData is silently truncated to sixteen bits, and an RGB colour needs twenty-four.

Splitting each colour across two slots would have worked and would have been a lie about what a menu’s data vector is for. Instead the menu carries the block’s position, written into its opening packet by NeoForge’s extra-data menu factory (IMenuTypeExtension.create), and both sides read everything off the block: the style from its blockstate, the woods and the paint from its block entity. The client already has that block entity — it has to, because the model reads the woods — so the screen is showing the same values the model is drawing with by construction, rather than by two code paths agreeing.

Stacking beats the screen, and that needed an override

ServerPlayerGameMode.useItemOn calls a block’s useWithoutItem whenever its useItemOn returns TRY_WITH_EMPTY_HAND — the hand does not have to be empty, despite the name. Only a sneaking player with something in their hands suppresses it. So a block that opens a screen from useWithoutItem opens it even when the player is holding the block itself.

For a chest that is fine. For a jump it is not: height is stacking, so putting one on top of another is the most common thing anybody does to this block, and it would have needed a shift-click forever. JumpBlock.useItemOn therefore returns InteractionResult.PASS — which is not a TryEmptyHandInteraction, so the dispatch skips the screen and falls through to the item’s own useOn — for a JumpItem and only for one.

The collision box is taller than the model

JumpBlock.getCollisionShape returns the same boxes as getShape but topped at 24/16 rather than 15 and 16 — vanilla’s own fence number. The outline and the model stay tight to the rail, so the block still looks right; only what you bump into is taller.

Verified 2026-09-20, in play, and this is why it exists. The first build let collision follow the model, which put the rail under a horse’s 1.0 step height. Owner’s verdict: the jumps “look great, expand great”, but a horse walked straight over them and it “doesn’t feel immersive”. The fix is the fence trick and nothing else — no model change, no new state.

Two consequences worth knowing before changing it. The render and collision shapes are built by one function with two different tops, and the rail and the standards do not share a top in the drawn shape (15 and 16) — collapsing those into one number quietly lops a pixel off every post. And the tall box applies to players as much as horses, so a course cannot be crossed on foot. That is intended, and it is what a fence does.

The crossrails are not UV-locked

The X is drawn with model element rotation, which vanilla caps at ±45° about one axis — exactly enough for a crossrail and no more. A 16-long bar turned 45° projects to about 11 across, so it stays inside its own block. That is why crossrails do not run together the way the other two styles do: each is its own X. Intended, not a limitation waiting to be fixed.

UNVERIFIED, and reasoning rather than observation: the crossrails blockstate sets uvlock: false where the other styles set true. UV-lock re-projects a face’s texture against the block axes after the blockstate’s y rotation, and on an element that is itself rotated 45° the two rotations fight and the grain shears. The axis-aligned styles lock cleanly. Worth looking at a crossrail facing each of the four ways before trusting it.

The geometry is written three times

This is the thing to be careful about. The same shape exists as boxes in bake-jumps.mjs, as a VoxelShape in JumpBlock, and again in GeneratedJumps for modded woods. Change one, change all three.

Each drift fails differently and none of them logs. Model against shape gives you a jump you can see in one place and collide with in another. Baker against generator gives you a modded jump that is a purple cube while every vanilla one is fine.

Left, right, and the suffix that was wrong

LEFT and RIGHT are fence-style connection flags: true means another jump continues the rail on that side, so the standard there is not drawn. Left is facing.getCounterClockWise(), the same definition DoubleGates.partnerDirection uses — keep the two families agreeing about which way is left.

Verified 2026-09-20, by check-block-models.mjs, on its first run. The blockstate composed its model suffix as (left?"_l":"") + (right?"_r":""), giving _l_r, while the models were written _lr. Only the both-connected case differs, so a lone jump and a row of two were perfect and the middle of every row of three was a purple cube.

Both generators now derive the suffix from one function rather than composing it, and the check is a repo tool: node neoforge-26.1.2/tools/check-block-models.mjs, which walks every blockstate, item definition and model parent and asserts the file is there. It covers the shipped assets only — the run-time generated pack is not on disk and is still guarded by nothing but the class docs.

The axe tag is shared, so it merges

mineable/axe holds the double gates as well, and a datapack has exactly one file per tag. Both bakers therefore merge into it rather than writing {values: ours}, and GeneratedGates.mergeBlockTag does the same for the run-time pack so it does not matter which generator runs first.

Getting this wrong is worth describing because of how quietly it fails: the losing family keeps working, mines slowly, and drops nothing without an axe. That reads as a balance decision, not a bug, and could sit there for months. After running either baker, check both families are still in the tag.

Unverified

The look and the connection behaviour were confirmed in play on 2026-09-20 — they “look great, expand great”, and the row-of-three case that was broken drew correctly. The height ladder has not been ridden. It follows from a 1.5-block collision box and a horse’s 1.0 step height, and which rung a given horse stops at is exactly the thing still to be measured — along with whether it agrees with HorseUnits.jumpMetres. Stacking, and the height that was added beside it

This section used to argue against a settable height, and it was overruled. The argument was that a height state makes the jump assert a height, and that a block has no business knowing one — stacking makes height a consequence of geometry instead, so the horse is answering a question about the world rather than about a number somebody typed into a blockstate. It also cost nothing, and the ladder came out evenly spaced for free because blocks are one block tall.

Two things were wrong with it. The first is that a one-block granularity is a bad instrument: the interesting part of the range for a real horse sits between nothing and one block, and a ladder whose bottom rung is “a fence most horses clear” cannot see into it at all. The second is that it rules out the ground pole, which is not a measurement at all — it is the thing you school a young horse over, and it has to be walkable.

So height is a settable ladder of ten now, and stacking still works on top of it. The honest part of the original argument survives intact: the jump still asserts nothing about the horse. It is a number about the obstacle, the horse either clears it or does not, and the reading is the same reading. What changed is only the resolution of the ruler.

The cost is real and is worth knowing. A height is a scale on baked geometry, and there is no per-position transform on a BlockStateModelPart — so every rung is a complete second copy of every part, baked with a scaling ModelState composed into it. Ten heights is ten times the bake work. That is why it is a ladder of ten chosen heights and not a slider, and why the rungs are fine below one block and coarse above it.

It is also why the height lives on the block entity rather than in the blockstate: ten values as a property would have multiplied every state in the file by ten. The shape reads it per position instead, which is what vanilla’s shulker box does for the same reason.

Why style is cosmetic

An oxer is a harder fence than a vertical in life, and it would have been easy to make one here. It is deliberately not. The height ladder is the mod’s one honest reading of a horse’s jump genetics, and it stays readable only while a stack of three means the same thing whatever it is built from. A style that jumped differently would make the measurement depend on decoration, and you would no longer be able to compare two horses without also comparing the fences you happened to build for them.

So difficulty lives in the one axis that is honest about itself — how high you stack it — and style is what the course looks like. That also means a rider can rebuild a course to taste without changing what it tests.

What a stack can actually measure

This matters more than a decoration has any right to. Nothing else in the mod makes jump visible: the debug yard’s stat pens measure it by printing Attributes.JUMP_STRENGTH, which is reading the answer rather than asking the question.

And there is a claim waiting to be checked. common/trait/HorseUnits.jumpMetres converts jump strength to metres through a cubic — it is what the info screen prints as “x.x m” — and its own class doc admits the fit is unverified against this mod’s horses, assuming scale 1.0 and normal gravity. A stack of jumps is the instrument that can finally test it: breed for a known strength, build the ladder, and see which rung the horse stops at. If the cubic is right, the rung and the metres agree.

That is a genuine experiment the mod could not previously run, and it is the reason this family was worth building before the prettier obstacle types.

Why the art is ours

Three existing Minecraft projects cover this ground — DragN’s XC Jumps, EquestrianMod’s obstacles, and the Showjumping Craft CIT pack. All three are All Rights Reserved for their assets, and the one that splits its licences (MIT code, reserved art) reserves precisely the half that would have been worth having. The author policy behind the first is explicit that models and textures may not go into a standalone mod; the exceptions are texture packs and dependent addons, which we are not.

Our own licence makes it worse rather than better: this mod is CC BY-NC 4.0 and its jars go to outside testers, so shipping someone’s reserved art would mean sublicensing it, which needs written permission and not a friendly nod. So the geometry here is drawn from the real-world object. A showjumping vertical is a rail between two standards; that is not anyone’s invention, and building it from the same vocabulary is clear.

The rest of the vocabulary, and standards of any solid block

Standards of any solid block

built: any wood, per half; the material is already block-entity data

Cobblestone, brick, glass, dirt — whatever a player wants holding the rails up. Deferred deliberately: “not necessary for the first release” (owner, 2026-09-20). Recipe unchanged — a jump is still crafted from wood — and paint stays planks-only; this is a swap you make after the fact, in the block’s own screen.

The scoping is done and it is cheaper than it sounds, so it is worth not re-deriving. No art is generated: a material costs fifteen three-line models (three styles × five connection states) that point at the block’s existing texture, exactly as modded woods point at their own mod’s plank sheet. Two findings make it cheaper still, both verified against the 26.1.2 artefacts:

  • The model files are avoidable entirely. ModelBaker.materials().get(new Material(id), name) resolves any already-atlased texture to a sprite at bake time, and BakedQuad is a record now — so re-skinning one baked geometry per material is about ten lines of UV remapping (UVPair.pack/unpackU, rebuilt through QuadCollection.Builder into a SimpleModelWrapper). No vanilla or NeoForge sprite-remap helper exists in this version; those ten lines would be ours.
  • The roster need not be curated. A block’s representative texture is reachable generically as its own baked model’s particle sprite — ModelEvent.ModifyBakingResult exposes a mutable BakingResult after baking, and BlockStateModelSet does the same at runtime. So “any solid block” can be a runtime test rather than a hand-written list. Roughly 270–355 of vanilla’s 1,144 blocks qualify, plus whatever mods add, free.

The one real cost is not the materials. JumpModel.Unbaked.bake loops every material once per blockstate variant — ninety-six times over — which is already wasteful at twelve woods and is what would hurt at three hundred. It wants a bake cache keyed on (model id, rotation), and that is worth doing whether or not this ships.

Obstacles beyond the three styles

built: vertical, oxer and crossrails, any wood, crossrails spanning up to three blocks

The rest of the vocabulary: logs, brush, ditches, fillers, walls, water. Each is geometry in the same baker, so the cost per type is low, and the owner’s hard rule bounds it — no style is more than one block deep, because a jump that occupies two blocks stops being a thing you put in a row and starts being a structure. Also unbuilt: anything that notices a round — no timing, no scoring, no knocked rail, no refusal. A jump is scenery with a collision box, and the measuring-stick argument is the only gameplay it has. Note the three that exist collide identically on purpose, so a stack of three means the same thing whatever it is built from; anything that made a style matter mechanically would be taking that back, and the height ladder is an honest reading of genetics because it does not.

There is a second plan for jump materials, and it lives on another page. The cart and jump bench would build a jump out of per-piece materials at a bench rather than by swapping a half in the block’s own screen — the same material question this section asks, reached from the crafting side.

  • A jump keeps its height through break-and-replace, like its woods and its paint - it is a fifth component on the item.
  • Breaking an oxer gives back an oxer, not a vertical. The loot table is a switch on the style property; before, the style was silently lost.
  • The recipe is 3 fences of one wood + 1 horse hair → 4 of that wood. Twelve recipes now make one item and the wood arrives as a component. Craft spruce and check you get spruce, not oak. Mixed woods still do not craft, which is now deliberate rather than pending.
  • Modded woods need a second mod installed and have never been generated. Wood.fenceId() and the plank id are both composed rather than scanned, so an unusually-named wood gets a jump that cannot be crafted or repainted, and a warning.
  • Shift-clicking a stack of two or more planks does both halves at once, and a single plank does the rails only.
  • Stacks inherit facing from the jump below, so a two-high fence cannot come out crosswise. Style is deliberately not inherited — an oxer held over a vertical should still place an oxer.
  • Style is inherited when you extend a run — from the jump below, else from either jump beside it along the rail. Place one oxer, then build a line off it and check you do not have to press a button again. There is one item now, so this is the only thing stopping a course of oxers being thirty button presses.

  • Restyling a jump in the middle of a run grows its own standards back straight away, now that only same-style jumps share a rail.

The measurement nobody has taken

Which rung of a stack a given horse stops at, and whether it agrees with the metres on the horse screen. This is the whole reason the family was built before the prettier obstacle types: HorseUnits.jumpMetres is an unverified cubic, and a stack of jumps is the first instrument that can test it. It has already paid for itself once — see gap 287, where the Anglo-Arabian turned out to jump high enough to hurt itself landing.
