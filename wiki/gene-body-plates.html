Magical gene / dominant / grows geometry, paints nothing

Body plates

One copy and the horse grows overlapping plates of bone over its shoulders and its hips, stepped down the flank like the lames of a suit of armour, the same on both sides. Whether they are smooth, ridged or spiked, and how big, how many, how tightly lapped and how flared they are, is written on the allele. Their colour is a gene of its own, body plate colour.

Crossing two of them

One copy, either sex

Dominant: Plt/n and Plt/Plt both grow plates, so a plated horse bred to a plain one gives a plated foal at least half the time. It is not sex-limited. Foals grow none; the plates come in when the horse grows up, and stay.

Where they sit

Two clusters on each side: one over the shoulder, just behind the chest, and one over the hip. The top plate sits just under the line of the back, and each one below it laps over the next. The saddle sits between the two clusters, so a saddle or a rider hides none of them.

Finding one

Rare in the wild: about three horses in a thousand have plates, and because one copy is enough, every horse that carries the gene shows it. The skeleton horses can grow them in bare bone, as one of their horn-like parts.

What varies

  • Form: smooth (plain plates with a rim along the bottom), ridged (a raised rib along the middle of each) or spiked (a spike standing out from each).
  • Size: three classes, small, broad and massive. Small to broad in the wild; a line bred for size reaches massive and stops.
  • Count: how many plates each cluster has, from the top down, three to five in the wild. A line can be bred down to a single plate per cluster.
  • Overlap: how far each plate laps the one below it.
  • Spikiness: how far the plates flare out from the body, and how tall the rib or how long the spike stands.

Everything stacks: a horse can wear plates, a row of dorsal spines, a sail and every head part at once.

Is it good for the horse?

It does nothing at all, for good or ill: no stat, no armour value, no hitbox. It is a look.

Slice C of the body parts

The third body part (model parts), and the one that brought the flank anchors and a mesh of two rows. No new render path: the only client change is the anchor’s case in AttachedPartLayer.anchor. It is two kinds (one per side), PlateGenerator and PlateSize, and one line in GrownParts.of dressed by its colour locus. The granting locus is in GrownParts.shapes’s every-part baseline.

The mesh: slabs that hang and lap

A slab is a flat box (PartNode.width, thin across the horse) turned half round z, so it hangs from its top edge, and leaned out at the bottom by the flare. Slab j starts j × drop below the top, still flush with the body, so the one above stands proud of it. drop is a share of the slab’s height set by the overlap bucket, and is stepped closer for a big slab that would otherwise reach past ROOM (nine units down the flank). Every slab carries a lip along its bottom edge, and a ridged slab adds a rib along its middle; both are bars turned a quarter round x to run along the body. A spiked slab adds a spike of two boxes standing straight out. All of them hang straight off the slab, which is what makes them the tip colour (see the colour page). A slab sinks a tenth of a unit into the body so its top shows no gap.

Slab j of the shoulder and slab j of the hip are both group j, numbered top down (owner, 2026-10-05), so the count shows the top k of each cluster and grows the next one in on both. BodyPlatesGeneTest holds that numbering, the mirroring and the colour shape.

The epigenome, by cost

  • size: the class is the mesh’s size bucket (three; where the slabs step down the flank), the remainder a uniform per-element grow, so stretch and girth are equal.
  • form, overlap and spikiness: baked, PlateGenerator.style(form, overlap, spikiness), three of each.
  • count: shown out of the most baked, per cluster, the antler’s tine rule.

There is no separate girth: the treatment lists size, count, overlap and spikiness, and every number on the copy has to reach the part.

Invented

No horse grows armour. The look borrows from the animals that do: the bony osteoderms in the skin of crocodiles and armadillos, the overlapping scutes of a pangolin, and the plated hide of an ankylosaur, arranged here like the lames of a pauldron over the shoulder and a tasset over the hip. This is fantasy and makes no claim about any real gene. It is dominant (the treatment’s call), so like the dorsal spines it shows on every carrier, and the sail is the body part you have to breed for.

What needs verifying here

The plates have been photographed standing still, on both flanks and in every form; how they sit in motion, against a saddle’s flaps and barding, at massive size, and whether their small, tightly stacked look is the one wanted are still open.

What a photo shoot showed, and the look

2026-10-05, -PphotoShoot, eight still frames of wild-sized founders, re-rolled for a count of four or more. Both clusters sit on the outside of the flank, over the shoulder and the hip, in the top half of the side and clear of where the saddle goes. From behind, the spikes and lips stand out from both flanks, so the mirroring and the outward direction are right. Red/Blu and Grn/Yel horses wear the earlier colour on the slab and the other on its lip and rib. Bone plates are ivory beside a bone dorsal row. Seen side-on, a spike points at the camera and reads end-on.

Two things for the owner to judge. At wild size the plates are small and tightly stacked, a narrow column of four or five bars at each end of the body rather than broad pauldrons. And on a ridged slab the rib and lip cover most of the outer face, so a two-tone plate reads mostly as its edge colour with the base colour in thin rims. Wider slabs, a wilder size range, or a thinner rib would each change that. None of it is a bug.

In motion, and massive

Each form at small and at massive size, from the side and from behind, while the horse walks, grazes and rears. The plates should ride the body with no gap opening at their tops and no slab sinking into the body. A massive hip cluster reaches nearest the saddle and the croup: no slab should hang past the back of the body or below the belly.

Against a saddle, barding and a rider

The treatment’s open look, decided once it has been seen and not before. Put a saddle, then horse armour, on a horse with massive plates. Today the clusters stand clear of the vanilla saddle box, nothing hides them, and body armour is drawn over whatever stands proud. Say whether the armoured horse should hide its plates, show them through, or neither.

Two counts in one frame

Two plated horses side by side with different counts (one and five per cluster). Each should show its own count at both shoulder and hip; a horse wearing the other’s count is the deferred-submit trap.
