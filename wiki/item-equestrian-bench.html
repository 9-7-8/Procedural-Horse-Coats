Block / tack

Tack Dyeing Bench

A saddler’s bench. It dyes a saddle’s seat, its bridle and its metal fittings separately, does the same three-way job on leather horse armour, and one day will cut a banner into a saddle pad. Two horses need not look alike.

It used to be called the Equestrian Bench

Renamed on 2026-09-18, because the old name said nothing about what the block does. Only the name changed. The id is still horsegenetics:equestrian_bench — and so are the class names, the recipe, the loot table and this page’s own filename — so benches already placed in a world, and anything pointing at the id, carry on working. Renaming the id would have meant re-baking the barn structure NBT and the recipe reference for a change players see as one word, which was not worth it.

What it does

Recipe (shapeless): 1 crafting table + 1 leather + 2 horse hair.

There is also one already standing at every cowboy’s homestead, at the far end of the frontage, past the hitch and the four posts — so you are likely to meet one before you know to want it. The recipe means you never have to go back there for it.

Right-click it and you get a saddle slot, a name field, and three labelled rows — one per part. Put a saddle in, drop a dye or an ingot into whichever rows you want to change, and the recoloured saddle appears on the right. Take it and the saddle is spent, along with only the materials that actually changed something.

An empty row means “leave this one alone”, not “no colour”. Recolouring just the bridle costs one dye and leaves the seat and the fittings exactly as they were.

  • Seat — the leather you sit on. Takes any of the sixteen dyes — and any dye another mod added, including the odd ones whose colour vanilla has no name for.
  • Bridle — the headstall, noseband, bit and reins. Also a dye, and independent of the seat: a black saddle with a red bridle is two visits, not a compromise.
  • Fittings — the buckles and rings. Takes a material rather than a dye, which is the whole character of the row: you are not painting the hardware, you are saying what it is made of. Fifteen of vanilla’s, plus every ingot and gem any other mod added.

Three of those pairs sit close together, and were pulled apart on purpose. Basalt and netherite are both near-black, bone and quartz are both near-white, and prismarine and ender pearl are both blue-greens. Each was moved away from its material’s own average colour rather than set to it — basalt cooler and lighter, bone creamier and darker, ender pearl deeper than prismarine. Two fittings a player cannot tell apart on a horse are worth one option, not two.

Diamond is the loud one deliberately. It was a pale blue-white at first and read as washed-out pewter; spending diamonds on tack that does nothing whatever except look expensive ought to look expensive.

Bone, not bone meal

Bone meal carries a dye component, so it would satisfy the seat and bridle rows as well — a single item meaning two different things in a bench whose entire premise is leather-or-material. The bone itself has no such ambiguity.

Coal is not #000000, and that is a trap rather than a taste call

The colours here feed getArmorLayerTintColor, and returning zero from that hook tells the renderer to skip the layer altogether. Pure black would therefore not give you black hardware; it would give you no hardware. Coal is a near-black with a slight cool cast, which reads identically and cannot trip it.

And whatever your other mods brought

The fifteen are no longer the whole list. Any item another mod files under the c:ingots/* or c:gems/* common tags is a fitting too, coloured from its own item art — so a pack with a tin, a silver and a ruby in it has three more fittings, with nothing to install and nothing to switch on. The tooltip names the material rather than the item, so a modded fitting reads “tin” the way the fifteen above read “brass”.

The fifteen win on a clash, and that is the important half. Several of them were deliberately moved away from their material’s own average colour — basalt cooler, bone creamier, ender pearl deeper — precisely so that two fittings a player cannot tell apart are not both offered. A modded copper that averaged to its own true colour would quietly redefine copper and undo that, so anything already on the list above answers first and the modded lookup is only ever a fallback. The colours themselves are read off the art because there is nowhere else an ingot’s colour is written down.

Nothing pulls a modded fitting away from its neighbour. The hand-picked fifteen were separated by eye, one at a time; a generated one is simply the average of its own picture. So a pack with several similar metals in it can perfectly well end up with two fittings that look the same on a horse — which is the exact thing the fifteen were tuned to avoid, and there is no equivalent tuning pass available for materials nobody has seen. It has not been looked at in a game either way.

What the wheel still lacks: pink and magenta, and probably for good. Blue and black were the real holes and lapis and coal fill them. But no vanilla material is pink, and the only way to reach it would be admitting dyes into the fittings row — which would cost that row the thing that makes it interesting. You are not painting the hardware, you are saying what it is made of, and nothing the world is made of is pink.

Naming a saddle

The field across the top names the saddle — Daisy’s Saddle — and the name travels with it, so a tack room full of saddles is a tack room you can read. Naming costs nothing and can be done in the same pass as dyeing, or on its own.

What it costs

One material per part you actually change. Dye all three rows at once and it costs three materials and one pass; change one and it costs one. The bench will not charge you for a part that is already the colour you asked for, and a dye will not colour metal nor an ingot leather — in either case no result appears at all rather than a material quietly vanishing.

Reading a saddle you already own

Hover it. The tooltip names all three parts — “Seat: red, Bridle: black, Fittings: gold” — by dye and metal name rather than as hex codes. That matters because the icon can only show one colour, and it shows the seat: the largest area, and the one people mean when they say what colour a saddle is.

Two things it politely refuses. A dye will not colour metal and an ingot will not colour leather, so the result stays empty until the material suits the part you picked. And if the part is already that colour nothing appears either — the bench will not take a dye for doing nothing. Putting iron in is how you put the fittings back to plain steel, because plain steel is what iron means here.

Nothing is eaten if you change your mind

Close the screen and whatever is still in the slots comes back to you. Only taking the result spends anything.

An undyed saddle is exactly the saddle you know

The bench works by colouring three layers that were cut out of vanilla’s own saddle texture, and a saddle nobody has dyed carries no colour at all — so it renders pixel for pixel as it always has. That matters beyond the horse: donkeys, mules, skeleton horses and zombie horses wear the same saddle, and none of them changed. Camels, pigs, striders and nautiluses were left alone entirely.

It does leather horse armour too

Put leather horse armour in the top-left slot instead of a saddle and the bench works exactly the same way — except that the three row labels change, because a caparison has no seat and no bridle:

  • Blanket — the broad sheet over the body. This is the part vanilla has always let you dye.
  • Trim — the brown strapwork and the saddlebags.
  • Fittings — the buckles and bars, from the same list of materials as a saddle’s: the fifteen plus whatever your other mods brought.

Iron, gold, diamond and netherite horse armour are not accepted, and that is deliberate rather than an oversight: none of them is dyeable, so taking them would only promise a result that never appears. The generated horse armours for other mods’ metals are not accepted either, for the same reason — each one already is its metal’s colour, and there is no second zone on it to dye.

The crafting-grid recipe still works, and still does only the blanket

You can keep dyeing leather horse armour the way you always have, in a crafting grid with dyes. That path colours the blanket alone, exactly as it did before this bench existed — the trim stays brown and the fittings stay steel.

That took deliberate work, because the obvious implementation makes vanilla worse rather than merely different. Vanilla reads a stack’s dye once and hands the same value to every dyeable layer, so simply adding trim and fitting layers would have made a grid-dyed armour turn one flat colour over straps and buckles alike. Those two layers are pinned to their own undyed constants whenever no bench colour is present. The bench is the only thing that can colour them, which is the distinction worth having: the grid is the crude tool and the bench the fine one.

The two tools compose, in that order. Bring an armour you dyed in a grid years ago, change only its trim, and the blanket keeps the colour you gave it. The bench reads an untouched armour’s existing dyed_color rather than assuming brown — without that, editing one row would have written the blanket back to plain leather and thrown your dye away without a word about it.

Not yet: the saddle pad, and cutting a banner into one. Those are saddle-pad roadmap, along with the reasoning behind every decision here.

Loom-shaped, not furnace-shaped

It computes its result the moment a slot changes and keeps nothing between uses, so there is no block entity — no ticker, no save and load, no ContainerData to sync. ContainerLevelAccess is the whole connection to the world, exactly as vanilla’s loom does it. The research shelf needs one only because it ticks; this does not.

The part being dyed travels through clickMenuButton, vanilla’s own plumbing, so unlike the shelf this needs no custom packet — a zone is a small int and vanilla already carries small ints.

Three colours on one item

Vanilla holds one dye per stack and reads it once, outside its layer loop, so every layer of an equipment asset takes the same value however many there are. That is why this cannot be dyed_color. The three colours live in a tack_tint component and reach the renderer through NeoForge’s getArmorLayerTintColor, which is handed the layer index for exactly this limitation.

The component’s indices are the layer order in assets/minecraft/equipment/saddle.json, and the two must not drift. The three textures are disjoint, so a wrong order would not overlap anything — it would quietly paint the bridle in the seat’s colour, which is the kind of bug that looks like a decision. How the three were cut out of vanilla’s sheet, and why an undyed saddle recomposes to it exactly, is on the item roster.

Absent means undyed, and that path is load-bearing rather than incidental: with no component each layer resolves its own color_when_undyed, and those constants were chosen so the three recompose to vanilla. It is what lets all of this ride on minecraft:saddle rather than needing an item of our own, so a saddle you already owned can be brought to the bench and dyed.

What a slot accepts is now a question, not a table lookup

Both slot filters used to be membership tests — METALS.containsKey(item) for the fittings row, stack.has(DataComponents.DYE) for the two leather rows. Both are now a call to the same method the result uses: metalColour(stack) and dyeColour(stack) return a colour or null, and a slot accepts whatever is not null.

That matters beyond tidiness. mayPlace, quickMoveStack and the recipe all three used to ask the question in their own words, and anything added to one had to be remembered in the other two. Now the set of things the bench takes is defined in exactly one place per row, which is what made admitting modded materials a change to one method rather than to five conditions. The precedence is inside that method: the hand-picked fifteen answer first and the modded lookup is the fallback, and for dyes it is the other way up — vanilla’s component first, because it is the answer for most modded dyes too, and the art-averaged colour only for the ones that carry no component at all.

Verified

Played 2026-09-16, and the dyeing works. Confirmed in a world by the owner: a saddle dyes, the three zones take colours independently, recolouring the fittings works, and an undyed saddle is indistinguishable from before — “if there’s a difference, I can’t tell”, which is the visual confirmation of the zero-pixel measurement. Inputs come back when the screen is closed.

One thing that play caught and measurement had not: the dyes came out duller than vanilla’s dyed leather. Tinting multiplies, so vividness is decided by how bright the base is, and the base was being divided by vanilla’s leather brown. That constant is free — any value reproduces an undyed saddle, provided no channel clamps — so it is now the per-channel maximum of the leather pixels. Mean luminance went 151.2 to 180.9 on the seat against vanilla leather’s 167.5, and blue, which the old brown had capped at 183, reaches a full 255. The fittings needed no such change because their constant already was their maximum, which is exactly why they looked right when the leather did not.

The window, the name field, the tooltip and the icon all confirmed the same day, after the brightening: “the dye is now very bright and looks great … the item caption works perfectly, the item icon recolors, the name box looks great, you can fill all three rows at once”. Shift-clicking routes a saddle, a dye and an ingot each to their own slot. Two defects were found by playing and fixed in the same session — both worth keeping, because neither was visible from the code:

  • The name box trapped Escape. The guard was copied from the anvil without understanding what it tested: EditBox.canConsumeInput() is isActive() && isFocused() && isEditable() — it says nothing about which key, so once focused it swallowed every key including Escape and the window could not be closed. Escape is now handled before the box sees it, and unfocuses the box if it is focused rather than closing straight away.
  • The tooltip never appeared, despite the component implementing TooltipProvider correctly. Implementing it is necessary and not sufficient: ItemStack.addDetailsToTooltipComponents walks a hardcoded list of vanilla components, so a modded one is never asked. It goes through NeoForge’s ItemTooltipEvent instead.

Still unseen: the bench that generates at a cowboy’s homestead — the crafted one is confirmed, the placed one has never been laid eyes on — plus the brighter diamond and the quartz fittings, built after that session. See the Verification tab.

What needs verifying here

Before anything horse-shaped, put on a leather chestplate — the override rewrites equipment/leather.json, which every leather item in the game reads, and a changed player armour is worse than anything else here — then grid-dye a horse armour, recolour only its trim at the bench, and check the grid colour survives.

Dyeable leather horse armour (2026-09-16, NOT played)

Check the player’s leather armour first, before anything horse-shaped

There is no leather_horse_armor.json. The asset is equipment/leather.json, and that one file carries horse_body, humanoid, humanoid_baby and humanoid_leggings together — leather horse armour and every leather chestplate, helmet, legging and boot in the game read it. Our override rewrites horse_body only and copies the other three verbatim, the way saddle.json leaves camels and striders alone. So put on a leather chestplate, dye one in a crafting grid, and confirm both look exactly as they always have. If the player’s armour changed, the override reached further than intended and that is far more serious than anything below.

The bench now takes leather horse armour as well as a saddle, and the three row labels change with it — Seat / Bridle / Fittings for a saddle, Blanket / Trim / Fittings for armour, and the saddle’s words when the slot is empty. Shift-clicking either from the inventory should land it in the top-left slot. Check the labels actually swap when you drop armour in, since an empty bench shows the saddle’s wording and the swap is the easy thing to get wrong.

Vanilla’s crafting-grid dye must still work, and must still colour only the blanket. This is the one that could quietly get worse than vanilla rather than merely broken: vanilla reads a stack’s dye once and hands it to every dyeable layer, so our two extra layers would take it too and a grid-dyed armour would go one flat colour over trim and buckles alike. Those two layers are pinned to their own undyed constants when no bench tint is present. Dye an armour in a grid and confirm the straps stay brown and the hardware stays grey. Check the icon too — a grid-dyed piece should still show its colour in the hotbar.

Then the two together, in that order, which is the check most likely to catch something. Dye an armour in a crafting grid, take that piece to the bench, and recolour only the trim. The blanket must keep its grid colour. This was a real defect found by reading the committed code rather than by playing: the bench assumed any armour without its own tint was plain brown, so editing one row wrote the blanket back to leather and destroyed the player’s dye silently. It now seeds the blanket from dyed_color. Worth confirming precisely because the failure is invisible — you get a result, it just is not the one you asked for.

An undyed leather horse armour must be indistinguishable from before. Same guarantee as the saddle and the same arithmetic behind it: vanilla’s leather_overlay.png was split three ways — 417 texels of brown trim, 79 of metal, 92 of near-black shadow that is deliberately not dyeable — and the bake asserts zero orphans, no clamping and a worst round-trip error of 1. The shadow stays untinted on purpose: it reads as depth rather than as material, and tinting it is how dyed tack starts looking like a flat sticker.

The split was measured, not reasoned, and two guesses died on the way: that the dark greys were outlines (they are not — the bag outlines are dark brown), and that hardware was grey above 0x3E (that threshold cuts each metal bar in half). The boundary is 0x20, where thresholds plateau and every bar comes out solid.

Two more fittings have never been rendered either: lapis lazuli, which fills the blue the wheel completely lacked, and coal for a true black — netherite is the darkest of the old set but is really a warm plum, so check the two read as different materials rather than as one.

The fittings slot, with another mod's metal in it (2026-09-18, nothing drawn)

The mod reads every other mod's jar at startup and treats each ingot or gem they add as a fittings material. It has only ever been exercised against a synthetic test mod on a dedicated server, where nothing draws. This needs a real mod with an ingot in it installed — the compatibility page's checklist covers picking one and reading the run out of the log.

  • The bench takes their ingot. Put a modded ingot or gem in the fittings slot. Pass: it is accepted, the saddle's hardware comes out in that metal's colour, and the tooltip names the material (“tin”) rather than the item. Also worth a look: whether two of that pack's metals come out looking the same on a horse — the fifteen were pulled apart by hand and a generated one is just the average of its own picture.

A dyeable saddle: what is still unseen (2026-09-16)

What has been checked already: a dedicated server boots clean on this work — Done (0.289s), no errors or exceptions, 194 genes and 122 breeds registered. That matters because the names drop-in runs from the mod constructor, which is the one place a change fails only at load. It also caught a real bug, a warning about our own README that would have appeared on every boot. What a server boot cannot tell you is anything below, because none of it is rendering.

The saddle is dyeable now, and an undyed one should be indistinguishable from before. That is the part worth checking hardest, because it is what keeps every other mob looking untouched: the textures are derived from vanilla by dividing out the leather tint, and both the worn saddle and the inventory icon were verified to recompose to vanilla with zero differing pixels. So a plain saddle on a donkey, a mule, a skeleton horse or a zombie horse should look exactly as it always has. If any of them looks pale, washed out or grey, the tint is not being applied and the base texture is showing raw — that is the failure mode to watch for.

Camels, pigs, striders and nautiluses were deliberately left alone. Our equipment asset carries vanilla's entry verbatim for those five layer types, so they should be untouched. Worth a glance at a strider or a saddled pig to confirm the override did not reach further than intended.

There is a crafting route now, and it is the whole point: the Tack Dyeing Bench. Craft one, or find the one standing at a cowboy’s homestead past the four posts — the generated one has never been seen, so confirm it is actually there and not buried in a wall or floating. Then dye a saddle’s seat, bridle and fittings in turn and check all three survive on the horse independently.

The bench itself is confirmed and has moved to its own page — colours apply, the three zones are independent, the window, the name field, the tooltip and the icon tint all work, and the brightened dyes were checked against vanilla’s dyed leather. What is left here is only what has never been looked at.

Five fittings have never been rendered: redstone, ender pearl, basalt, bone and prismarine. The three worth actually comparing side by side are the pairs that were deliberately pulled apart rather than set to their material’s own colour — basalt against netherite, bone against quartz, and prismarine against ender pearl. If either half of a pair is not obviously its own colour on the horse, the separation was not enough and the constant should move further, not the material be dropped. Note also that the fittings tooltip now consults the materials before the dyes, so redstone must read “Fittings: redstone” and never “Fittings: red”.
