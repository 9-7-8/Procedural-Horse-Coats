2026-09-17, after dark — A doorway is a hole, and a hole leaks

    A stall you walk into through a plain gap could not be bound. The sign refused, and the
    only ways to make it take were to hang a gate in the gap or brick it up — owner:
    “if you put the sign on the y=2 level, you’re forced to brick over the
    entrance.” The fix is a second way for a room to close, described on
    stall signs; what it does not yet prove is
    gap 153.

Why the floor alone could never see it

    The detector walks floor columns, and that rule was itself bought with a
    dead horse: the version before it flood-filled air three blocks up, climbed the
    one-block fence at the front of every real stall and spilled into the aisle. Walking the
    floor fixed that permanently and should not be touched. But it means the room’s
    boundary is read at exactly one height — and at that height a doorway is simply
    missing. The floor of the stall and the floor of the yard are the same surface, so the
    fill strolled out through the gap, ran past MAX_COLUMNS and reported
    “not a room”. Every symptom pointed at the sign’s height because that
    is what the owner was varying, but height was never the variable; the gap was.

    The wall above the gap is not missing. A doorway has a lintel. So when the floor
    runs away, each height within three blocks of the sign is flood-filled for a ring of wall
    that does close, and the floor is then walked inside that ring —
    the ring is the boundary, the floor is still what the room is made of. The search is
    StallFill.fillWithin, in common/ beside the fill it wraps,
    because the lesson from the dead horse was that a piece of pure logic parked on the
    Minecraft side is one nobody can write a five-line test for. It is two lines of graph
    work and a mask; the NeoForge side only answers “is this cell wall”.

Lowest ring, not smallest — and the bug that settled it

    Several heights can close at once, so one of them has to win. Smallest is
    the obvious rule and is wrong: it is precisely what
    gap 223 did, where
    smaller() chose a one-tile pocket over the real stall and a ticket put a
    horse in a wall. A one-tile pocket at some odd height is exactly the shape that rule would
    hand back. Lowest is the rule that matches the building: a doorway’s
    lintel is the first height at which the gap seals, and every ring above it is a
    larger room containing this one — the barn the stall stands in, or, once the walls
    run out, the sky. The doorway’s own square belongs to the ring, so the stall ends
    there, which is the same answer hanging a gate in the gap gives. A door, trapdoor or gate
    counts as wall at ring height too, since the top half of a door is where a doorway’s
    ring closes; a sign does not, for the same reason a sign is never floor.

What play confirmed, and the one thing it could not

    Three bind-and-ticket cycles in the yard. The stall bound with the sign at head height,
    and — the half of the complaint this settles — measured the same room
    whether the sign hung level with the floor at y=129 or a block above it at y=130:
    room 17, 129, 100 to 20, 136, 104 (20 tiles), the ticket landing dead centre
    at 19.00, 129.00, 102.50 with the box fitting first try, no refusal and no
    inWall anywhere in the log. Owner: “making stalls works
    perfectly”.

    It does not prove the ring did it. A gated entrance and an open doorway
    produce an identical success line, so the log cannot say which code path approved those
    three binds, and the discriminating test — break the lintel so the gap runs open to
    the sky, expect a refusal, put it back, expect it to take — was not run. That is
    written down rather than glossed: gap 153 carries the
    question and verification carries the test. It is the first
    thing to do next, and it is one block of work.
