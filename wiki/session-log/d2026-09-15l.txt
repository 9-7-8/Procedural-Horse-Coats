The bake, and what it says about hand-written breed files

    Run in the prescribed order: bakeBreedFiles on its own, then the tests, then the
    designer assets, then the build. It rewrote three files — the Tersk, the Trottatore
    Italiano and the Ukrainian Riding Horse — and every change was 1.0 becoming
    1 in a size band. That is BreedSpecWriter's number
    formatting disagreeing with hand-written JSON, and nothing else. The useful result is the negative
    one: nothing was warned-and-dropped at load, so the allele tokens that were checked
    by eye against the new Allele(…) lines held up, and the hand-written field order
    was right everywhere it mattered. index.json needed no change. The browser bundle had
    been sitting at 108 entries against 122 on disk, which was the visible symptom of the wait, and is
    current again.

    Green: every *Breed*Test class — twelve of them, including
    DhampirBreedTest and BreedingPreviewTest, which a grep turned up beyond the
    obvious ten — plus ConceivableTest, DietGeneTest and
    ReproRulesTest, the three outside the breed package that touch breeds at all. Then
    :web:bakeDesignerAssets, the designer boot check,
    :neoforge-26.1.2:build, check-links and check-parity. The full
    :common:test was deliberately not run; what it would still cover is the coat goldens and
    the gene suite, which adding a breed cannot move.

The two documents, and why the country table was not imported

    Neither file was a breed draft. One was a breed-count-by-country table, marked INCOMPLETE by its
    author; the other a long list of registries sorted by how a horse gets into them. Both were
    read, mined into a roadmap entry and deleted.

    The country table is not a coverage oracle, and saying so is the point of the entry.
    Every row but one already ships — the exception is the Dutch Warmblood, awkward because the
    Gelderland's own notes name KWPN as the book it was folded into — and it
    under-represents the mod badly in the other direction, carrying no row at all for China or the
    Balkans, where a good part of the catalogue now comes from. Importing it as data would have taught
    the mod nothing and implied a gap that is not there.

    The registry document gave a candidate list (the continental draft cluster, the natives, the gaited
    and show breeds, the spotted-pony registries, the sport-horse books) and one genuinely new
    design question: open versus closed studbooks are not modelled anywhere, and the breed format cannot
    express them — BreedSpecParser's key whitelist has no such field, and the
    idea survives only as prose in notes. It is written up as an owner's call rather than a
    plan, because gap 134 already reasoned against studbooks in
    another context and the nearest hook, strains, is itself limited by
    gap 256. The same entry records that country sits
    on nearly every breed with nothing reading it, which is worth settling before the catalogue
    grows by another country's worth of drafts.

The wasm is not reproducible, and that is not what the TeaVM spike proved

    web.wasm came back dirty from the bake with no common/ Java change behind
    it. It was not staleness: no common/src/main/java commit exists since the binary was
    last baked, and two forced rebuilds of an identical tree produced two different binaries at
    identical size. The rebuild was reverted — a data-only bake should leave the binary
    alone — and it is written up as gap 261.

    This does not contradict the 2026-09-06 TeaVM spike, and a later session should not
    “correct” one against the other. That spike proved the compiled code produces
    byte-identical coats to the JVM; this is about the binary not being reproducible
    between builds. The practical consequence is one-directional: the CLAUDE.md rule holds
    as written — common/ changed with the wasm unchanged still means the designer is
    running yesterday's mod — but the converse is false, and committing a moved wasm spends a
    2MB binary diff on nothing while camouflaging a real one.
