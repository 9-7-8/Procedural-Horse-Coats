2026-09-16, overnight — a saddle worth dyeing, and the tack bug was never the one we recorded

    Asked to build our own dyeable saddle, to leave other mobs looking default, and to look
    into what tack is reachable without learning 3D modeling. The last of those turned up two
    findings that overturned things this project had already written down, which is the more
    useful result.

The saddle, derived rather than drawn

    The leather/hardware split needed no artistic judgement, and that is why it could
    be a script. Vanilla's 64×64 saddle sheet holds exactly nine colours: six
    browns at 66–76% saturation and three pure greys. Leather is
    “saturated”, hardware is “R equals G equals B” — 208 pixels
    and 36, summing to vanilla's 244 with no overlap. That rule is stated once, in
    tools/tack/bake-saddle-textures.ps1, and asserted, so a future vanilla
    retexture that breaks the assumption fails the bake instead of shipping a muddy saddle.

    The base is vanilla's colour divided by the undyed tint, so base × undyed
    reproduces vanilla exactly. Measured worst error: zero, on both the
    entity sheet and the inventory icon, verified twice — once inside the bake and
    again by reading the written files back and recomposing them against vanilla. That
    exactness is not vanity: it is what lets the asset cover donkeys, mules, skeleton and
    zombie horses without anyone noticing, which was the requirement.

    The icon needed its own constant, and finding out why was the useful part.
    The first attempt reused the entity divisor and the bake refused it — worst error
    81. The icon's palette is genuinely brighter (channel maxima 241/153/136 against
    138/85/46), so dividing by the entity's constant overflowed and clamped. The same run
    also reported zero hardware pixels, which was itself wrong: the icon's three hardware
    pixels are warm greys at ~22% saturation, so the neutral test never matched
    them. Three pixels out of ninety-two on a 16×16 icon are not worth a second layer,
    so the icon is treated as all leather and tints very slightly. The guard earned
    its keep twice in ten minutes, which is the argument for writing the assertion
    before the asset.

    Scoped to the horse family only. Camel, pig, strider and nautilus keep vanilla's entry
    verbatim in our override — a horse mod has no business repainting camels.

The tack-fit bug was misdiagnosed on this project's own roadmap

    §23.1 said the equipment layers render at vanilla scale over a resized body and need
    to inherit state.scale. They already inherit it.
    LivingEntityRenderer.submit scales the pose stack and then iterates the
    layers inside the same push/pop. The real cause is that vanilla applies
    MeshTransformer.scaling(1.1F) to three layers — HORSE,
    HORSE_ARMOR, HORSE_SADDLE — in
    LayerDefinitions, one level above the mesh function that
    HdHorseModel copied. So the transform was never in the copied part, and its
    absence is invisible on inspection: that class's doc discusses UV layout at length and
    scale not at all.

    It is also not only a scale —
    scaled(f).translated(0, 24.016 × (1 − f), 0) — so tack sat
    2.4 units off vertically as well as 10% large. The bug was on every horse,
    including scale 1.0; the size locus multiplied the gap rather than causing it,
    which is exactly why it read as “worse on big horses”. One line to fix. Both
    ends were re-verified by hand before touching anything, because the same report also
    contained the claim that turned out to be wrong.

What the research changed about the tack plan

    Geometry is hidden with alpha, not with JSON. Equipment draws through an
    alpha-cutout pass, so a cube whose texels are transparent does not appear — which
    means a halter, a bridle and a blanket are all re-textures of meshes that already exist,
    with no modeling and no Java. A blanket does not even need a minecraft:
    override, because equipment assets are keyed by their own file's namespace. And for two
    colours on one item, NeoForge already provides
    getArmorLayerTintColor with a layer index, which is twenty lines against the
    hundred-and-fifty of the custom render layer this roadmap had proposed — a layer
    that would not have worked as written anyway, since rendering is deferred and toggling
    part.visible between submits mutates one shared model.

The saddle learns three zones

    The two-way split became a three-way one: seat, bridle and metal, each
    its own dyeable layer, which is what lets the seat and the bridle be dyed independently
    and the hardware recoloured for an ingot. The interesting part is that the two splits are
    found differently. Metal is a colour question — vanilla's
    sheet holds six saturated browns and three pure greys, so hardware is exactly
    R=G=B. Seat versus bridle is a
    geometric one, because both are the same brown; they are separated by
    which of EquineSaddleModel's cubes owns the texel.

    Verified on the written files rather than in the bake: 134 seat, 74 bridle, 36 metal,
    summing to vanilla's 244, with no texel in two layers, none uncovered, none painted
    outside vanilla's silhouette, and zero recompose error. An undyed saddle
    is still pixel-identical to the one players know, which is what keeps donkeys, mules,
    skeleton and zombie horses untouched.

A formula error that invalidated its own verdict, and then survived it

    The first three-way bake refused to ship: two painted texels belonged to no cube. They
    turned out to be a bug in the analysis, not a gap in the sheet. A cube's
    side row unwraps as west(d) | north(w) | east(d) | south(w), and the south
    face had been placed at u+d+w+w rather than u+d+w+d —
    wrong for every cube whose depth and width differ, which is most of them.

    That formula had already produced a published answer. The
    “seat 513, bridle 374, no overlap” figures given the day before came from it,
    as did a “four unexplained texels” loose end that was never a mystery at all.
    Recomputed: 522 and 380, still no overlap, nothing unexplained. The verdict
    survived; the numbers did not — and it was only caught because the bake
    asserts that every painted texel finds a home and refuses to write otherwise. An
    assertion written before the asset is what turned a wrong published number into a
    ten-minute correction.

The Equestrian Bench, designed

    A block with its own screen that dyes the three zones, recolours the metal for an ingot
    or a gem, and turns a chosen part of a banner into a saddle pad. Eight decisions settled
    with the owner and written up on the saddle-pad page;
    nothing built beyond the zones above. The order is deliberate — bench and dyeing
    first, the pad and the banner after — because the dyeing is nearly certain and the
    banner half holds all the uncertainty.

    Two research results reshaped it. Rasterising a banner is not a risk:
    pattern layers are ordinary 64×64 resource PNGs and the composite is a multiply and
    an alpha-over, so the coat engine's existing texture pipeline takes it unchanged. And
    the danger in the horse-inventory slot is not the slot — it is
    quickMoveStack, which derives every shift-click range from hardcoded indices
    and is shared with donkeys, mules, llamas and nautiluses. A mis-ranged move there
    duplicates items and looks fine until somebody shift-clicks with a chest on the horse.

And then phase one of it was built

    Block, menu, screen and three-zone dyeing — the half with no uncertainty in it.
    The bench turned out small, and for a reason worth recording: it
    computes its result the moment a slot changes and keeps nothing between uses, so it is
    shaped like vanilla's loom rather than like the research shelf. No block entity, no
    ticker, no save and load, no ContainerData sync — none of which would
    have had anything to carry. The zone picker rides clickMenuButton, so
    unlike the shelf it needs no custom packet either: a zone is a small int and vanilla
    already carries small ints.

    The screen had to land in the same change as the block, not after it.
    registerBlockItem auto-joins the creative tab, so the bench was obtainable
    the instant it was registered — and a menu with no registered screen crashes the
    client on the first right-click. That is a nasty shape of half-finished: it compiles, it
    builds, and it is broken only for whoever opens it.

    The barn coordinate was probed, not chosen. The obvious next cell along
    the homestead frontage turned out to be the doorway — the oak step is its
    threshold and the door sits behind it — which is why the hitch and the table flank
    it rather than sitting in a row. The bench went one further along, fronting a solid log
    corner with headroom. put() overwrites without checking bounds or
    occupancy, so guessing would have silently replaced a block and looked fine until
    somebody walked into a barn and found a door bricked up. The reasoning is recorded
    beside the call, and the bake's own palette count going 41 to 42 is what proved the
    block actually entered the structure.

Played, and four things came back from it

    The bench went into a world and the dyeing worked — zones independent, fittings
    recolouring, an undyed saddle indistinguishable, inputs returned on Escape. Four notes
    came back, and the most interesting one was measurable.

    “Not as brilliantly coloured as vanilla’s dyed leather.”
    That is a headroom problem, not an art problem. Tinting multiplies, so vividness is
    decided entirely by how bright the base is, and the base was vanilla’s saddle
    divided by vanilla’s leather brown. That constant is free: base
    × undyed reproduces vanilla whatever it is, so long as no channel clamps. Setting
    it to the per-channel maximum of the leather pixels lifted mean luminance from
    151.2 to 180.9 against vanilla leather’s 167.5 — and lifted blue, which the
    brown’s B=0x40 had capped at 183, to a full 255. The fittings zone
    needed nothing because 0x717171 already was its maximum, which is precisely
    why the owner reported the metal looking right while the leather did not. The diagnosis
    was in the report if you read it as a measurement rather than a complaint.

    “Store the dyed information as NBT on each saddle.” It
    always was — a component, which is the modern form of exactly that. But the
    icon still read vanilla’s dyed_color, which our saddles do
    not carry, so a saddle rendering in three colours on the horse sat in the hotbar looking
    plain. From the outside that is indistinguishable from not being stored at all, and the
    report was right even though the stated cause was not. A custom
    ItemTintSource fixes the icon; a tooltip names all three zones, because an
    icon can only ever show one.

    The zone selector is gone, replaced by three labelled rows so all three
    can be dyed in one pass, and the window grew to carry a name field — a saddle can
    be “Daisy’s Saddle” now. Naming needed its own payload, because
    vanilla’s rename packet is hard-wired to AnvilMenu; the keyboard guard
    is the anvil’s too, without which typing an e closes the window.
