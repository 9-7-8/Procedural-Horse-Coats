Horse care: gated healing, bond & herds
(Gameplay / non-genetic)
Three non-genetic systems that apply to every horse and share one slow tick ( server/HorseCareHandler ): an invisible hunger that pays for healing beside water, a per-horse bond number that changes how a horse behaves toward its owner, and an emergent herd grouping. First slice built 2026-09-02, not play-tested — checklist in the Verification tab . This page is the single source of truth for the machinery; what is still missing is on the roadmap tab .

How free-roaming horses actually live The herd system models the American mustang on Bureau of Land Management range. It is the best-studied free-roaming horse population, and the one the sources below describe. The headline: a “herd” is not one group with a leader. It is a network of small social bands sharing broad, overlapping ground. Bands gather for a while at water, a salt lick or especially good forage, but they do not defend a fixed territory the way a territorial animal does. The framework below is consistent across them. The details are local. Family bands A breeding band is commonly one adult stallion, several adult mares, this year’s foals, yearlings and some older juveniles, and now and then one or more subordinate adult or near-adult males. Bands run from two to twenty or more horses. One comparison found about eight in open sage-steppe and four or five in wooded or dense-cover country, and multi-stallion bands turned up mostly among the larger bands. “One stallion, many mares” is fundamentally right, but it oversimplifies: The stallion is the band’s reproductive defender and cohesion-keeper , not necessarily the one who decides where it goes. Mature mares, especially older established ones, often decide movement, resting places and water use. In a USGS behavioural study the most dominant horse in a band was often a mare, and the stallion’s rank relative to his own mares varied from band to band. Mares are together almost all the time and settle a working order among themselves; the stallion spends much of his time on the edge. A subordinate “beta” stallion may be tolerated, may help defend or herd, and may or may not breed. Mares are not possessions. They form lasting friendships, can change bands, can resist or follow different stallions, and often carry a band through a change of stallion. What the band stallion actually does His work is mostly female defence , not leading from the front: staying alert to nearby males and bands, and putting himself between a rival and his mares; driving a straying mare back to the band — herding , with a lowered head and neck, pinned ears, nips and short chases, usually from behind or alongside; chasing bachelors and rival band stallions; guarding a mare in heat especially closely; investigating urine and dung with the flehmen lip-curl, and marking with urine or with dung “stud piles”; threatening, displaying and, when he has to, fighting. Defence is not constant violence. Most conflict is settled by ritualised signals long before a real fight: pinned ears, head and neck posture and the “snake neck”; approaching, displacing, yielding or turning away; tail swishing and threatening faces; chasing or a brief bite; and only in escalated cases rearing, striking and boxing. Serious stallion fights do happen, mostly over a mare or a takeover, but fighting wastes energy and risks injury, and many challenges end when one male simply yields. Mares are the social core Stallions are displaced, die, lose mares or take over other bands; mares and their relationships supply the continuity. A mare’s ties include her foal, especially in its first year; preferred grooming partners; familiar adult mares, relatives in some populations; a resident stallion, who may be a lasting partner or may change with band turnover; and the young horses who learn routes, boundaries and feeding and water routines from her. Mutual grooming of the neck, withers, mane and shoulder is both insect control and bonding. Resting horses stand in close groups, often head to tail , so each tail keeps flies off a neighbour’s face. Bachelor bands and how a stallion gets mares Bachelor bands hold colts that have left their natal band, young stallions not yet able to keep mares, stallions that lost their band, older former band stallions, and males between chances. The BLM handbook names breeding bands and bachelor bands as the two main social structures. A bachelor band is not a waiting room. It is where young males learn to negotiate rank and distance, how hard to push before another horse retaliates, play-fighting and combat, herding and courtship, how to approach a band without provoking all-out aggression, and who is dominant, who is an ally and who is too dangerous to challenge. Bachelors graze, travel, rest and groom together, but each is also a future competitor, so membership and hierarchy are looser than in a family band. They show more open play and rank-testing than settled band stallions: sparring and neck wrestling, chasing games, mounting and sexual play, bites and threats, rearing and mock fights, flehmen around mares’ scent, and shadowing breeding bands at a distance. A bachelor may graze peacefully for hours and then leave to trail a mare, try a sneak mating, claim a dispersing filly or challenge a stallion. A male rarely wins a whole harem in one battle. The routes to a band are: recruiting a dispersing young mare , the common route; taking or attracting an adult mare from another band, often around social disruption, a reproductive change or a shift in the stallion’s quality; replacing an old, injured, dead or defeated stallion; building a band gradually, one mare and then more; succeeding the resident in a multi-male band. Over a lifetime a stallion may move repeatedly between bachelor, solitary, subordinate and band-stallion roles. Growing up and leaving Real horses have no “age-up” moment; development is gradual and socially negotiated. The stages that matter: Foal, birth to weaning. Most wild horses breed and foal in spring. A foal stays close to its dam, nurses often, sleeps a lot, and slowly starts grazing and copying adults. Its dam is its attachment, but it learns from the whole band: which adults are safe, how close it may go to each, when the group moves, rests, drinks or flees, and what yielding, ear position, threats, grooming invitations and play limits mean. Foals play hard with peers: chasing, bucking, mock bites and short play fights. Weanling and yearling. Nutritionally more independent, still socially part of the band, building friendships and a rank among the young. BLM counts a horse as an adult from the January after its birth year, which is an administrative line, not social maturity. Colts leave at about one to two years. BLM says one or two; USGS puts it around two, with some staying to two or three if the stallion does not drive them out. The departure can be forced (the stallion threatens, chases, bites and excludes him), voluntary (he ranges farther, takes up with nearby bachelors and stops coming back), or triggered by peers, his maturity, the stallion’s tolerance, band size or a new foal. A maturing son is a future rival and could breed the band’s mares. Once out he may join bachelors, travel with one or two other males, linger near home or be briefly solitary. Fillies leave later , commonly near sexual maturity. They may join another breeding band, be recruited by a bachelor or outside stallion, move after their natal band is disrupted, or occasionally stay, especially if the resident stallion is not their sire. Female dispersal is what keeps bands from inbreeding and links bands together, and it turns bands over without any fight. Adults. A mare usually settles into one or more breeding bands over her life. A male may stay a bachelor, go solitary, be a subordinate, gain one mare, build a larger band, lose it, and go round again. Maturity is not one switch. Physical maturity, social confidence, rank, familiarity with local horses and routes, reproductive state, whether the resident stallion tolerates a young male, and whether there are dispersing females or vacant bands all move separately. Food and water Mustangs spend about half their daylight feeding, and in arid country they keep moving through grazing areas and often travel between forage and scattered water every twelve to twenty-four hours. Most competition is indirect , or exploitative: one horse eats forage another could have eaten, more horses means less per horse, and repeated grazing slows plant recovery. Drought, winter and poor water make the same number of horses functionally more crowded. The cost shows as poorer body condition, lower conception and foaling success, lower foal survival, more walking, crowding at the remaining water and green forage, and more displacements there. Direct competition happens at clumped resources. Within a band, rank decides first access: a dominant mare displaces a subordinate from a patch, a mineral source, or limited shade or forage with pinned ears, a threat, a snake neck, a bite or a chase. The subordinate usually yields. Foals and young horses get displaced for being low-ranked, though their dams can step in. On broad rangeland horses graze side by side without contesting each bite; hunger, crowding, the patch’s value and room to back away all change how much aggression there is. Water is the sharpest pressure point , being more concentrated than forage. Horses queue, approach cautiously, drink at different times or displace each other at a trough, spring, pond or seep. Different bands share the same water, sometimes peacefully and sometimes with brief threats and chases. BLM treats forage, water, cover and space as the four essentials. Each area’s Appropriate Management Level is the population range meant to fit long-term rangeland health; the current year’s foals are left out of that headcount. Home range, not territory A band uses a familiar area year after year, but it is not exclusive. The BLM handbook says bands are not territorial and do not defend preferred habitat; bands graze and water near one another, and horses move between bands. The one exception is brief: USGS documented stallions defending a mineral source while their band was using it, with more aggression as other bands approached. Concept What it means for mustangs Home range A familiar area a band commonly uses over time Core-use area Places visited especially often: preferred water, shelter, minerals or forage Territory An area actively defended against others as exclusive space Mustang reality Overlapping home ranges, not strict territoriality A GPS study in Utah’s Conger and Frisco areas found harems and bachelors choosing herbaceous vegetation, avoiding forest relative to shrubland, and shifting movement and water use by season. Harems had distinct winter ranges, and bachelors moved faster than harems in every season. Snow, heat, water, plant growth, fences, drought and terrain keep changing where horses go and where bands meet. Rank is pairwise, not an alpha Dominance is real but local. Within a band it decides who displaces whom at a feeding spot, who gets the preferred resting place, who drinks first when access is tight, who can start or refuse grooming, which horse the young yield to, and how close horses can come to one another. Age, confidence, familiarity, reproductive state, size and past encounters all feed it, and a mare can outrank the stallion in day-to-day access. His advantage is reproductive control and his willingness to fight outside males, not command over every mare. The useful model is a set of “A yields to B” relationships, not one ladder. When bands meet Two bands may ignore each other while grazing close by, exchange looks and scent, drift apart, let mares and stallions investigate, display, herd or chase if a stallion tries to recruit a mare, or gather for a while at water or forage. Tension between bands is most likely when a mare is in heat, a bachelor presses close, resources are unusually short, or a rival sees a real chance at a mare. Breeding, foaling and paternity Mustangs are female-defence polygynists : the stallion defends access to a set of mares, not a map boundary. That does not make him the sire of every foal in his band. A challenger or bachelor can mate successfully, a takeover can be recent, a mare can change bands while pregnant, a band can hold several males, and mate-guarding is never perfect. A resident stallion tends a mare in heat closely: trailing her, blocking other males, herding her away from a rival, investigating her urine or dung and covering it with his own. Foaling reshapes the band. The mare stays close to and protects the foal, and a newborn’s pace and safety set the band’s movement. Several foals make a peer group and a play network. A new foal crop can coincide with older juveniles drifting to the edge or leaving. In hard conditions, shortage reaches mares and foals first, through body condition and milk. How BLM management shapes all of it BLM range is not an untouched ecosystem. The agency’s stated aim is self-sustaining, healthy populations in balance with the habitat and other uses, managed at the minimum feasible level so free-roaming behaviour survives. Management still changes age structure, sex ratio, density, genetic connectivity, and access to water and forage. Density. Unmanaged populations can double in about four to five years. Over capacity, social behaviour carries on under pressure: more overlap at water and forage, more frequent band encounters, fewer chances for subordinates to avoid dominant horses, worse body condition and breeding, poorer survival in drought or hard winters, and fewer places for dispersers to start a band without conflict. Gathers and removals. Familiar bands can be split; a stallion can lose some mares and keep others; mare-heavy or stallion-heavy remnants form bands differently; young males can join bachelors earlier or later than they would have; a returning horse comes back to a changed social landscape; and removing local animals unevenly breaks genetic and social continuity. A realistic post-gather population reforms, splits, absorbs strangers, replaces stallions and goes through a period of instability, rather than snapping back. Fertility control sits beside gathers and sex-ratio adjustment in the handbook. Fewer foals means fewer future dispersers, a population that ages over time, fewer juvenile peer groups, and possibly changed mare behaviour and stallion tending depending on the method and timing. A day in a band’s life The band grazes for long stretches, spread out but in sight of each other. Mares make most of the ordinary moves; the stallion keeps to the rear or the edge. Foals play and keep returning to their dams. Horses stop to rest, groom, roll and deal with insects. The band walks to water, often by habitual routes. Nearby bands may be in sight or arrive at the same water. Most encounters stay at watching, positioning, sniffing or a brief displacement. If a bachelor approaches a mare in heat or tries to peel a mare away, the stallion becomes much more active: herding, guarding, chasing, possibly fighting. At rest, familiar horses cluster, lower-ranked ones keep a respectful distance, and vigilance passes between adults. The group moves on through its overlapping seasonal range, never patrolling a territory. Forage and water set the ecological limits; affiliation, dominance, mare choice, stallion defence, age and dispersal draw the social map. Sparring is not fighting Horse sparring is ritualised, usually low-to-moderate social fighting, most common among colts, young males and bachelor stallions. It is partly practice, partly rank negotiation, and sometimes a real test of who should yield, but it is not automatically a serious fight. A study of bachelor males found most of their aggressive interactions were sparring, with serious fighting and clearly playful aggression both less common. What a bout is made of A bout builds from posture and testing rather than opening with full-force attacks. The USGS ethogram lists neck wrestling, circling, boxing, parallel prancing, posturing, head-bumping and herding as recognisable parts of male agonistic behaviour. Mutual inspection: sniffing the other horse, urine, dung or a stud pile, often followed by flehmen. High-headed posturing: neck arched, standing tall, muscles stiff, prancing or short deliberate steps. Head bobbing or bowing: two males lowering and raising their heads in rhythm while facing each other. Parallel prancing: shoulder to shoulder in slow, high, elevated strides, necks arched, attention locked. Nipping and muzzle contact at the crest, neck, shoulder, flank, groin, hindquarters or legs. Neck wrestling: each hooking or pressing head and neck against the other’s neck or shoulder, pushing and twisting for leverage. Circling head to tail, nipping at flank, rump, groin and hind legs. Partial mounting , a foreleg or both across the other’s body, which can be sexual, dominance or part of the sequence. Rearing and boxing: both up on their hind legs striking with the forelegs. Dramatic, and far riskier than neck wrestling. Short pursuit: one turns away and the other chases a few strides, which says the first lost that exchange. Play, rank-testing and a real fight Play wrestling and a violent stallion fight can contain the same moves. The difference is context, force, repetition, willingness to disengage, and risk. Chase games, bucking and mock bites become wrestling as males mature. They learn balance, timing, bite placement, controlled rearing, reading faces, and above all how to stop before friction gets expensive. That is why bachelor groups matter: a dispersed colt practises what he will need to recruit and defend mares. Bachelors build a workable order. Sparring lets future rivals keep assessing each other without a damaging fight at every meeting. The result is a fairly stable sense of who yields at close range, who gets a small resource first, who displaces whom, who to avoid, and who is safe to graze or spar beside. Because horses in a local population know each other, many encounters resolve by posture and ritual. Mature males test and advertise. For an adult stallion sparring can be a low-level challenge, an assessment or a warning near mares, advertising condition, confidence, familiarity and willingness to escalate. The stakes climb when a mare is in heat, a bachelor trails a band closely, a challenger tries to peel off a mare, the resident is old, hurt, tired or socially weak, space is tight at water, minerals, shade or food, or a band is already unsettled by a transfer or turnover. A resident stallion usually tries to head off escalation by herding, positioning, threats and chasing before another male reaches a mare. How a bout unfolds Detection and orientation. One horse notices another, stops grazing, raises its head, points its ears and turns toward it. Assessment. They approach directly or in an arc, sniff dung or urine, show flehmen, prance, and show off neck carriage and size. Posture exchange. Nose to nose, head bobs, shoulder bumps, arched necks, stamps and small feints. Contact. Neck wrestling, mutual biting, pushing, circling or partial mounting. Possible escalation. If neither yields: rearing, foreleg strikes, harder bites, short chases. Yield and resolution. One turns away, retreats, lowers its intensity, allows itself to be displaced or leaves; the other may follow briefly, then stop. Back to normal. After low-stakes sparring both may graze on close together. After a serious contest the loser may be kept from a mare, a resource or an area for a while. A horse does not have to be beaten down for a bout to count. A small step away, a turned shoulder, a lowered neck or a decision not to re-engage can settle rank. Sparring among mustangs On BLM range sparring is most common in bachelor bands , among recently dispersed colts and young adult males: rehearsal for adult competition, and a way to manage rank among males who are companions and reproductive rivals at once. A band stallion may spar or fight too, but his behaviour is aimed at protecting access to mares : he keeps between the challenger and the band, herds his mares away, chases the challenger before contact, and escalates only if the rival stays, challenges or reaches a mare. A bachelor who seems to be “just hanging around” is, from the resident’s side, a possible thief, challenger, successor or mating competitor, so even a mild exchange carries reproductive meaning. For example, two two-and-a-half-year-old bachelor colts might start with parallel prancing, take turns nipping each other’s crests, neck-wrestle for twenty seconds, rear once, then break off and graze a few body lengths apart. That is sparring. If an adult bachelor then approaches a mare in a resident stallion’s band and the resident herds her away, charges with pinned ears, bites or strikes, and chases the intruder well past the band, that is a resource-and-reproduction conflict, not practice.
