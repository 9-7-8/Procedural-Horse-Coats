Jumps
(Block / the yard)
A showjumping fence in three styles, and a real obstacle: like a fence, it cannot be stepped over . Place them side by side to widen it and on top of each other to raise it — there is no height setting, because the stack is the height — or set one directly, anywhere from a ground pole to double. One item : its height, its style, its two woods and its two coats of paint are all chosen after you put it down, by right-clicking it. And it is the first thing in the mod that makes a horse’s jump stat something you can watch rather than read.

What it is for A jump is one rail across the block with an upright standard at each end of the run. Put two beside each other and they become one continuous rail with a post at either extremity, the way a real fence line looks; the standards in the middle disappear on their own, so you never have to think about it. Recipe (shapeless): 3 vanilla fences of one wood + 1 horse hair 4 jumps of that wood . There is one jump item. The wood rides on the stack, which is why an oak jump and a birch jump are two stacks with different names rather than two items; the style is not on the item at all. Three fences of mixed woods do not craft — put the jump down and swap a half in its screen instead. Four to a hair on purpose. A course is the one thing you build dozens of, and a hair is sheared one to three at a time, once per horse per day — so a twelve-fence course costs three hairs, which is an afternoon with one horse rather than a reason not to build. Three styles, two woods and two coats of paint Every jump is one of three shapes. None of them is more than one block deep — real courses put ground poles in front of an oxer, and we deliberately do not, because a jump that occupies two blocks stops being a thing you put in a row and starts being a structure. Style What it is Vertical One rail. The plain fence, and what a jump starts as. Oxer Two rails with a spread, front and back, with wider standards to hold them. Crossrails Two poles crossed in an X, lowest in the middle — the shape you school a young horse over. One crossed pair spans up to three blocks : build a row of them and you get one wide X, not a row of little ones. Right-click a placed jump and it opens. Two slots for the rails and the standards , a button per style, and a height you step up and down. Everything about a jump that is not its position is changed here, which is why there is only one item. A plank in a slot re-woods that half. One plank is spent and a plank of the wood it used to be comes straight back to you , so re-dressing a built course costs nothing at all and you can put it back exactly as it was. Shift-click a stack of two or more and both halves change at once. A dye in a slot paints that half — and the dye is gone for good. The paint is a wash over the wood, not a replacement for it: the colour reads as the colour you chose, and the grain and shading of whatever wood is underneath still show through it. The only way to strip paint is a fresh plank in that half , which pops the old plank out and takes the paint with it. So a coat of paint is a decision and a change of wood is not — you can always get a jump back to bare wood, and you can never get the dye back. Height and style both apply to the whole fence , not to the block you clicked — a fence has a height and is one kind of fence; each block of it is neither. To build a mixed or stepped line on purpose, break the run and set the halves separately. Ten rungs, from 0.1 (a ground pole) to 2.0 (double), and the screen shows both the height and what a horse has to clear. The style and height buttons are free. Right-clicking a jump opens the window whatever is in your hand , another jump included — sneak to place instead, the same thing you already do to put a block against a chest. Style changes nothing about the jumping. All three collide identically, so a stack of three is the same fence whichever shape you build it from. That is on purpose — see why style is cosmetic . Height is a measuring stick A jump is an eighth of a block taller to clear than it looks . It stops you like a fence does, and a sliver of it is invisible — without that a horse simply steps over the rail, because a horse steps a full block without breaking stride. That sliver does not scale , which is what makes the whole range work without a special case. A jump is height + ⅛ blocks to clear, so what you have to clear is never far from what you can see: Height To clear What it is 0.1 0.25 blocks A ground pole — schooling, not an obstacle 0.5 0.625 A trot pole. Everything under a block is walked over and everything from a block up has to be jumped — a rule you can read off the fence rather than one you learn by failing at it. And you can still stack. A jump on top of a jump adds its own height to the one below, so the range above 2 is reached the way it always was. You cannot jump one on foot , any more than you can jump a fence, so a course is something you walk around and ride through. That is deliberate and it is the vanilla behaviour. The point is not the number. It is that two horses that look identical on a stat screen are separated by a fence one of them stops at, which is the whole argument for the mod being about breeding rather than about rolling. Is it dangerous? No. A jump is a fence: a horse that refuses it stops, and one that hits it takes nothing. It has no effect on a horse’s health, its condition or its bond , and failing one costs nothing but the attempt. Building a course Facing is set when you place it, from the way you are looking, and the rail runs across that. A row placed while walking sideways comes out straight. A jump placed on top of a jump takes the facing of the one below , so a stack lines up however you walk around it while building. And its style — from the jump below it, or failing that from either jump beside it along the rail. There is one item, so a new jump has to get its style from somewhere, and continuing the run you are building is the answer that needs no clicks. The first jump of a course is the only one you have to dress. A single jump is already a fence. There is no trot-pole height — the lowest thing you can build is one a horse has to leave the ground for. A jump facing north and one facing south are the same fence , so they join up. You do not have to approach a row from a consistent side to build it. Jumps join to jumps and to nothing else — they will not connect to a fence, a double gate or a wall. A run is posted every three blocks , with an upright at the join rather than a T through the middle of a block. So three in a row has uprights at its two ends and nothing between them; six has one more, where its two halves meet. A crossrail run makes one X up to three blocks wide , and the poles get shallower as it widens. Three in a row is always one three-wide X; a run of five is a three and a two; a run of seven is a three, a three and a single. The two poles sit at slightly different depths and pass each other, the way real ones rest in their cups. And only to jumps of the same style. An oxer beside a vertical is two obstacles, not one fence, so each keeps its own standards. They are genuinely different shapes — an oxer's rails are front and back where a vertical's is down the middle, and its standards are deeper to hold them — so sharing a standard left an oxer's two rails running into thin air with nothing holding them up. An axe is the right tool, and a jump drops its own style — break an oxer, get an oxer back. The jump lives in the horse tab with the carrots and the papers, as one oak item . It was filed into vanilla’s Building Blocks for one build, beside each wood’s fence, on the same argument the double gates use — and the owner’s call was that the argument does not carry: a gate is a building block horses happen to care about, and a jump is horse equipment, useless without one. It then had a tab of its own for a day, back when there were thirty-six of them. There is one now, and a tab holding one thing is worse than a row. Every wood has one, including woods other mods added — those are generated at run time from that mod’s own plank texture, so they arrive looking right without anybody drawing anything. See compatibility . What it is not, yet Three styles is not a course designer’s vocabulary. A wall is the next one, and the thing that was blocking it is now gone: “wall” means cobblestone, brick, stone brick — a material axis that is not the twelve woods — and a jump now carries its material as data rather than being one block per wood, so a second axis costs a list and some models rather than another dozen blocks. Also waiting on nothing but the work: crossrails spanning up to three blocks as one crossed pair rather than a row of separate X’s, and half and third height jumps as walkable ground poles. Beyond that, cross-country wants logs, brush, ditches, fillers and water, and none of it is built. A warning for whoever builds the short ones. Stacked clearances do not add on a block grid. A third-height jump on top of a full one clears exactly what the full one did, because the obstacle is the block column, not the sum of the rails in it. The height ladder below only reads honestly while every jump is one block. None of it costs an item any more , which is the point of there being one. Style, wood and paint are all chosen in the screen, so a new style is a fourth button rather than a thirty-seventh item, and the roster stops growing however much vocabulary the courses gain. Nothing scores a round, times one, or notices that a horse knocked a rail, because a jump is scenery with a collision box. The Roadmap tab is where that lives.
