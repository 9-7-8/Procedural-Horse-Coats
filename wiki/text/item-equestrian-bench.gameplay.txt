Tack Dyeing Bench
(Block / tack)
A saddler’s bench. It dyes a saddle’s seat , its bridle and its metal fittings separately, does the same three-way job on leather horse armour , and one day will cut a banner into a saddle pad. Two horses need not look alike.

What it does Recipe (shapeless): 1 crafting table + 1 leather + 2 horse hair. There is also one already standing at every cowboy’s homestead , at the far end of the frontage, past the hitch and the four posts — so you are likely to meet one before you know to want it. The recipe means you never have to go back there for it. Right-click it and you get a saddle slot, a name field, and three labelled rows — one per part. Put a saddle in, drop a dye or an ingot into whichever rows you want to change, and the recoloured saddle appears on the right. Take it and the saddle is spent, along with only the materials that actually changed something. An empty row means “leave this one alone” , not “no colour”. Recolouring just the bridle costs one dye and leaves the seat and the fittings exactly as they were. Seat — the leather you sit on. Takes any of the sixteen dyes — and any dye another mod added, including the odd ones whose colour vanilla has no name for. Bridle — the headstall, noseband, bit and reins. Also a dye, and independent of the seat : a black saddle with a red bridle is two visits, not a compromise. Fittings — the buckles and rings. Takes a material rather than a dye, which is the whole character of the row: you are not painting the hardware, you are saying what it is made of. Fifteen of vanilla’s, plus every ingot and gem any other mod added . The fifteen hand-picked fittings Material Reads as Iron ingot Plain steel — and the way back to undyed Gold ingot Brass Copper ingot Warm orange Redstone Red Emerald Green Prismarine shard Pale sea-green Ender pearl Deep teal Diamond A deliberately loud cyan Lapis lazuli True blue Amethyst shard Purple Nether quartz Clean white Bone Aged cream Basalt Cool blue-grey stone Netherite ingot Dark warm gunmetal Coal Black Three of those pairs sit close together, and were pulled apart on purpose. Basalt and netherite are both near-black, bone and quartz are both near-white, and prismarine and ender pearl are both blue-greens. Each was moved away from its material’s own average colour rather than set to it — basalt cooler and lighter, bone creamier and darker, ender pearl deeper than prismarine. Two fittings a player cannot tell apart on a horse are worth one option, not two. Diamond is the loud one deliberately. It was a pale blue-white at first and read as washed-out pewter; spending diamonds on tack that does nothing whatever except look expensive ought to look expensive. Bone, not bone meal Bone meal carries a dye component, so it would satisfy the seat and bridle rows as well — a single item meaning two different things in a bench whose entire premise is leather-or-material. The bone itself has no such ambiguity. Coal is not #000000 , and that is a trap rather than a taste call The colours here feed getArmorLayerTintColor , and returning zero from that hook tells the renderer to skip the layer altogether. Pure black would therefore not give you black hardware; it would give you no hardware. Coal is a near-black with a slight cool cast, which reads identically and cannot trip it. And whatever your other mods brought The fifteen are no longer the whole list. Any item another mod files under the c:ingots/* or c:gems/* common tags is a fitting too, coloured from its own item art — so a pack with a tin, a silver and a ruby in it has three more fittings, with nothing to install and nothing to switch on. The tooltip names the material rather than the item, so a modded fitting reads “tin” the way the fifteen above read “brass”. The fifteen win on a clash, and that is the important half. Several of them were deliberately moved away from their material’s own average colour — basalt cooler, bone creamier, ender pearl deeper — precisely so that two fittings a player cannot tell apart are not both offered. A modded copper that averaged to its own true colour would quietly redefine copper and undo that, so anything already on the list above answers first and the modded lookup is only ever a fallback. The colours themselves are read off the art because there is nowhere else an ingot’s colour is written down . Nothing pulls a modded fitting away from its neighbour. The hand-picked fifteen were separated by eye, one at a time; a generated one is simply the average of its own picture. So a pack with several similar metals in it can perfectly well end up with two fittings that look the same on a horse — which is the exact thing the fifteen were tuned to avoid, and there is no equivalent tuning pass available for materials nobody has seen. It has not been looked at in a game either way. What the wheel still lacks: pink and magenta, and probably for good. Blue and black were the real holes and lapis and coal fill them. But no vanilla material is pink, and the only way to reach it would be admitting dyes into the fittings row — which would cost that row the thing that makes it interesting. You are not painting the hardware, you are saying what it is made of, and nothing the world is made of is pink. Naming a saddle The field across the top names the saddle — Daisy’s Saddle — and the name travels with it, so a tack room full of saddles is a tack room you can read. Naming costs nothing and can be done in the same pass as dyeing, or on its own. What it costs One material per part you actually change. Dye all three rows at once and it costs three materials and one pass; change one and it costs one. The bench will not charge you for a part that is already the colour you asked for, and a dye will not colour metal nor an ingot leather — in either case no result appears at all rather than a material quietly vanishing. Reading a saddle you already own Hover it. The tooltip names all three parts — “Seat: red, Bridle: black, Fittings: gold” — by dye and metal name rather than as hex codes. That matters because the icon can only show one colour , and it shows the seat: the largest area, and the one people mean when they say what colour a saddle is. Two things it politely refuses. A dye will not colour metal and an ingot will not colour leather, so the result stays empty until the material suits the part you picked. And if the part is already that colour nothing appears either — the bench will not take a dye for doing nothing. Putting iron in is how you put the fittings back to plain steel , because plain steel is what iron means here. Nothing is eaten if you change your mind Close the screen and whatever is still in the slots comes back to you. Only taking the result spends anything. An undyed saddle is exactly the saddle you know The bench works by colouring three layers that were cut out of vanilla’s own saddle texture, and a saddle nobody has dyed carries no colour at all — so it renders pixel for pixel as it always has. That matters beyond the horse: donkeys, mules, skeleton horses and zombie horses wear the same saddle, and none of them changed. Camels, pigs, striders and nautiluses were left alone entirely. It does leather horse armour too Put leather horse armour in the top-left slot instead of a saddle and the bench works exactly the same way — except that the three row labels change , because a caparison has no seat and no bridle: Blanket — the broad sheet over the body. This is the part vanilla has always let you dye. Trim — the brown strapwork and the saddlebags. Fittings — the buckles and bars, from the same list of materials as a saddle’s: the fifteen plus whatever your other mods brought . Iron, gold, diamond and netherite horse armour are not accepted, and that is deliberate rather than an oversight: none of them is dyeable, so taking them would only promise a result that never appears. The generated horse armours for other mods’ metals are not accepted either, for the same reason — each one already is its metal’s colour, and there is no second zone on it to dye. The crafting-grid recipe still works, and still does only the blanket You can keep dyeing leather horse armour the way you always have, in a crafting grid with dyes. That path colours the blanket alone , exactly as it did before this bench existed — the trim stays brown and the fittings stay steel. That took deliberate work, because the obvious implementation makes vanilla worse rather than merely different. Vanilla reads a stack’s dye once and hands the same value to every dyeable layer, so simply adding trim and fitting layers would have made a grid-dyed armour turn one flat colour over straps and buckles alike. Those two layers are pinned to their own undyed constants whenever no bench colour is present. The bench is the only thing that can colour them , which is the distinction worth having: the grid is the crude tool and the bench the fine one. The two tools compose, in that order. Bring an armour you dyed in a grid years ago, change only its trim, and the blanket keeps the colour you gave it. The bench reads an untouched armour’s existing dyed_color rather than assuming brown — without that, editing one row would have written the blanket back to plain leather and thrown your dye away without a word about it. Not yet: the saddle pad, and cutting a banner into one. Those are saddle-pad roadmap , along with the reasoning behind every decision here.
