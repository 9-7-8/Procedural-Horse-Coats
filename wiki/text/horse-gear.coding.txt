Horse gear: the nineteen slots
(Gameplay / non-genetic)
Everything a horse wears, in one place. A Gear tab on the horse information screen holds nineteen slots laid out on a picture of the horse itself — head, mane and tail, body, chest, a slot per leg and a slot per hoof, and two for saddlebags. Two of them are real vanilla equipment slots and the other seventeen are a data attachment of our own, which is the constraint the whole system is shaped by. The slots exist; almost nothing to put in them does yet. The roster, the storage and the screen shipped first on purpose — every piece that follows is one row in a tag. What is still planned is on this page’s own Roadmap tab, which is the single home for gear plans.

The two-slot ceiling This is the constraint the whole system is shaped by. EquipmentSlot has exactly two animal entries — BODY and SADDLE — and EquipmentClientInfo.LayerType is not extensible. Nineteen slots were therefore never going to be nineteen equipment slots. The two real ones are spent on the saddle and the barding, which are the two vanilla already draws for free; every other piece is a key in a synced data attachment. The roster is the layout entity/HorseTackSlot is the whole table. Each constant carries its backing (an EquipmentSlot , or null for a gear slot), its zone, its label, its one-line hint, and its position on the paper doll as a fraction of the doll’s box. That last part is what keeps the screen out of it: adding a slot is a line in the enum plus a tag, and the screen is not touched. isVanilla() is the difference between the two backings, and nothing outside the enum should need to ask — on() and set() cover both. usableOn(horse) is vanilla’s canUseSlot for the two real slots and “not a foal” for the rest. accepts(horse, stack) asks vanilla for the two real slots and asks the slot’s item tag for the rest. What a slot accepts is a tag Every gear slot reads an item tag named for it — horsegenetics:gear/mane , one tag per non-vanilla slot, seventeen of them. All seventeen are empty. A slot whose tag is empty correctly refuses everything rather than accepting dirt, which is exactly the behaviour wanted while the pieces do not exist. Shipping a piece means adding it to its tag, and a datapack can do the same without touching Java. Why an attachment and not a container data/HorseGear is the store: an immutable map of slot name to ItemStack , registered as HORSE_GEAR in ModAttachments , serialized, synced and copyOnDeath . A container was the other option and is worse on two counts: A widened container is reachable by hoppers. An attachment is not. AbstractMountInventoryMenu.quickMoveStack is shared with donkeys, mules, llamas and nautiluses, and computes every shift-click range from hardcoded indices. A slot inserted anywhere in that arithmetic is how items duplicate or vanish, and it is the failure that looks fine until somebody shift-clicks with a chest on the horse. This is also the shape vanilla itself uses: the saddle and armour slots are not backed by the horse’s container either — Mob.createEquipmentSlotContainer fronts the equipment array with a view that owns no storage. One authority, and every stack is copied The attachment is the only place a worn piece lives and is never mirrored into a container. Every stack crossing the boundary is copied, so a caller holding the stack it passed in cannot mutate what the horse is wearing, and the map is unmodifiable — every change returns a new HorseGear . Keyed by name, not by ordinal The same reason TackSlotPayload sends the name: an ordinal in saved data silently re-points at a different slot the moment the enum gains a constant in the middle, and this enum is expected to keep growing. An unknown key is kept rather than dropped, so a piece parked behind a removed slot is still there if the slot comes back. The click path Unchanged in shape from the two-slot version, and still one packet. HorseInfoScreen.clickTack sends TackSlotPayload(entityId, slotName) ; ModNetworking.handleTackSlot checks the horse is the player’s and within eight blocks, then calls HorseTackSlot.set — the one place that knows which backing a slot has. A vanilla slot also gets setGuaranteedDrop , so tack a player put on is tack they get back. The paper doll Cursor.gear draws the horse first and the slots over it, each at anchorX/anchorY of the doll box, and records where each landed in tackHits rather than working the geometry out twice — which is what keeps a scrolled page’s slots clickable in the right place. The horse is HorsePortrait.drawPosed , a new fixed-angle companion to the mouse-following draw . The doll’s horse has to hold still : its slots are positioned anatomically, so a portrait that swung to follow the cursor would put the near fore leg somewhere different from the boot slot that belongs to it. The pose is a three-quarter view because face-on overlaps the near and off legs, and four boot slots would then point at one visible leg. The roster checks itself at boot HorseTackSlot.verify() runs from the mod constructor, beside DietFoods.verify and for the same reason: every way this table can be wrong fails silently in the game. Two slots given the same anchor draw exactly on top of each other, so one is unclickable and the tab merely looks like it has one slot fewer. An anchor outside 0..1 puts a slot off the doll, invisible but still holding its click box. A third constant handed a real EquipmentSlot would take one the saddle or the barding needs. None of that throws on its own and none of it logs. Why it is not a unit test It cannot be. This module’s test source set has no Minecraft on its classpath on purpose — see MaterialScanTest , which imports none — and HorseTackSlot cannot even be loaded without one, since its fields are EquipmentSlot and TagKey . A boot-time throw is the cheapest check that actually runs. Nothing is drawn on the horse yet There is no render layer for any of the seventeen gear slots, because there is nothing to render. The saddle and the barding draw as they always have, through vanilla. Every piece past those two needs a render layer of our own — the pattern this mod already has ten instances of — and the plan for deriving that geometry rather than modelling it by hand is on the Roadmap tab.
