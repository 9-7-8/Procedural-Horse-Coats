Jumps
(Block / the yard)
A showjumping fence in three styles, and a real obstacle: like a fence, it cannot be stepped over . Place them side by side to widen it and on top of each other to raise it — there is no height setting, because the stack is the height — or set one directly, anywhere from a ground pole to double. One item : its height, its style, its two woods and its two coats of paint are all chosen after you put it down, by right-clicking it. And it is the first thing in the mod that makes a horse’s jump stat something you can watch rather than read.

Stacking, and the height that was added beside it This section used to argue against a settable height, and it was overruled. The argument was that a height state makes the jump assert a height, and that a block has no business knowing one — stacking makes height a consequence of geometry instead, so the horse is answering a question about the world rather than about a number somebody typed into a blockstate. It also cost nothing, and the ladder came out evenly spaced for free because blocks are one block tall. Two things were wrong with it. The first is that a one-block granularity is a bad instrument : the interesting part of the range for a real horse sits between nothing and one block, and a ladder whose bottom rung is “a fence most horses clear” cannot see into it at all. The second is that it rules out the ground pole , which is not a measurement at all — it is the thing you school a young horse over, and it has to be walkable. So height is a settable ladder of ten now, and stacking still works on top of it . The honest part of the original argument survives intact: the jump still asserts nothing about the horse . It is a number about the obstacle, the horse either clears it or does not, and the reading is the same reading. What changed is only the resolution of the ruler. The cost is real and is worth knowing. A height is a scale on baked geometry, and there is no per-position transform on a BlockStateModelPart — so every rung is a complete second copy of every part , baked with a scaling ModelState composed into it. Ten heights is ten times the bake work. That is why it is a ladder of ten chosen heights and not a slider, and why the rungs are fine below one block and coarse above it. It is also why the height lives on the block entity rather than in the blockstate: ten values as a property would have multiplied every state in the file by ten. The shape reads it per position instead, which is what vanilla’s shulker box does for the same reason. Why style is cosmetic An oxer is a harder fence than a vertical in life, and it would have been easy to make one here. It is deliberately not. The height ladder is the mod’s one honest reading of a horse’s jump genetics, and it stays readable only while a stack of three means the same thing whatever it is built from . A style that jumped differently would make the measurement depend on decoration, and you would no longer be able to compare two horses without also comparing the fences you happened to build for them. So difficulty lives in the one axis that is honest about itself — how high you stack it — and style is what the course looks like. That also means a rider can rebuild a course to taste without changing what it tests. What a stack can actually measure This matters more than a decoration has any right to. Nothing else in the mod makes jump visible : the debug yard’s stat pens measure it by printing Attributes.JUMP_STRENGTH , which is reading the answer rather than asking the question. And there is a claim waiting to be checked. common/trait/HorseUnits.jumpMetres converts jump strength to metres through a cubic — it is what the info screen prints as “x.x m” — and its own class doc admits the fit is unverified against this mod’s horses , assuming scale 1.0 and normal gravity. A stack of jumps is the instrument that can finally test it: breed for a known strength, build the ladder, and see which rung the horse stops at. If the cubic is right, the rung and the metres agree. That is a genuine experiment the mod could not previously run, and it is the reason this family was worth building before the prettier obstacle types. Why the art is ours Three existing Minecraft projects cover this ground — DragN’s XC Jumps, EquestrianMod’s obstacles, and the Showjumping Craft CIT pack. All three are All Rights Reserved for their assets , and the one that splits its licences (MIT code, reserved art) reserves precisely the half that would have been worth having. The author policy behind the first is explicit that models and textures may not go into a standalone mod; the exceptions are texture packs and dependent addons, which we are not. Our own licence makes it worse rather than better: this mod is CC BY-NC 4.0 and its jars go to outside testers, so shipping someone’s reserved art would mean sublicensing it, which needs written permission and not a friendly nod. So the geometry here is drawn from the real-world object. A showjumping vertical is a rail between two standards; that is not anyone’s invention, and building it from the same vocabulary is clear.
