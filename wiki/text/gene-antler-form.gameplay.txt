Antler form
(Magical gene / dominance series / shapes the antlers, paints nothing)
How an antlered horse's rack grows: forked like a red deer's, swept back with a brow tine like a reindeer's, broad and palmate like a moose's, crowned with a cup of tines, or a plain spike. One habit, drawn on both sides. On a horse without antlers it does nothing at all.

Crossing two of them The five habits Allele Habit Looks like Pal Palmate A short beam opening into a broad palm, with points round its edge — a moose, a fallow buck Crn Crowned A tall beam ending in a cup of tines splayed every way — a royal head Brw Brow-tined Swept back and over in a long curve, one tine thrown forward over the face — a reindeer Frk Forked Branched up the beam, the lower tines longest — a red deer. The commonest n Spike A plain spike, forking once when it is large — a yearling, a brocket Each allele hides the ones below it: a horse with Pal and anything else is palmate, one with Brw and Frk is brow-tined. So two forked horses can throw a spike-antlered foal, one time in four, if both carry n underneath. Finding one Every horse carries a form, antlered or not — it simply does nothing until the horse has antlers. About half of wild copies are forked, a fifth are spike, and the other three habits are rarer. That makes an ordinary-looking horse a reasonable way to bring a palmate rack into an antlered line. Is it good for the horse? It does nothing to the horse at all, for good or ill — it only changes the shape of antlers it might have. Its gene carrot
