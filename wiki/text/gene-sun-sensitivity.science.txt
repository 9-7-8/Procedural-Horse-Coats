Sun sensitivity
(Magical gene / behaviour)
Two copies and daylight burns the horse. Caught under open sky by day it runs for the nearest deep cover or water — jumping a fence if it can — and stays there until dark. One copy shows nothing at all.

Horses really do burn Sunburn is common on pink-skinned horses — white faces and socks, cremellos, heavily white paints — because unpigmented skin has nothing to absorb ultraviolet light. That is a coat question, not a gene of its own. Photosensitisation is different and worse: something in the blood reacts to sunlight and damages the skin from within. In horses it is almost always acquired rather than inherited — eating plants such as St John's wort or buckwheat, or liver damage that lets a chlorophyll breakdown product (phylloerythrin) build up. Affected horses seek shade on their own, which is the one piece of real behaviour this gene borrows. The inherited version belongs to other animals and to people: the porphyrias , a family of mostly recessive enzyme defects in which light-reactive compounds accumulate in the skin. Congenital erythropoietic porphyria turns up in cattle and cats, stains the teeth red, and has been suggested — not convincingly — as a root of the vampire legend. A heritable recessive that makes a horse burn in daylight is fantasy; the mechanism it would need is not.
