Gameplay / non-genetic

Horse care: gated healing, bond & herds

Three non-genetic systems that apply to every horse and share one slow tick (server/HorseCareHandler): an invisible hunger that pays for healing beside water, a per-horse bond number that changes how a horse behaves toward its owner, and an emergent herd grouping. First slice built 2026-09-02, not play-tested — checklist in the Verification tab. This page is the single source of truth for the machinery; what is still missing is on the roadmap tab.

1. Hunger and healing

Every horse has a hunger level you cannot see. It drops slowly on its own — an ordinary-sized horse is hungry after about one Minecraft day and starving after about two — and a hurt horse spends it to heal. A horse that has eaten heals faster than horses used to, and a starving horse cannot heal at all. Hunger never hurts a horse, and nothing else about the horse depends on it.

Big horses get hungry sooner. The drain follows the horse’s body, so a shire empties in about two thirds of a day where a falabella takes two and a half, and a yard of draught horses wants feeding noticeably more often than a yard of ponies. It is simply proportional — twice the horse, twice the drain.

  • Where it heals. A hurt horse heals when there is water within 3 blocks (#horsegenetics:horse_water — water, bubble_column, water_cauldron — or any block holding water) and it is not starving: 1 health a second, 2 in a herd, and every point costs a little hunger. It no longer needs food beside it, because it goes and eats instead.
  • No free healing. Vanilla gives every horse one point about every 45 seconds whatever state it is in. That is switched off for horses, so a starving horse really stays hurt. Donkeys, mules and llamas keep it.
  • When it eats. Once hungry, a horse looks up to 10 blocks round for food and eats until it is nearly full. A horse that is not hungry yet but has eaten into its reserve (below three quarters full) takes a mouthful about once every two minutes: food dropped within a couple of blocks of it if there is any, otherwise the grass it is standing on.
  • What it looks for, best first: its favourite food lying on the ground; any dropped food it will eat; cake; hay bales; crops, pumpkins, melons and sugar cane; grass; moss; mushrooms; flowers. The nearest of the best kind wins.
  • Dropped food is judged by what it grazes, not by what it takes from a hand. An ordinary horse picks up crops, fruit, vegetables, berries, mushrooms, small flowers and cake — and the same things from other mods, so a modded grain or fruit is food without this mod having heard of it. Hand-feeding is unchanged and stays vanilla's shorter list.
  • Eating uses food up. Cake, hay bales, crops, mushrooms and flowers are eaten whole, and grass and moss turn to dirt, as they do under a sheep. With mob griefing off the horse is still fed and the block stays. A hay bale in a pen does not last.
  • Hand-feeding heals exactly as before, needs no water, and feeds the horse too — its favourite food most.
  • A healing horse gives off a couple of heart particles, and heals toward its own max health.
Diet changes what counts as food

A horse with a narrow diet only goes for what it can eat: a wheat-eater takes hay and wheat, a vegetable-eater carrots, potatoes, beetroot, pumpkins and melons, a cake-eater cake, and none of them graze. A carnivore with nothing to eat hunts cows, pigs, sheep, chickens and rabbits and then eats what they drop, and a fish-eater hunts fish. A blood-drinker never regenerates: its bite is its meal and its heal. A horse that cannot be fed never goes looking.

Not built yet

Milking — the other half of the original design — is not in this slice. When it lands, its "mare must be at full health" rule is the hook back into this system.

2. Bond

Every tier needs a way to get there — a sight line is not enough. Before it looks at or walks toward its owner, the horse asks the navigator for a real path and drops the whole behaviour if there is none, so a horse shut in a pen whose owner is outside the fence behaves like any other horse: it grazes, spars, grooms and answers a mare in heat instead of standing at the rails.

Two goals rather than one, because what a goal claims matters as much as what it does: tier 1 is head-turning, so it claims LOOK alone and sits below band life, while tiers 2–3 claim MOVE and sit above it. Both read the number and return false from canUse() below their tier, rather than being added and removed as the number crosses a line, and both are inert while the horse is leashed or ridden.

Gains — capped at +15 per Minecraft day

Riding bareback bonds exactly as well as riding saddled. This is worth saying because the obvious implementation gets it wrong: vanilla only regards a rider as the horse's controlling passenger once the horse is saddled, so the natural test (getControllingPassenger() == owner) quietly pays nothing for a bareback rider, and the two cases look identical from the saddle. They are not identical in what they mean. Bareback is how you talk a wild horse round in the first place, and it is the least equipment-mediated thing you can do with an animal — a system that rewards you for buying leather first has the relationship backwards. The gate is hasPassenger.

Hearts show whenever the bond actually moves, and stop when it cannot. They are emitted from awardBond — the one function that knows whether a point was really granted — so every path that bonds a horse gets the feedback without asking, and a horse that has hit the daily cap goes quiet. That silence is the only signal a player has that the cap exists.

A foal born to a tamed dam starts at a quarter of her bond. Starting every foal at zero made a horse born in your own stable, to your own mare, exactly as wary of you as one caught wild that morning — which is backwards. A quarter rather than all of it, because the bond is still the foal’s own to earn: this is the head start of having been born to somebody who trusts you, not an inheritance. It is written straight onto the attachment rather than through awardBond, whose job is metering against a daily cap a well-bonded dam would blow straight through.

A "day" is 24 000 game ticks (DAY_TICKS); dayStamp = getGameTime() / 24000, monotonic and reload-safe. bondToday resets when it rolls over. This answers the once-per-Minecraft-day rule for the bond side.

Taming by hand

Crouch, hold out something the horse eats, look at it and keep still. It comes over, eats out of your hand, and each mouthful is one roll at taming — the same roll as being thrown off its back. Deliberately the same odds: this is a second door into taming, not an easier one, and each feed costs an item the way each mount costs a fall.

All four conditions are re-tested every tick, so breaking the pose stops the horse mid-walk, which is what teaches the mechanic. Crouching is the gesture and the thing that stops it firing whenever somebody walks past a herd holding wheat; looking picks which horse without needing a click; and stillness is checked against where you stood when it set off, so walking at the animal ends the approach and a little shuffling does not.

What counts as food is a genetic question rather than a vanilla one — a carnivore is not coming over for a carrot, and a blood-drinker is not coming over at all. Foals are left out for the same reason vanilla leaves them out of riding. See server/CrouchFeedGoal.

Horses come to food — and run for their favourite

Any horse drifts toward a player holding something it can eat (server/FoodTemptGoal), tamed or wild, foal or adult. No crouch, no gesture: hold food and horses take an interest. It is deliberately a drift — well under walking pace, it stops a couple of blocks short rather than pressing into you, and it ends the instant the food goes away, so a player who keeps walking simply leaves. A horse should be interested in food, not glued to it.

Its favourite food is the exception, and it runs. Twice the notice radius and more than twice the speed, so it picks its head up from across the paddock and comes at a gallop. That gap is the point of the feature: it is the only way the preference locus is visible before you have fed it. Until now you had to already know which of the favourites a horse carried in order to try it; now the horse tells you, by reacting to one item in a way it reacts to nothing else.

What counts as edible is genetic, the same test the crouch-feed goal uses — a carnivore does not come over for a carrot. A favourite overrides the diet, exactly as it does on the interaction, so a meat-eater with a taste for apples still runs at one. A ridden, leashed or led horse ignores food entirely, because all three mean somebody else is already deciding where it goes.

A saddle buys you steering, and steering is what lets you go far enough for the proximity gain and the riding gain to stack for any length of time. So a saddle is worth having; it is simply not what the horse is counting — and at the top bond tier it is not even required (below).

Bareback steering — what the top tier is for

At behaviour tier 3 (bond 81+), a horse steers without tack. Mount it bareback and it answers the reins as though saddled; drop below the tier and it stops. That is the payoff at the top of the bond ladder, and it is the one bond reward a player can feel rather than read: everything else the tiers do is the horse moving toward you on its own.

It works by lending the horse a phantom saddle for as long as the rider qualifies, because vanilla decides who may steer from inside the saddle slot and there is no other hook — server/BarebackSteeringHandler puts it on and takes it straight back off. Three consequences worth knowing: it is a real saddle while it is there, so the only thing between this and an item duplicator is the sweep that removes it (gap 156) — and now also the check in a bolting horse, which takes a saddle off and must not hand that one over; a horse whose bond falls loses the ability mid-ride rather than at a tidy moment; and a horse that bolts stops steering bare too, because the lender stands down for as long as it is running.

3. Wild bands

Wild horses live the way free-roaming horses really do (the science tab has the research): not in one herd with a leader, but in small bands that change over time. Nothing here has been played yet — see the verification tab.

Who is who

  • Family bands have one band stallion, his mares and their young. The band follows its lead mare — the oldest mare — while the stallion keeps to the edge.
  • Bachelor bands are stallions without mares, following one of their own.
  • Open a horse’s information screen and its Social section shows its role, where it stands among its band-mates, its closest companions and any rival, by name.

What you will see

  • Herding. A mare that wanders off gets driven back by her stallion, who comes round behind her with his ears pinned (angry particles over his head).
  • Guarding. An outside stallion who comes near a band finds its stallion standing between him and the mares — and if he keeps coming, a short chase.
  • Sparring. Colts and stallions who know each other square up, rear and shove for a few seconds until one gives way and backs off. It never does damage; it decides who yields to whom.
  • Pushing in at food and water. A horse at a hay bale, a trough or a pond gets shouldered off by a horse that outranks it. No kicks.
  • Mothers and foals. A foal stays near its mother, she goes to it if it strays, and she goes for anything that hurts it.
  • Grooming partners — usually mares — now and then stand head to tail together.

How bands change

  • Growing up and leaving. A colt leaves the band he was born in a day or three after he grows up, and joins a bachelor band. A filly leaves later, three to five days on, for another family band — or with a bachelor, who then has a band of his own.
  • Takeovers. Now and then a bachelor challenges a band stallion. It is a real fight, but it stops short: the first to fall below about 40% of his health breaks off and runs, so nobody dies of it. A winning challenger takes the band; the loser becomes a bachelor.
  • A band outlives its stallion. If he dies, the band stays together behind its lead mare until a bachelor claims it.
  • Mares move. Occasionally a mare drifts to a nearby band where she already knows the mares.
  • Tamed horses keep their friendships, sparring, grooming and mothering — but never leave, get taken over or get stolen.

Tamed herds

Natural wild herds (2026-09-05)

Wild horses spawn into a natural herd right away — a traditional or bachelor band built by server/HerdManager, not the together-timer below. Its members share a lead (HorseCareAttachment.herd = the lead horse's UUID), a breed (herdBreed) and a band (herdBand). The together-timer herd formation here skips any horse that is inWildHerd(), so it only ever applies to tamed horses now.

  • Each scan, a horse looks for another live horse within 10 blocks. togetherTicks accumulates +30 while company is present (capped at 2 × TICKS_TO_FORM_HERD) and decays −30 while alone.
  • Crossing 12 000 ticks (ten minutes) with no herd yet: the horse joins a nearby horse's herd if one has an id, otherwise it mints a new UUID and pulls in every herdless neighbour that is itself at least half-way to the threshold.
  • togetherTicks decaying back to 0 while in a herd → the horse leaves it (clears its id).
  • Alpha is not stored. The oldest / highest-bond member is computed on demand by whatever needs it, with an explicit tiebreak, so it can't flicker.
  • Herd comfort buff: restated as faster regen (the mod has no stamina resource). A horse in a herd heals +2 per scan instead of +1 when the water/food gate is met.

4. When one of your horses is hurt

Your horses tell you when something is happening to them. Whenever a horse you own takes a hit, you get one line in chat — nobody else does — naming the horse, what hurt it, and what to do about that:

What the line looks like

Ravensong took 1 heart from Zombie. Something is attacking it — light and fence the pen, or move the horse.

Ravensong took half a heart from sunlight. Daylight burns this one — shade, a roof or a stable by day. It runs for cover on its own if there is any.

Ravensong took 3 hearts from a fall. Fence the drops around the pen. It is down to 2 hearts — a horse heals beside water, once it has eaten.

  • It is an alarm, not a log. One line per horse every five seconds at most, so a pen that catches fire is something you can read rather than a wall of text, and it turns red when the horse is down to a quarter of its hearts.
  • How to heal it is only added when it matters — below that same quarter. Above it, the sentence is about keeping it from happening again.
  • You are not told what you did yourself. Hitting your own horse produces nothing; you are looking at it.
  • You have to be in the same world as the horse. A horse hurt in the horse dimension while you are in the overworld says nothing, and neither does one hurt while you are logged out. That is what the browser's planned log tab is for, not this.
  • A lethal genotype is not an injury. A foal dying of one says so, and says the only thing that would have prevented it: not pairing those two.
  • Server owners who would rather have none of it can turn it off — notices.owned_horse_damage in the server config, on by default.

5. When one of them dies

A horse dying is the one thing everybody on the server hears about. One line, once, naming the horse, whose it was and what killed it — grey for everyone, and red in the owner's own chat, so that on a server keeping hundreds of horses you can find your own loss in the scroll:

What the line looks like

Ravensong, Ixora's horse, was killed by Zombie.

Ravensong, Ixora's horse, was killed by lava.

Thornfoal, Ixora's horse, did not survive a genetic defect.

  • Only a horse somebody owns. A wild horse, or one a cowboy is still keeping, dies quietly. So does a horse nobody has tamed yet, however good it was.
  • Whoever killed it is named, player or mob. When nothing dealt the blow — lava, a fall, drowning — the line names that instead.
  • Everybody, wherever they are. Unlike the hurt notice, this one does not care which world you are in or whether you were anywhere near it. It is news, not an alarm you can still do something about.
  • You do not have to be online to be named in it. The owner's name comes from the server's own name cache, so a mare that dies while her owner sleeps still says whose she was.
  • It obeys showDeathMessages. A server that turned vanilla's death messages off gets no horse ones either.
  • Turn it off with notices.owned_horse_death in the server config, on by default.

6. When one of them is about to die

A horse down to a fifth of its hearts stops taking direction and starts saving itself — with you still on it. Your saddle comes off into your pack, it drops any fight it had picked, and it runs: faster and higher than it can while anybody is steering, at a fence rather than along it, carrying you out of whatever is killing it.

What you read when it happens

Ravensong has bolted, and will not be steered until it is calm. Your saddle is in your pack.

  • You stay on. It is not throwing you — it is taking over. You are a passenger until it calms down, and then the horse is yours to saddle and ride again. Getting off mid-flight is up to you.
  • Your saddle is not lost. It goes straight into your inventory; if your inventory is full it lands on the ground beside you, and the line in chat says which. Putting it back on a horse that is still bolting does nothing but return the saddle to your pack again — the saddle is how the horse hands the reins back, so it will not wear one until it has calmed.
  • It is faster, and it jumps higher. About a third more speed and two fifths more jump for as long as it is running. The jump is the part that matters: unboosted, the weakest horses cannot clear a fence at all, and with it almost any horse can leave a fenced pen. Its information screen honestly reads the higher figure while it is bolting.
  • A bareback horse loses the reins too. At the top bond tier a horse steers with no tack, and that stops as well while it is bolting — the same behaviour, reached a different way. You are not handed a saddle you never owned.
  • A fifth of its own hearts. A frail horse and a Percheron bolt at very different numbers and at the same fraction of themselves.
  • It keeps going for about ten seconds after the last blow, and stops only once it has healed clear of the threshold — not the moment it crosses back over it, or one point of healing would leave it standing in front of the thing that was killing it.
  • A fighter runs too. A horse with an aggression gene holds its ground against what it has targeted at full health; at two hearts it lets go and leaves. Nothing a horse can be doing outranks not dying — including being read about, which normally holds it still.
  • A tied horse pulls. It is not exempted, so it strains the lead like any other animal that wants to be somewhere else.
  • An empty-backed horse keeps its tack. A saddled horse nobody is riding bolts with the saddle still on it — there are no reins to take away.
  • The fifth is behaviour.escape_health_fraction in the server config. Set it higher for horses that give up sooner, lower for horses that fight on, and 0 to turn the whole thing off.

Where it lives

  • data/HorseCareAttachment — the per-horse state (bond, herd id, the bondToday / dayStamp daily-cap pair, a bondTicks fractional accumulator, a togetherTicks herd-formation counter). Registered as horsegenetics:horse_care in ModAttachments, copyOnDeath, serialized by its own MAP_CODEC.
  • server/HorseCareHandler — the shared tick (EntityTickEvent.Post, every 30 ticks, phase-staggered by tickCount + entityId), plus the PlayerInteractEvent.EntityInteract hook for feed-bond and the EntityJoinLevelEvent hook that adds the goal.
  • server/BondFollowGoal — the AI goal that reads the bond tier, added at priority 4 to every Horse, with its Watch (tier 1, head-turning, LOOK only) added at priority 8, below all of band life. Its ReachCheck is the shared "can I walk there?" cache — a real createPath every two seconds, not every tick.
  • network/HorseCareSyncPayload → client/ClientHorseCareCache — bond + in-herd flag pushed to trackers on change and on start-tracking, shown on the horse information screen's Overview tab (the i button on the horse inventory).
  • common/care/HurtNotice → server/HorseHurtNoticeHandler — the owner-only hurt notice: the words and the quiet period in the game-free module, the damage hook and the owner lookup in the game one. Switched by notices.owned_horse_damage.
  • Block tags data/horsegenetics/tags/block/horse_water.json and horse_food.json.
Why the food goals cache the genotype

The goal resolves the horse's diet and favourite once and keeps them. Both lookups parse the genetic code out of the record and dietOf parses the epigenome as well, and a goal's canUse runs every tick for every horse - so without the cache a paddock of thirty horses would have been doing sixty full genome parses a tick, doubled for two hands, for as long as anyone stood nearby holding anything. Both facts are fixed at birth, so resolving once is correct as well as cheap. It is lazy rather than done in the constructor because the goal is added on entity join and the record is filled on the founding tick.

What counts as dropped food — and why it is not the hand-feeding test

There are two questions, and they had one answer until 2026-09-24. DietFoods.accepts is what a hand may offer, and for an ordinary horse it answers false and hands the interaction back to vanilla, which means #minecraft:horse_food: wheat, sugar, a hay block, an apple, a carrot and the three golden foods. DietFoods.acceptsFromGround is what a horse picks up off the floor, and for an ordinary or eat-anything horse it is deliberately wider — the item form of every rung HungerFoodGoal.rungOf already sends it walking to:

  • c:crops, c:foods/fruit, c:foods/vegetable, c:foods/berry, c:mushrooms, #minecraft:small_flowers, and cake;
  • plus #minecraft:horse_food itself, and any mod's bale via HayBales.

The common tags are the point of the shape: another mod's grain or fruit is food without this mod naming it, which is the case the owner hit — a heavily modded server drops almost nothing that is in vanilla's eight-item tag. A narrow diet is untouched and still answers accepts, because a wheat-eater walking past the rest is the whole point of it.

The defect this closes was visible without mods: the block rungs let an ordinary horse cross a pen for a planted potato, and the item rung, asking GeneAbilityHandler.eats, made it ignore the same potato lying at its feet. That call also re-parsed the genotype per dropped item examined — see the note above — so the search now asks with the diet it resolved once. And because the ask was "food dropped near them", not "when starving", grazeInPlace takes a dropped mouthful within 2 blocks before it takes the grass, on the same one-in-1,200 roll.

A horse only walks to food it can reach

The owner's pasture backs onto a farm, and on 2026-09-23 every horse in it stood pressed against the dividing fence. The crops were the best rung in sight and well inside the ten-block search; the horse took the nearest one, moveTo handed it the partial path that stops at the rail, and it leaned there for the full pursuit before giving up, ignoring that one block, and starting again on the block beside it. A farm is a hundred such blocks, so the pen never ran out of turns and the shoving never stopped.

HungerFoodGoal.search now asks createPath/canReach before it commits to a candidate — the same question BondFollowGoal.ReachCheck asks of an owner behind a door, at vanilla's own moveTo accuracy of 1. That number is the whole trick: a manhattan block still reaches a hay bale the horse stands beside, or the grass block under its feet, and is two short of a crop on the far side of a fence.

The check alone would have swapped pushing for starving. CROP outranks GRASS, so a horse in a grass pen beside a fenced farm would reject crops every search and never fall through to what it was standing in. The block scan therefore keeps the nearest candidate of every rung rather than one global best, and walks them best-first until one is reachable; an unreachable farm quietly demotes the horse to its own grass. A rejected candidate is ignored for the usual minute, so the next search does not simply pick its neighbour, and a search spends at most three path tests — fewer than the repaths the pursuit it replaces was already spending every twenty ticks. tick drops a pursuit whose path stops short as well, for the gate that shuts behind a horse that set off while it was open.

This is the pathing half of the pasture-edge report only. A horse that is beside the fence for some other reason can still eat a crop through it: the goal's eating radius is 2.5 blocks and does not ask what is in between, which is a separate bullet.

The hurt notice — whose chat, how often, and why sunlight is a special case

common/care/HurtNotice is the words and the rules; it is in the game-free module for repro/ReproText's reason — the wording is the feature, so a test should be able to pin it down (HurtNoticeTest). It groups vanilla's damage types into a Cause by the advice each earns rather than by what they are: a campfire, a hot floor and a burning horse are three damage types and one thing to do about it. Each cause carries one prevention sentence. What to do about the injury itself does not vary by cause — it is water and a fed horse every time — so it is appended once, and only below BADLY_HURT (a quarter of the horse's own maximum). A line on every scratch that ended "it heals beside water" would train the owner out of reading the half that changes.

server/HorseHurtNoticeHandler is the translation, on LivingDamageEvent.Post — the same hook DebugWorldWatch logs damage from, and the one that reports the damage actually taken after armour rather than the amount aimed at the horse. It answers the entity question before the config one, because it fires for every hit anything in the world takes.

  • The owner, via getOwner(), and only if that returns a ServerPlayer. That method answers only for an owner loaded in the same level, which is exactly this message's audience: a line in the chat log of somebody who logged out an hour ago is not an alarm. The offline and cross-dimension cases are the browser's planned log tab, deliberately not this.
  • Never when the damage source's entity is the owner. A player who hits their own horse is looking at it — and in the test yard the line would be constant.
  • One line per horse per QUIET_TICKS (five seconds), keyed on the horse rather than on the cause: a burning horse takes damage every two seconds and a horse among zombies several times a second. Five seconds because the message is an alarm the owner is meant to be able to answer, and no shorter because the second line about the same fire says nothing the first did not. The map is a HashMap cleared on LivingDeathEvent and swept of five-minute-old entries whenever it passes 512 horses.
  • Sunlight is told apart from fire by whether the horse is alight. SunSensitivityHandler burns a sun-sensitive horse with vanilla's onFire damage and never ignites it, so isOnFire() is false exactly in the sun case — and HurtNotice.of can never return SUNLIGHT from a damage type alone, which is what the test asserts. Giving the burn its own damage type would have been cleaner and would also have moved it out of the fire-resistance and fire-immunity tags, so it was not done.
  • AbstractHorse, not Horse — a donkey or a mule is somebody's animal too. The name falls back from the tag, to this mod's record, to whatever vanilla calls the species.
  • Damage type ids are matched, not verified (hard rule 8). They are vanilla's own strings and an unrecognised one is simply "something", so a wrong id costs a sentence of advice and never correctness — which is also what makes another mod's damage safe to be hit by.

Naming the horse and reading the cause are server/HorseNotices, shared with the death notice below so that the line about a burning horse and the line about the horse that burned to death cannot drift apart.

The death notice — why it is not vanilla's sentence

Vanilla says nothing at all when a horse dies, not even to its owner. The block that tells an owner their pet died is in TamableAnimal.die, and AbstractHorse extends Animal and merely implements OwnableEntity — so there is no vanilla line here to duplicate, to suppress, or to be beaten to. That answers the question this bullet was filed with: the dedup problem is not against vanilla, it is against the event firing twice.

CombatTracker.getDeathMessage() was the obvious thing to broadcast and is not used either, because it names the entity. A horse of this mod carries its name in its record rather than on a name tag, so vanilla's sentence would announce "Horse was slain by Zombie" about a named mare with a pedigree and an owner — and the name, the owner and the cause are the entire content of the message. So the sentence is common/care/DeathNotice, beside HurtNotice and for the same reason, with DeathNoticeTest on it.

  • It reuses HurtNotice.Cause and drops the advice. One table of damage ids for both messages; but a dead horse is not the moment to be told to fence the drops, so only phrase() is read. The one cause that changes the verb is GENETIC_DEFECT — "did not survive a genetic defect", matching the mod's own damage type in lang, because a lethal genotype is not something that killed the foal.
  • The owner's name, online or not, through HorseOwnership.ownerName (widened to AbstractHorse for this) and so through the server's name caches. A name neither cache has seen degrades to "somebody's horse" rather than losing the clause.
  • Everyone gets the line; the owner's copy is red. Which means a loop over getPlayerList().getPlayers() comparing UUIDs rather than broadcastSystemMessage, since the two styles differ. The owner is matched by getOwnerReference(), not getOwner(), so an owner in another dimension or offline is still the owner for naming purposes.
  • Once. LivingDeathEvent fires from LivingEntity.die before vanilla's own already-dead guard, so a second die() in the same tick would say it twice. ANNOUNCED is a HashMap of horse to tick with the hurt notice's sweep.
  • The showDeathMessages game rule is checked, in vanilla's own order of business: a server that turned death messages off has said what it wants of its chat, and a horse is not an exception to that.

How free-roaming horses actually live

The herd system models the American mustang on Bureau of Land Management range. It is the best-studied free-roaming horse population, and the one the sources below describe. The headline: a “herd” is not one group with a leader. It is a network of small social bands sharing broad, overlapping ground. Bands gather for a while at water, a salt lick or especially good forage, but they do not defend a fixed territory the way a territorial animal does.

BLM manages horses in 175 Herd Management Areas across ten western states, and they differ enormously in terrain, water, forage, density, sex ratio, management history, predators and how connected they are to neighbouring range. The framework below is consistent across them. The details are local.

Family bands

A breeding band is commonly one adult stallion, several adult mares, this year’s foals, yearlings and some older juveniles, and now and then one or more subordinate adult or near-adult males. Bands run from two to twenty or more horses. One comparison found about eight in open sage-steppe and four or five in wooded or dense-cover country, and multi-stallion bands turned up mostly among the larger bands.

“One stallion, many mares” is fundamentally right, but it oversimplifies:

  • The stallion is the band’s reproductive defender and cohesion-keeper, not necessarily the one who decides where it goes.
  • Mature mares, especially older established ones, often decide movement, resting places and water use. In a USGS behavioural study the most dominant horse in a band was often a mare, and the stallion’s rank relative to his own mares varied from band to band. Mares are together almost all the time and settle a working order among themselves; the stallion spends much of his time on the edge.
  • A subordinate “beta” stallion may be tolerated, may help defend or herd, and may or may not breed.
  • Mares are not possessions. They form lasting friendships, can change bands, can resist or follow different stallions, and often carry a band through a change of stallion.

What the band stallion actually does

His work is mostly female defence, not leading from the front:

  • staying alert to nearby males and bands, and putting himself between a rival and his mares;
  • driving a straying mare back to the band — herding, with a lowered head and neck, pinned ears, nips and short chases, usually from behind or alongside;
  • chasing bachelors and rival band stallions;
  • guarding a mare in heat especially closely;
  • investigating urine and dung with the flehmen lip-curl, and marking with urine or with dung “stud piles”;
  • threatening, displaying and, when he has to, fighting.

Defence is not constant violence. Most conflict is settled by ritualised signals long before a real fight: pinned ears, head and neck posture and the “snake neck”; approaching, displacing, yielding or turning away; tail swishing and threatening faces; chasing or a brief bite; and only in escalated cases rearing, striking and boxing. Serious stallion fights do happen, mostly over a mare or a takeover, but fighting wastes energy and risks injury, and many challenges end when one male simply yields.

Mares are the social core

Stallions are displaced, die, lose mares or take over other bands; mares and their relationships supply the continuity. A mare’s ties include her foal, especially in its first year; preferred grooming partners; familiar adult mares, relatives in some populations; a resident stallion, who may be a lasting partner or may change with band turnover; and the young horses who learn routes, boundaries and feeding and water routines from her.

Mutual grooming of the neck, withers, mane and shoulder is both insect control and bonding. Resting horses stand in close groups, often head to tail, so each tail keeps flies off a neighbour’s face.

Bachelor bands and how a stallion gets mares

Bachelor bands hold colts that have left their natal band, young stallions not yet able to keep mares, stallions that lost their band, older former band stallions, and males between chances. The BLM handbook names breeding bands and bachelor bands as the two main social structures. A bachelor band is not a waiting room. It is where young males learn to negotiate rank and distance, how hard to push before another horse retaliates, play-fighting and combat, herding and courtship, how to approach a band without provoking all-out aggression, and who is dominant, who is an ally and who is too dangerous to challenge.

Bachelors graze, travel, rest and groom together, but each is also a future competitor, so membership and hierarchy are looser than in a family band. They show more open play and rank-testing than settled band stallions: sparring and neck wrestling, chasing games, mounting and sexual play, bites and threats, rearing and mock fights, flehmen around mares’ scent, and shadowing breeding bands at a distance. A bachelor may graze peacefully for hours and then leave to trail a mare, try a sneak mating, claim a dispersing filly or challenge a stallion.

A male rarely wins a whole harem in one battle. The routes to a band are:

  1. recruiting a dispersing young mare, the common route;
  2. taking or attracting an adult mare from another band, often around social disruption, a reproductive change or a shift in the stallion’s quality;
  3. replacing an old, injured, dead or defeated stallion;
  4. building a band gradually, one mare and then more;
  5. succeeding the resident in a multi-male band.

Over a lifetime a stallion may move repeatedly between bachelor, solitary, subordinate and band-stallion roles.

Growing up and leaving

Real horses have no “age-up” moment; development is gradual and socially negotiated. The stages that matter:

  • Foal, birth to weaning. Most wild horses breed and foal in spring. A foal stays close to its dam, nurses often, sleeps a lot, and slowly starts grazing and copying adults. Its dam is its attachment, but it learns from the whole band: which adults are safe, how close it may go to each, when the group moves, rests, drinks or flees, and what yielding, ear position, threats, grooming invitations and play limits mean. Foals play hard with peers: chasing, bucking, mock bites and short play fights.
  • Weanling and yearling. Nutritionally more independent, still socially part of the band, building friendships and a rank among the young. BLM counts a horse as an adult from the January after its birth year, which is an administrative line, not social maturity.
  • Colts leave at about one to two years. BLM says one or two; USGS puts it around two, with some staying to two or three if the stallion does not drive them out. The departure can be forced (the stallion threatens, chases, bites and excludes him), voluntary (he ranges farther, takes up with nearby bachelors and stops coming back), or triggered by peers, his maturity, the stallion’s tolerance, band size or a new foal. A maturing son is a future rival and could breed the band’s mares. Once out he may join bachelors, travel with one or two other males, linger near home or be briefly solitary.
  • Fillies leave later, commonly near sexual maturity. They may join another breeding band, be recruited by a bachelor or outside stallion, move after their natal band is disrupted, or occasionally stay, especially if the resident stallion is not their sire. Female dispersal is what keeps bands from inbreeding and links bands together, and it turns bands over without any fight.
  • Adults. A mare usually settles into one or more breeding bands over her life. A male may stay a bachelor, go solitary, be a subordinate, gain one mare, build a larger band, lose it, and go round again.

Maturity is not one switch. Physical maturity, social confidence, rank, familiarity with local horses and routes, reproductive state, whether the resident stallion tolerates a young male, and whether there are dispersing females or vacant bands all move separately.

Food and water

Mustangs spend about half their daylight feeding, and in arid country they keep moving through grazing areas and often travel between forage and scattered water every twelve to twenty-four hours.

  • Most competition is indirect, or exploitative: one horse eats forage another could have eaten, more horses means less per horse, and repeated grazing slows plant recovery. Drought, winter and poor water make the same number of horses functionally more crowded. The cost shows as poorer body condition, lower conception and foaling success, lower foal survival, more walking, crowding at the remaining water and green forage, and more displacements there.
  • Direct competition happens at clumped resources. Within a band, rank decides first access: a dominant mare displaces a subordinate from a patch, a mineral source, or limited shade or forage with pinned ears, a threat, a snake neck, a bite or a chase. The subordinate usually yields. Foals and young horses get displaced for being low-ranked, though their dams can step in. On broad rangeland horses graze side by side without contesting each bite; hunger, crowding, the patch’s value and room to back away all change how much aggression there is.
  • Water is the sharpest pressure point, being more concentrated than forage. Horses queue, approach cautiously, drink at different times or displace each other at a trough, spring, pond or seep. Different bands share the same water, sometimes peacefully and sometimes with brief threats and chases.

BLM treats forage, water, cover and space as the four essentials. Each area’s Appropriate Management Level is the population range meant to fit long-term rangeland health; the current year’s foals are left out of that headcount.

Home range, not territory

A band uses a familiar area year after year, but it is not exclusive. The BLM handbook says bands are not territorial and do not defend preferred habitat; bands graze and water near one another, and horses move between bands. The one exception is brief: USGS documented stallions defending a mineral source while their band was using it, with more aggression as other bands approached.

A GPS study in Utah’s Conger and Frisco areas found harems and bachelors choosing herbaceous vegetation, avoiding forest relative to shrubland, and shifting movement and water use by season. Harems had distinct winter ranges, and bachelors moved faster than harems in every season. Snow, heat, water, plant growth, fences, drought and terrain keep changing where horses go and where bands meet.

Rank is pairwise, not an alpha

Dominance is real but local. Within a band it decides who displaces whom at a feeding spot, who gets the preferred resting place, who drinks first when access is tight, who can start or refuse grooming, which horse the young yield to, and how close horses can come to one another. Age, confidence, familiarity, reproductive state, size and past encounters all feed it, and a mare can outrank the stallion in day-to-day access. His advantage is reproductive control and his willingness to fight outside males, not command over every mare. The useful model is a set of “A yields to B” relationships, not one ladder.

When bands meet

Two bands may ignore each other while grazing close by, exchange looks and scent, drift apart, let mares and stallions investigate, display, herd or chase if a stallion tries to recruit a mare, or gather for a while at water or forage. Tension between bands is most likely when a mare is in heat, a bachelor presses close, resources are unusually short, or a rival sees a real chance at a mare.

Breeding, foaling and paternity

Mustangs are female-defence polygynists: the stallion defends access to a set of mares, not a map boundary. That does not make him the sire of every foal in his band. A challenger or bachelor can mate successfully, a takeover can be recent, a mare can change bands while pregnant, a band can hold several males, and mate-guarding is never perfect. A resident stallion tends a mare in heat closely: trailing her, blocking other males, herding her away from a rival, investigating her urine or dung and covering it with his own.

Foaling reshapes the band. The mare stays close to and protects the foal, and a newborn’s pace and safety set the band’s movement. Several foals make a peer group and a play network. A new foal crop can coincide with older juveniles drifting to the edge or leaving. In hard conditions, shortage reaches mares and foals first, through body condition and milk.

How BLM management shapes all of it

BLM range is not an untouched ecosystem. The agency’s stated aim is self-sustaining, healthy populations in balance with the habitat and other uses, managed at the minimum feasible level so free-roaming behaviour survives. Management still changes age structure, sex ratio, density, genetic connectivity, and access to water and forage.

  • Density. Unmanaged populations can double in about four to five years. Over capacity, social behaviour carries on under pressure: more overlap at water and forage, more frequent band encounters, fewer chances for subordinates to avoid dominant horses, worse body condition and breeding, poorer survival in drought or hard winters, and fewer places for dispersers to start a band without conflict.
  • Gathers and removals. Familiar bands can be split; a stallion can lose some mares and keep others; mare-heavy or stallion-heavy remnants form bands differently; young males can join bachelors earlier or later than they would have; a returning horse comes back to a changed social landscape; and removing local animals unevenly breaks genetic and social continuity. A realistic post-gather population reforms, splits, absorbs strangers, replaces stallions and goes through a period of instability, rather than snapping back.
  • Fertility control sits beside gathers and sex-ratio adjustment in the handbook. Fewer foals means fewer future dispersers, a population that ages over time, fewer juvenile peer groups, and possibly changed mare behaviour and stallion tending depending on the method and timing.

A day in a band’s life

  1. The band grazes for long stretches, spread out but in sight of each other.
  2. Mares make most of the ordinary moves; the stallion keeps to the rear or the edge.
  3. Foals play and keep returning to their dams.
  4. Horses stop to rest, groom, roll and deal with insects.
  5. The band walks to water, often by habitual routes.
  6. Nearby bands may be in sight or arrive at the same water.
  7. Most encounters stay at watching, positioning, sniffing or a brief displacement.
  8. If a bachelor approaches a mare in heat or tries to peel a mare away, the stallion becomes much more active: herding, guarding, chasing, possibly fighting.
  9. At rest, familiar horses cluster, lower-ranked ones keep a respectful distance, and vigilance passes between adults.
  10. The group moves on through its overlapping seasonal range, never patrolling a territory.

Forage and water set the ecological limits; affiliation, dominance, mare choice, stallion defence, age and dispersal draw the social map.

Sparring is not fighting

Horse sparring is ritualised, usually low-to-moderate social fighting, most common among colts, young males and bachelor stallions. It is partly practice, partly rank negotiation, and sometimes a real test of who should yield, but it is not automatically a serious fight. A study of bachelor males found most of their aggressive interactions were sparring, with serious fighting and clearly playful aggression both less common.

What a bout is made of

A bout builds from posture and testing rather than opening with full-force attacks. The USGS ethogram lists neck wrestling, circling, boxing, parallel prancing, posturing, head-bumping and herding as recognisable parts of male agonistic behaviour.

  • Mutual inspection: sniffing the other horse, urine, dung or a stud pile, often followed by flehmen.
  • High-headed posturing: neck arched, standing tall, muscles stiff, prancing or short deliberate steps.
  • Head bobbing or bowing: two males lowering and raising their heads in rhythm while facing each other.
  • Parallel prancing: shoulder to shoulder in slow, high, elevated strides, necks arched, attention locked.
  • Nipping and muzzle contact at the crest, neck, shoulder, flank, groin, hindquarters or legs.
  • Neck wrestling: each hooking or pressing head and neck against the other’s neck or shoulder, pushing and twisting for leverage.
  • Circling head to tail, nipping at flank, rump, groin and hind legs.
  • Partial mounting, a foreleg or both across the other’s body, which can be sexual, dominance or part of the sequence.
  • Rearing and boxing: both up on their hind legs striking with the forelegs. Dramatic, and far riskier than neck wrestling.
  • Short pursuit: one turns away and the other chases a few strides, which says the first lost that exchange.

Why horses spar

  • Young horses learn safely. Chase games, bucking and mock bites become wrestling as males mature. They learn balance, timing, bite placement, controlled rearing, reading faces, and above all how to stop before friction gets expensive. That is why bachelor groups matter: a dispersed colt practises what he will need to recruit and defend mares.
  • Bachelors build a workable order. Sparring lets future rivals keep assessing each other without a damaging fight at every meeting. The result is a fairly stable sense of who yields at close range, who gets a small resource first, who displaces whom, who to avoid, and who is safe to graze or spar beside. Because horses in a local population know each other, many encounters resolve by posture and ritual.
  • Mature males test and advertise. For an adult stallion sparring can be a low-level challenge, an assessment or a warning near mares, advertising condition, confidence, familiarity and willingness to escalate. The stakes climb when a mare is in heat, a bachelor trails a band closely, a challenger tries to peel off a mare, the resident is old, hurt, tired or socially weak, space is tight at water, minerals, shade or food, or a band is already unsettled by a transfer or turnover. A resident stallion usually tries to head off escalation by herding, positioning, threats and chasing before another male reaches a mare.

How a bout unfolds

  1. Detection and orientation. One horse notices another, stops grazing, raises its head, points its ears and turns toward it.
  2. Assessment. They approach directly or in an arc, sniff dung or urine, show flehmen, prance, and show off neck carriage and size.
  3. Posture exchange. Nose to nose, head bobs, shoulder bumps, arched necks, stamps and small feints.
  4. Contact. Neck wrestling, mutual biting, pushing, circling or partial mounting.
  5. Possible escalation. If neither yields: rearing, foreleg strikes, harder bites, short chases.
  6. Yield and resolution. One turns away, retreats, lowers its intensity, allows itself to be displaced or leaves; the other may follow briefly, then stop.
  7. Back to normal. After low-stakes sparring both may graze on close together. After a serious contest the loser may be kept from a mare, a resource or an area for a while.

A horse does not have to be beaten down for a bout to count. A small step away, a turned shoulder, a lowered neck or a decision not to re-engage can settle rank.

Free-ranging horses avoid all-out fights because injuries are expensive: a lame or badly hurt horse may lose water, fall behind, lose standing, or be unable to defend itself.

Sparring among mustangs

On BLM range sparring is most common in bachelor bands, among recently dispersed colts and young adult males: rehearsal for adult competition, and a way to manage rank among males who are companions and reproductive rivals at once. A band stallion may spar or fight too, but his behaviour is aimed at protecting access to mares: he keeps between the challenger and the band, herds his mares away, chases the challenger before contact, and escalates only if the rival stays, challenges or reaches a mare. A bachelor who seems to be “just hanging around” is, from the resident’s side, a possible thief, challenger, successor or mating competitor, so even a mild exchange carries reproductive meaning.

For example, two two-and-a-half-year-old bachelor colts might start with parallel prancing, take turns nipping each other’s crests, neck-wrestle for twenty seconds, rear once, then break off and graze a few body lengths apart. That is sparring. If an adult bachelor then approaches a mare in a resident stallion’s band and the resident herds her away, charges with pinned ears, bites or strikes, and chases the intruder well past the band, that is a resource-and-reproduction conflict, not practice.

Open, found 2026-09-21 while surveying other mods: nothing in this mod stops an owned horse despawning, and whether vanilla does is unproven. setPersistenceRequired() is called on cowboy stock (CowboyHandler, Cowboy), egg-spawned horses (HorseEggSpawner), stable stock (StablePopulator), lycanthropy victims and the debug pens — and never on taming, on binding, or on ownership. The test is cheap: tame a horse, leave it unleashed and unridden well outside the simulation distance, travel far enough that its chunks unload, and come back — ideally with one leashed control horse beside it.
