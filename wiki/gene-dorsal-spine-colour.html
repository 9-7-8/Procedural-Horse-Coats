Magical gene / codominant / colours the dorsal spines, paints nothing

Dorsal spine colour

What colour a horse’s dorsal spines are. The same colours and rules as horn colour (white, the rainbow, pink, black, grey or chaos, and two different copies make a two-tone part), on a locus of its own. On a horse without spines it does nothing.

Crossing two of them

The colours, and two-tone rows

Exactly the horn colour table. Two copies of one colour give a row of that colour. Two different ones make every spine two-tone: the earlier colour in that list at its root and the other at its point, fading between (owner, 2026-10-05). The row does not fade from withers to croup. Every copy carries a little shade of its own, so no two red rows are quite the same red.

Bare bone

A Bon copy is ivory bone. No wild horse carries it; the skeleton horses do, so a skeleton’s spines are bare bone like its other horn-like parts.

Is it good for the horse?

It does nothing to the horse at all. It is only the colour of the spines.

One short subclass

A key, a name, a priority and the words its outcomes are written in. Everything else is AbstractPartColourGene’s, as for dragon horn colour. A two-tone row costs a submit per box rather than one for the whole row, because the layer draws two-tone parts a segment at a time (AttachedPartLayer). That is the existing horn path; a row is many short spines, so it pays more boxes for it. Two different colour alleles on an already rare horse is the uncommon case.

Invented

Dorsal spines are not real on a horse, so neither is their colour. It is a separate locus from every other part’s because the owner chose one colour gene per part. A shared one would force every part on a horse to match.

What needs verifying here

No coloured spine row has been seen in a running game: the colours and the per-spine two-tone fade are unit-tested, not watched.

The two-tone fade on every spine

Check a Wht/Red and a Red/Blu row at low and at towering height. Each spine should be the root colour at its base and the second colour at its point. A low row has two boxes per spine, so its fade is a hard step; a towering one has three.
