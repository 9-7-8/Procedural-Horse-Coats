The coat engine / one channel, five claimants

Eye colour & heterochromia

A horse has two eyes, and this is the single source of truth for what colour they come out. Three genetic routes change an iris, they are ranked in that order, and two more layers on top produce the eyes that are not one flat colour: one blue eye, a wedge of blue in a brown one, and the magical locus that splits an iris between two colours outright.

Built 2026-09-06, not yet play-tested

Every colour and every split below is asserted through the real pipeline in EyeColorTest. What is unknown is whether a four-texel iris split in two reads as heterochromia on a horse at riding distance, or whether the wedge shapes need to be restricted to the halves. Checklist on To be verified.

The iris is the dark texels, not the sclera

On the coat sheet an adult eye is a 2×2 block of pure black — the iris and pupil together, there being no room for both at 128px — beside a 2×2 block of near-white sclera. A foal's is the black block alone. So eye colour means colouring the black texels and leaving the white ones white: CoatOverlay.tintIris weights each texel by how dark it already is (1 - luma), so black takes the colour outright, white is untouched, and the antialiased grey between them takes a proportional share.

Worth stating because the obvious tool is the wrong one. shadeToward scales its target by the texel's own brightness so that a gold hoof keeps the template's shading — which on an eye means the sclera takes the colour and the iris, being black, comes out black. It is the right blend for light's glowing gold eye and the leopard complex's white sclera rim, and exactly backwards for an iris.

It all happens in the overlay phase, after CoatRegions.redrawEyes has copied the template's eyes back over the finished coat — which it does precisely so a gene painting a wide white pattern can never blind a horse. That copy is why anything wanting to colour an eye on purpose has to run after it. See the pipeline.

Three ranks, because there are three routes

A horse has one iris colour, so "what colour are its eyes" has exactly one answer and several genes have a claim on it. That makes it a channel with a single owner and a contribution hook, like the LUT and the cutie mark — not a gene that other genes fight with. A gene implements EyeColorContribution and returns an EyeColor claim; the composer collects them in code order and applies the highest-ranked one.

The ranks are not priority numbers pulled out of the air. They are the three ways a real horse's iris changes colour, and they nest for a reason.

RankRouteClaimantsWhy it sits there
30 — depigmented The iris never got enough melanocytes at all MITF, PAX3, EDNRB, KIT (broad white and up) An absence cannot be overpainted. There is nothing left in a depigmented iris for a pigment gene to recolour, so blue beats everything.
20 — iris-specific pigment A gene that changes what colour the iris is, and nothing else Tiger eye A gene aimed at the eye beats one that reached it on the way past.
10 — whole-body dilution Every pigment the horse has is diluted, iris included MATP (cream / pearl), champagne The least specific claim anything can make on an eye.

Ties — two dilutions, say — go to the earlier gene in Genes.codeOrder(), which puts MATP (priority 40) ahead of champagne (50). A cremello that is also champagne has a cremello's eye.

Note the blue eye is not a new gene and never should be. It is a property the white loci already have — the same failure of pigment cells to reach the skin leaves them out of the iris — and a "blue eyes" locus that mysteriously only ever appeared on white horses would be a worse model of the same fact.

Complete and sectoral heterochromia, from the white loci

One MITF copy produces "anything from subtle facial white or one blue eye to extensive splash". One blue eye is not a separate trait: it is the same failure of melanocyte colonisation as two blue eyes, stopped one iris earlier. A blue wedge in an otherwise coloured iris is the same thing again, stopped part-way across one eye.

So the white loci answer two separate questions. Is this horse blue-eyed is an allele question, answered by EyeColorContribution on each locus and documented on its page. How far did the blue get is not an allele question at all, and it is rolled once as an EyeSpread off the epigenetic seed of the locus that won the claim — inherited with that allele copy, stable for the life of the horse.

OutcomeShare of blue-eyed horsesWhat you see
Both whole62%The two blue eyes splash is known for
One whole22%Complete heterochromia — one blue eye, the other whatever the horse's pigment genes gave it
A wedge somewhere16%Sectoral heterochromia — part of an iris blue and the rest its own colour; sometimes a wedge in each eye, and then never the same shape twice

This is why the pigment layer has to be painted first and in full, with the blue laid over as much of it as the spread says. A depigmented iris is not a colour painted over the pigment, it is pigment that never arrived — so what shows where the blue stops is the horse's own eye, and it has to still be there underneath. A splashed white tiger-eye horse with one blue eye has an amber other one.

It is deliberately one roll rather than one per white locus. A horse carrying splash and frame at once would otherwise stack two independent masks and end up unable to have a single blue eye at all, which is backwards.

Four quadrants, twelve shapes

An iris is four texels, so a sector is a four-bit mask over them (EyePatch): 

bit 0   bit 1        0b0011  the top half
bit 2   bit 3        0b0101  the left half
                     0b1000  one corner
                     0b0111  three corners

EyePatch.WEDGES lists every mask that is neither empty nor the whole iris except the two diagonals. At four texels a diagonal does not read as a wedge of colour in an eye, it reads as two stray pixels — which is a rendering fact about a 2×2 block, not a claim about horses. That leaves twelve: four halves, four single corners, four three-corner shapes.

The quadrants are in texture space, and the two eyes' faces are mirrored on the sheet (see body space), so "left half" is not the same side of the horse on both eyes. That does not matter for a randomly placed wedge and it is not worth un-mirroring for. Which texels are iris at all is worked out from the coat itself — the dark texels in the eye rect, bounding-boxed and halved both ways — rather than hard-coded, because the iris sits at a different offset in each of the two mirrored eyes.

The second layer: genes that paint an iris

EyePatchContribution is the companion hook, and the difference between the two is the difference between the two things an eye can be. The colour channel is ranked, because a horse has one eye colour and several genes claim it. Patches compose: they are absolute writes over the finished iris, applied in code order, last writer to a quadrant wins — the same rule the rest of the overlay phase runs on.

One gene implements it: magic sectoral heterochromia, whose whole point is two colours in one iris. Because patches paint over the channel rather than competing with it, a splashed white horse carrying two colour alleles shows the two colours.

Patches do not stack

Every blend in the overlay phase walks from the unmodified coat, not from the patch beneath it, so two overlapping patches do not accumulate. Use strength 1 unless blending with the template's own iris is what you meant.

What it costs the coat cache

The eyes are drawn into the baked coat texture, so a locus that changes them is in the horse's texture key — its expressing outcomes are real marker()s rather than wild types, and two horses that differ only there do not share a cached texture. Whether it costs one entry per outcome or one per horse then depends on whether the locus varies:

LocusVaries?Cache cost
Tiger eye, MATPNo — every amber eye is the same amberOne entry per outcome
ChampagneYes — the iris shade is rolledOne per horse
The white lociAlready did — their patterns varyUnchanged
Magic sectoral heterochromiaYes — shapes and chaos colourOne per horse

Champagne going non-deterministic is the one that looks like a real cost and is not: almost every horse already carries something that varies (bay's black climb, grey's dapples), so the marginal number of forked textures is small.

Writing a gene that changes eyes

Implement one of the two hooks on your Gene:

HookReturnUse it when
EyeColorContribution Optional<EyeColor> Your gene says what colour the whole iris is. Pick your rank from the three routes above; EyeColor.dilution and EyeColor.pigment are the two factories.
EyePatchContribution Optional<EyePatches> Your gene paints part of an iris, or wants the two eyes to differ from each other in a way the spread does not describe.

Both are handed the pair, the whole genotype, the horse's Epigenome (which may be null — a question asked about a genotype rather than about a horse) and the horse's resolved white coverage. Both may vary per horse, and if yours does, take every number from AlleleRandomness so it is inherited with the allele copy, and declare the outcome varies() or two visibly different horses will share one cached texture.

whiteCoverage is the fraction of the horse's mapped texels the white loci left with no pigment at all, measured on the finished coat. It is passed rather than recomputed because it is the honest signal for "is this horse white enough to have blue eyes": a horse broadly white from two mild loci stacking has the same claim as one white from a single bold allele, and no per-locus test can see that. The threshold is WhitePatternEyes.COVERAGE_THRESHOLD.

What is deliberately not modelled

  • Champagne foals' blue-green eyes. A real champagne foal is born blue-green and matures to amber or hazel; here it is the adult colour from birth. The pipeline knows whether it is baking a foal, but the eye-colour hook is not told, and there is no aging in this mod for it to mature through. On the roadmap.
  • Filmy and blind eyes. Cloudiness is cornea, lens or inflammation rather than iris pigment, and is not an inherited eye-colour category at all.
  • The leopard complex's white sclera. It is drawn, but as a sclera rim in the overlay phase, not through this channel — LP changes what is around the iris, not the iris. See the leopard complex.
