Magical gene / dominant / grows geometry, paints nothing

Cheek spikes

One copy and the horse grows a short row of bone spikes along each cheek, pointing out from the head and a little back, the longest at the rear. How long and thick they are and how many each cheek has is written on the allele. Their colour is a gene of its own, cheek spike colour.

Crossing two of them

One copy, either sex

Dominant: Chk/n and Chk/Chk both grow spikes, so a spiked horse bred to a plain one gives a spiked foal at least half the time. It is not sex-limited. Foals grow none; the spikes come in when the horse grows up, and stay.

Where they sit

In a row along the side of the head, below the eye and above the mouth, the same on both sides. The spike nearest the neck is the longest and each one toward the muzzle is shorter.

Finding one

Rare in the wild: about three horses in a thousand have cheek spikes, and because one copy is enough, every horse that carries the gene shows it. The skeleton horses can grow them in bare bone, as one of their horn-like parts.

What varies

  • Length: three classes, short, medium and long. Short to medium in the wild; a line bred for length reaches long and stops.
  • Girth: how thick each spike is for its length.
  • Count: how many each cheek has, from the back forward, two to four in the wild. A line can be bred down to a single spike per cheek, and the next one grows in gradually.

There is one shape. Everything stacks: a horse can wear cheek spikes with every other part.

Is it good for the horse?

It does nothing at all, for good or ill: no stat, no damage to anything, no hitbox. It is a look.

The mesh: a row that scales per spike

A spike is two boxes, a base and a narrower point, swept back about x and then laid over a quarter turn about z so it points out from the head. Four are baked, 1.3 units apart along the cheek and centred on the anchor. Spike 0 is the rearmost and the longest, so the count shows the big end of the row and grows the next one in toward the mouth.

The spikes point across the anchor’s up, so the layer’s whole-part stretch (which is along that up) would move them apart and not lengthen them. The kind therefore scales per element (PartKind.scalesPerElement), the spine row’s rule and its first use on the head: each spike is one tree rooted on the anchor, and PartModel puts the length and girth on each root in its own frame. There is one style. Length, girth and count are all applied at draw time, so the whole part is three meshes a side. CheekSpikesGeneTest holds the numbering and the mirroring.

The bone version

Bon/Bon on the colour locus. A spike is all bone, so nothing is left off. Both skeleton breeds carry cheek_spikes in their horn count group as a slot of its own, with the colour pinned to bone (owner, 2026-10-09).

Invented

No horse has cheek spikes. The look borrows from the spiny jaw fringe of a bearded dragon and the cheek horns of horned lizards, and from the jugal bosses on some dinosaur skulls. This is fantasy and makes no claim about any real gene. It is dominant (the treatment’s call), so it shows on every carrier.

What needs verifying here

The cheek spikes have been photographed standing still, in white, two-tone and bone; whether the row sits midway between the eye and the mouth as wanted, how it looks from the side and in motion, and long spikes against a bridle are still open.

What a photo shoot showed, and the placement

2026-10-09, -PphotoShoot, four still frames, re-rolled for a count near four. A row of four spikes sits along the side of the head just below the eye, largest at the back, pointing out. A Red/Yel horse has red bases and yellow points. A skeleton’s are bone.

For the owner to judge. The row sits close under the eye; the treatment asked for midway between the eye and the mouth, and the anchor was placed from the head box without knowing where the coat paints the eye. Moving it is one number (AttachedPartLayer.ADULT_CHEEK). At wild sizes the spikes are stubby studs, and seen from the front they point at the camera and read as cubes.

In motion, long, and from the side

Short and long spikes from the side and from the front while the horse walks, grazes and turns its head. They should ride the head with no gap at their roots. A long row should not reach the neck when the head turns.

Against a bridle and horse armour

Put a bridle, then horse armour, on a horse with long spikes. Nothing hides them today and armour is drawn over whatever stands proud. Say whether that looks right.

Two counts in one frame

Two spiked horses side by side with different counts (one and four per cheek). Each should show its own; a horse wearing the other’s is the deferred-submit trap.
