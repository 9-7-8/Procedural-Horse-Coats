Magical gene / recessive / grows geometry, paints nothing

Back sail

Two copies and the horse grows a sail along its back: a row of spines from the withers to the croup, with a see-through membrane stretched between them. Whether it peaks over the middle of the back, runs low the whole length or sags in scallops, and how tall, arched, long and see-through it is, is written on the allele. Its colour is a gene of its own, back sail colour. The one big, rare body part, and the first grown part you can see through.

Crossing two of them

Two copies, either sex

Recessive: only Sail/Sail grows a sail. Sail/n is a carrier and looks like any other horse. Two carriers bred together give a sailed foal about one time in four, and two sailed horses always do. It is not sex-limited. Foals grow none; the sail comes in when the horse grows up, and stays.

Under a saddle

The sail runs through where a saddle and a rider sit. A bare horse nobody is riding shows all of it. Put a saddle on, or get on, and the spines under the saddle are hidden, each taking the membrane in front of it, so what stays is a sail over the withers and another over the croup. A horse steered bare at the top bond has no saddle to draw, but its rider still hides the middle. A short sail that covers only the front of the back can vanish under a saddle almost entirely.

Finding one

Rare in the wild: about one horse in four hundred has a sail, and about one in ten carries a hidden copy. The skeleton horses can grow one as bare bone rays with no skin, as one of their horn-like parts.

What varies

  • Form: tall (highest over the middle of the back, like a Spinosaurus), low (a ridge fin the full length, under half as tall) or scalloped (the tall outline, with the membrane sagging in an arch between each pair of spines).
  • Height: four classes, low to towering, the tallest near a block high. Low to tall in the wild, and a line bred for height reaches towering and stops.
  • Arch: how far the ends fall below the peak, from a gentle dome to a sharp crest.
  • Reach: how far back along the back it runs, from two-thirds of the way to the croup to all of it.
  • Count: how many spines there are, from the withers back. Over half a row to a full one in the wild.
  • Membrane: how see-through the skin is, from faint to nearly solid. It never goes fully solid or fully clear.

Everything stacks: a horse can wear a sail, a row of dorsal spines and every head part at once.

Is it good for the horse?

It does nothing at all, for good or ill: no stat, no attack, no hitbox. It is a look.

Slice B of the body parts

The second body part (model parts), and the one that made translucency data on the kind instead of a constant in the renderer (translucent regions). Like the spines, it adds no layer, render-state field or config key. It is one kind (SAIL, centred), SailGenerator and SailSize, and one line in GrownParts.of dressed by its colour locus. The granting locus is in GrownParts.shapes’s every-part baseline.

The mesh: spines with panels between

SailGenerator stands its spines in the dorsal row’s places (DorsalSpineGenerator.rootZ), so a sail and a spine row number their elements the same way. Each spine is its own root chain in its own group, upright. Unlike a dorsal spine, a fin ray does not rake, because a panel is built in the frame of one spine and has to meet the next. The membrane between spine i - 1 and spine i is COLUMNS flat boxes (PartNode.width, thin across the horse) hung off spine i’s root, in group i. Spine 0 has none. That one choice is why the count and the saddle zone need nothing new: hiding a spine hides the panel in front of it, so no panel is ever left with nothing at its back edge, and the spine’s height scale reaches its panel because the panel is its child. A column stands as tall as a straight edge from tip to tip would be over it, less a sine sag on the scalloped form.

The epigenome, by cost

  • length: the class is the mesh’s size bucket (four; a taller sail is more boxes per spine), the remainder a per-element height stretch.
  • form and curve: baked, SailGenerator.style(form, curve), three forms by three curve buckets. The curve is the arch of the outline.
  • count: shown out of the most baked, front to back, the antler’s tine rule.
  • coverage: AttachedPart.span, a pose-stack scale along the row (row span). The saddle zone is recomputed for it.
  • opacity: AttachedPart.opacity, the membrane pass’s alpha, times the horse’s own fade.

There is no girth. The per-element scale across a spine would scale its panel’s width too, and open or close the gap to the next spine. So backSail passes girth 1, and the spines are as thick as their class bakes them.

How its colour lands

The colour locus’s base colour is the membrane and its tip colour the spines: the see-through region takes the base, as a crystal antler’s shafts do, and the solid regions the tip. The bone version is Bon/Bon on that locus. GrownParts.of then sets the opacity to 0, and the layer skips the membrane pass, which leaves bare bone rays. One bone copy is only a colour and keeps its membrane (AbstractPartColourGene.boneOnly).

Invented

No horse has a sail. The look borrows from the animals that do: the skin-covered neural spines of Dimetrodon, Edaphosaurus and Spinosaurus, whose purpose is still argued over (heat exchange, display, or a hump), and the webbed dorsal fin of a sailfish. This is fantasy and makes no claim about any real gene. It is recessive and rare on purpose (owner, 2026-10-02), so that the sail is the body part you have to breed for. The dorsal spines beside it are dominant and show on every carrier.

What needs verifying here

The sail has only been photographed standing still: whether its membrane really reads as see-through and sorts right against the coat, armour and other horses, how it sits in motion, the saddle zone at a short reach, and whether its comb-of-rods look is the one wanted are all still open.

The membrane against everything else that blends

The sail is the first large flat translucent part. It is drawn through entityTranslucent at submit order 1, like a crystal antler’s shafts. No-one has seen how that sorts against a fading horse’s coat passes, horse armour, a second sailed horse behind the first, water and particles. Look from the side and from behind (through both faces of the membrane) and from above a ridden horse while the rider looks down. Each panel should show the horse and the world through it, with no flicker, no panel vanishing behind another, and no black or white fringe. If a large membrane sorts wrongly, the treatment’s fallback is an opaque “solid fin” style. Report it to the owner rather than ship it.

What a photo shoot showed (2026-10-05, -PphotoShoot, still frames only). In 20 shots of 9 sails, from all four sides, close up and from above: no flicker, no missing panel and no fringe; the panels meet their spines; a two-tone Red/Blu sail is blue spines with red between them; the bone sail is bare rays. Two things it did not settle. First, against the dark horizon and the white stage a membrane at its wild opacity was hard to tell from a solid one, so “see-through” is still unobserved: look at one in front of something with detail (trees, another horse). Second, at a horse’s size the sail reads as a comb of rods rather than a smooth sheet, because the tapered spines and the stepped membrane columns both show. Whether that is the look wanted, or the spines should be thinner and the membrane reach their tips, is the owner’s call once she has seen it.

Where the sail sits

Each form at low and towering height, from the side, while the horse walks, grazes and rears. The first spine should stand just behind the mane, the membrane should meet both spines of every panel with no gap at the back, and the bottom edge should sit on the back without sinking in. A scalloped panel should sag visibly between its spines.

The saddle zone and a short reach

The four states the dorsal spines need (bare, saddled, ridden, phantom), plus a sail at low coverage. Its spines root nearer the withers and more of it should go under the saddle, recomputed for the drawn positions. No membrane should show through or above the saddle, and none should be left hanging free at the edge of the zone.

The bone sail

A Sail/Sail horse with back_sail_colour Bon/Bon, and a skeleton-breed founder that rolled a sail. Each should be bare ivory rays with no membrane at all, not even a faint one.
