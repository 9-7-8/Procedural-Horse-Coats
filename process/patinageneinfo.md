Patina - Patina is the dominant mutation responsible for the gradual and progressive oxidation of pigment within the carrying horse. On black-based horses, Patina may still be present, but the effect is often concealed by the depth of the black pigment, leading to the misconception that the mutation does not exist on black horses. In these cases, Patina may only cause subtle green undertones within the mane, tail, muzzle, flanks, or sun-bleached areas of the coat.


The oxidation process itself may last for years, with the first signs appearing in areas where the mutation is most visible before gradually spreading across the body. Since Patina is a dominant mutation, where it is present it is expressed, though the final phenotype of the carrier will vary greatly from horse to horse. Some Patina horses develop only faint traces of oxidation, while others become heavily marbled with verdigris coloration. The mutation may also affect the skin, resulting in mottling around the eyes, muzzle, and other exposed areas.


Dilution genes may influence how strongly Patina is displayed. Patina is not visible on Double Cream, or Pearl horses, though these horses may still carry and pass on the mutation. However, Cream and Champagne do not conceal Patina and may enhance the coloration by reducing the intensity of the base pigment, allowing the oxidized tones to become more apparent. 


The final appearance of a Patina horse is highly variable, with some individuals retaining large areas of their original color while others appear almost entirely transformed by oxidation. Patina typically reaches full expression between 8-10 years of age, though minor changes in coloration may continue throughout the horse's lifetime.

Patina is meant to imitate how copper oxidizes, causing green markings along where you'd see light patches in a seal bay.
